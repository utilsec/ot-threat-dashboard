High Incident — Supply-chain compromise of a managed-service cloud app; China-state aligned actor per Microsoft; energy and chemicals among targeted sectors; no OT impact reported

Microsoft ties the ConnectWise cloud compromise to China-aligned Storm-0940, which used the vendor’s own app identities to reach Microsoft 365 tenants — energy and chemicals are on its target list

Microsoft Threat Intelligence wrote on 21 October that it assesses Storm-0940, an actor it calls China-state aligned, was responsible for the recently disclosed compromise of ConnectWise’s cloud services. The attacker gained control of the identities of several ConnectWise cloud applications, ConnectWise Cloud Backup among them, and used their stolen credentials to sign in to customers’ Microsoft 365 tenants as the trusted app, so the users’ multi-factor authentication never came into play. Microsoft says Storm-0940 drove the campaign with ARTEX, an open-source penetration-testing framework that can use any AI model, and that more than 2,250 Microsoft 365 tenants received sign-in attempts — attempts, not compromises. Huntress says it saw unauthorised sign-ins at 162 tenants it monitors. A separate password spray from 10 to 14 October produced no successful sign-in. Once inside, it ran directory reconnaissance, added credentials to applications and changed roles and groups. ConnectWise, which says it first detected the activity on 14 October, suspended Cloud Backup (formerly SkyKick), took its SaaS Security and Cloud Management services offline and disabled the affected app identities, and says it has seen no indication so far that partner backup data was compromised; its chief executive said on 22 October that services will be restored in controlled phases. Microsoft lists aerospace and defence, chemicals, energy, manufacturing and government among the targeted sectors; it names no victims, and no OT impact has been reported. The sector read is ours: a Microsoft 365 tenant holds the email, SharePoint libraries and Teams channels where plant drawings, network diagrams, vendor remote-access details and incident plans tend to live, and many operators and their contractors buy backup through managed service providers.

Over the nights of 11–13 October, the second wave hitting stores with Dixell equipment, attackers remotely changed settings on web-managed refrigeration controllers in French supermarkets, forcing extended defrost cycles that pushed cabinets toward about 50°C and spoiled stock; the Paris prosecutor reports fires at some sites. Finance Minister Roland Lescure told Parliament on 13 October that roughly 700 of France’s 10,000 supermarkets were hit, across Carrefour, Coopérative U, E.Leclerc, Intermarché and Netto, and Monoprix. CERT-FR’s alert CERTFR-2026-ALE-012 named Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC controllers on 12 October, added Copeland and Dixell xweb on 13 October, and on 16 October said the campaigns are probably still under way; as of 28 October CERT-FR had issued no later version, and no new attack nights had been reported since 13 October. No actor has claimed it, no ransom has been demanded and the entry route has not been established; Danfoss and Copeland say most affected units are older, end-of-life models. Two follow-ups reach beyond retail. Nozomi Networks’ 13 October analysis links the same precondition — controllers left reachable from the internet for vendor management — to fuel automatic tank gauges, citing Shadowserver’s count of 909 exposed gauges in June. And Claroty Team82 reported on 13 October that an AI coding agent, redirected by a human, reproduced a previously disclosed Danfoss AK-SM 800A authentication-bypass and command-injection chain to root on a lab device in about 28 minutes for roughly $6; Team82 does not say those flaws were used in France. On 22 October Forescout counted 12,976 internet-exposed refrigeration management service observations on 6,017 IP addresses in 99 countries — services, not devices. This is not an oil and gas incident and it does not count toward this page’s score. The lesson is ours: tank gauges, forecourt and convenience-store refrigeration, building management and vendor-monitored packages at terminals and retail fuel sites belong to the same exposure class.

Low Research — Commodity malware in OT networks and how to clean it up; vendor guidance, no actor

Dragos: old worms such as Conficker, Sality and WannaCry keep turning up in OT incident calls — and the wrong cleanup can do more harm than the infection

Dragos’s Lesley Carhart and Jan Hoff wrote on 15 October that requests for help with legacy commodity infections — Sality, Gammima, WannaCry and Conficker among them — have risen sharply in recent years. Many OT networks Dragos is called into carry between 20 and 70 distinct commodity-malware variants spread across several subnets, often on systems older than Windows XP SP2. They put the rise down to more networking and remote access, better visibility, and regulation that expects critical-infrastructure networks to be clean. Their answer is a triage framework they call Now, Next and Never, published with a white paper, and a warning that a cleanup done the wrong way can be riskier than the infection it is meant to remove. There is no CVE and no actor here. The fit for oil and gas is our assessment and an easy one: refineries, gas plants and pipeline control centres keep operator stations, historians and vendor PCs on old Windows builds for years, and an aggressive cleanup — mass reboots, forced updates or an endpoint agent pushed onto running units — can stop a process the worm never touched.

No single patch — the work is triage, containment and cleanup planned around maintenance windows Action: Find out what is already there before deciding what to do about it. Use passive monitoring, or offline scans of disk images, to look for the legacy families Dragos names on operator stations, historians, engineering laptops and vendor PCs, and record where each sits and what it talks to. Then close the doors the infections came through — removable media, shared engineering laptops and flat paths from the business network.

Symantec’s Threat Hunter Team reported on 1 October that a group it calls Longlegs, which it describes as a China-nexus threat actor also tracked as Storm-2603, has attacked at least four organisations in the past two months: two critical infrastructure operators — a water utility and a telecommunications provider — plus a regional government body and a university, in Portuguese- and Spanish-speaking countries in Europe, Africa and Latin America. No victim or country is named. Symantec says the group typically gets in by exploiting several vulnerabilities in on-premises Microsoft SharePoint Server; the CVEs it names are the 2025 ToolShell flaws (CVE-2025-49704, CVE-2025-49706, CVE-2025-53770 and CVE-2025-53771), alongside unspecified related SharePoint flaws. It is on this page as cross-sector context, not an oil and gas incident: the targets are critical infrastructure operators, the entry point is the kind of on-premises collaboration server that engineering and document-control teams often run, and machine-key theft means a server patched after compromise can still be reopened.

Medium Threat Intelligence & Research — Ransomware statistics

NCC Group counts 1,073 ransomware attacks in August, a 2026 high, with the industrial sector bearing 31% — and energy incidents are still landing on IT, which is where the outage comes from

NCC Group’s review of August 2026, reported by Industrial Cyber on 28 September, counts 1,073 ransomware attacks, up 12 percent from 960 in July. The industrial sector accounted for 329 of them, about 31 percent, and North America for 473, about 44 percent. Qilin led with 164 claimed attacks, overtaking The Gentlemen. NCC also describes Aurora, a ransomware-as-a-service group active since late April that goes after manufacturing, legal, research and transportation organisations through weak or unauthenticated VPN access before encrypting hypervisors. A different tracker, Comparitech, reported on 14 September a record 997 attacks for the same month; the counts differ because each tracker has its own method, and both rest largely on leak-site claims, so a claimed victim is not a confirmed one, and neither breaks out oil and gas. The pattern they fit is the one the sector already knows from Colonial Pipeline onward — encryption lands on business systems, and operations stop because scheduling, billing, custody-transfer accounting or terminal ticketing depends on them, not because anything touched a controller.
