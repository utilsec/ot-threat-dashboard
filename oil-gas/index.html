High Incident — Supply-chain compromise of a managed-service cloud app; China-state aligned actor per Microsoft; energy and chemicals among targeted sectors; no OT impact reported

Microsoft ties the ConnectWise cloud compromise to China-aligned Storm-0940, which used the vendor’s own app identities to reach Microsoft 365 tenants — energy and chemicals are on its target list

Microsoft Threat Intelligence wrote on 21 October that it assesses Storm-0940, an actor it calls China-state aligned, was responsible for the recently disclosed compromise of ConnectWise’s cloud services. The attacker gained control of the identities of several ConnectWise cloud applications, ConnectWise Cloud Backup among them, and used their stolen credentials to sign in to customers’ Microsoft 365 tenants as the trusted app, so the users’ multi-factor authentication never came into play. Microsoft says Storm-0940 drove the campaign with ARTEX, an open-source penetration-testing framework that can use any AI model, and attempted sign-ins to more than 2,250 tenants; most of the targeting failed, but it gained privileged cloud persistence at many organisations within a short time. A separate password spray from 10 to 14 October produced no successful sign-in. Once inside, it ran directory reconnaissance, added credentials to applications and changed roles and groups. ConnectWise shut down Cloud Backup, took its SaaS Security and Cloud Management services offline and disabled the affected app identities, and says it has seen no indication so far that partner backup data was compromised. Microsoft lists aerospace and defence, chemicals, energy, manufacturing and government among the targeted sectors; it names no victims, and no OT impact has been reported. The sector read is ours: a Microsoft 365 tenant holds the email, SharePoint libraries and Teams channels where plant drawings, network diagrams, vendor remote-access details and incident plans tend to live, and many operators and their contractors buy backup through managed service providers.

FBI and Secret Service: the FortiBleed credential campaign against FortiGate firewalls is still running — and the energy sector is among its targets

In a joint advisory dated Tuesday 6 October (JCSA-20261006-01), the FBI and US Secret Service warned that the FortiBleed campaign against internet-facing Fortinet FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways remains an active threat, with attackers still scanning for devices they can log into using credentials obtained earlier. There is no CVE: the agencies describe abuse of reused or leaked credentials and of legacy SHA-256 password storage. Researcher reporting adds credential stuffing and spraying from leak dumps, offline cracking of extracted hashes and a tool, FortigateSniffer, that passively captures authentication traffic across 24 protocols. Intruders have created administrator accounts with names such as fortiAdmin, forticloud-sync and fgtsecure, and some owners were locked out after attackers reset or deleted the legitimate accounts. The agencies say targets span all 16 US critical infrastructure sectors, energy and food and agriculture among them, and the FBI links the access to Payload ransomware; SOCRadar says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments that began this way. SOCRadar’s figure of 86,644 working device credentials in 194 countries dates from 19 June and is not a new count. Researchers describe the operator as a Russian-speaking initial access broker whose buyers overlap with INC and Lynx affiliates; that is their assessment, not the agencies’. No OT system compromise has been described and no oil and gas victim named. The sector read is ours: FortiGate is one of the most common firewalls at the IT/OT boundary and at remote sites, so a working login on one is a step toward operations.

Confirmed active campaign — the FBI and Secret Service say it continues and that targets span all 16 US critical infrastructure sectors, including energy; ransomware counts and the operator profile are researcher assessments; no OT compromise or oil and gas victim reported No CVE — the advisory describes credential abuse and legacy password storage, not a product vulnerability

Then walk the path the agencies describe, from a FortiGate login to Active Directory enumeration to ransomware: confirm that a valid VPN session lands in a zone that cannot reach OT, and that access into the OT DMZ needs a second, separate authentication.

Cyber insurer Resilience has published an analysis of the energy and utilities claims it received between January 2023 and early September 2026, reported by Industrial Cyber on 21 October. Ransomware made up about 9% of the sector’s claims, against 6% elsewhere in Resilience’s book, but drove about 70% of incurred losses. The read for oil and gas is ours: the losses that land are business-side ransomware and the shutdowns it forces, so recovering the systems that schedule, measure and bill product matters as much as protecting the control network.

Low Research — Commodity malware in OT networks and how to clean it up; vendor guidance, no actor

Dragos: old worms such as Conficker, Sality and WannaCry keep turning up in OT incident calls — and the wrong cleanup can do more harm than the infection

Dragos’s Lesley Carhart and Jan Hoff wrote on 15 October that requests for help with legacy commodity infections — Sality, Gammima, WannaCry and Conficker among them — have risen sharply in recent years. Many OT networks Dragos is called into carry between 20 and 70 distinct commodity-malware variants spread across several subnets, often on systems older than Windows XP SP2. They put the rise down to more networking and remote access, better visibility, and regulation that expects critical-infrastructure networks to be clean. Their answer is a triage framework they call Now, Next and Never, published with a white paper, and a warning that a cleanup done the wrong way can be riskier than the infection it is meant to remove. There is no CVE and no actor here. The fit for oil and gas is our assessment and an easy one: refineries, gas plants and pipeline control centres keep operator stations, historians and vendor PCs on old Windows builds for years, and an aggressive cleanup — mass reboots, forced updates or an endpoint agent pushed onto running units — can stop a process the worm never touched.

Not an exploitation report — a vendor observation from incident-response work; no actor, campaign or victim named No CVE — legacy commodity malware, much of it spreading through long-patched flaws
No single patch — the work is triage, containment and cleanup planned around maintenance windows Action: Find out what is already there before deciding what to do about it. Use passive monitoring, or offline scans of disk images, to look for the legacy families Dragos names on operator stations, historians, engineering laptops and vendor PCs, and record where each sits and what it talks to.
