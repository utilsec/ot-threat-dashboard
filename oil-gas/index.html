Info Research — Isolation readiness, practitioner guidance

Six steps to being able to disconnect — and a planning horizon of three months running isolated

Nozomi Networks published a practitioner summary on 2 September of the Five Eyes “CI Fortify” guidance released on 28 July, reducing it to six steps: identify vital systems and their dependencies; establish trust levels and connection maps; pre-build the separation points; create and test graduated isolation plans; retain visibility while isolated; and run post-isolation checks before reconnecting. High Critical Advisory — Pipeline leak detection and integrity monitoring

A hard-coded key and MD5 password hashes inside AVEVA Pipeline Integrity Monitor — read access to the project files is enough

CISA published advisory ICSA-26-253-01 on 10 September covering four vulnerabilities in the PIMBoards component of AVEVA Pipeline Integrity Monitor, AVEVA’s real-time leak-detection and integrity product for liquid and gas pipelines. All versions up to and including 2025 SP1 P1 are affected. CVE-2026-81821, a hard-coded cryptographic key (CVSS v3 8.4), lets anyone who can read PIMBoards project files decrypt the sensitive information inside them. CVE-2026-81822, weak MD5 password hashing (CVSS v3 8.4), exposes password hashes to brute force and a potential route to administrator access. CVE-2026-81823, missing authorisation (5.3), allows unauthorised reads of PIMBoards data, and CVE-2026-81824, a cross-site scripting flaw (4.7), lets a malicious link run script in a user’s session. AVEVA found the first two itself; researcher Adham Khairy Ramadan reported the others. The fix is the 2025 SP1 P2 security update, and AVEVA notes that migrating legacy project files to the new cryptography is one-way. No exploitation has been reported. This is the most directly pipeline-specific advisory of the period: whoever can read or administer PIM can see, and potentially change, the leak-detection configuration and dashboards that controllers rely on for safety decisions.

Info Guidance — Detection engineering for critical infrastructure

CISA’s cyber decoys guide: tripwires and honeytokens as detection you can add without touching production

CISA published “Using Cyber Decoys to Strengthen Detection and Response” on 17 September. The guide defines decoys as assets that appear to be legitimate systems, accounts or data but exist to distract adversaries, detect their presence or support intelligence collection, and it structures deployment in three phases: preparation, execution and analysis. CISA positions decoys alongside Zero Trust rather than as a replacement for either segmentation or monitoring. The reason it is worth an oil and gas reader’s time is the constraint it fits: most detection improvements in OT require touching a production system, and decoys mostly do not. A fake engineering workstation credential that no legitimate process uses, a planted project file, a dormant account named to look like a vendor login — each of these produces a signal with a very low false-positive rate and no change to the control system itself. That makes it one of the few detection moves that can be made between turnarounds rather than during one.
