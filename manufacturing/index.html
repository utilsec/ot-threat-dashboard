High Incident — Lateral movement across a shared carrier network

An attacker crossed a shared private cellular network from one operator’s site into another’s plant

CERT Polska’s follow-up report describes an intrusion that started at a wind farm and ended at an unrelated combined heat and power plant, where a turbine was taken out of service. The bridge was a private APN both organisations bought from the same carrier. A private APN is an addressing and billing arrangement, not a security boundary: if the devices on it can reach one another, another subscriber’s compromised router becomes your ingress. Manufacturers should read this as a warning about a common convenience — the cellular links used for remote machine monitoring, OEM diagnostics, outbuildings, tank farms and satellite sites are frequently on exactly this kind of shared arrangement.

Confirmed — CERT Polska documents intrusion and a turbine taken out of service; incident occurred 29 Dec 2025, disclosed 8 Aug 2026 No CVE — the finding is a network design and carrier-isolation failure
No patch applies — remediation is carrier routing policy, per-site isolation and device-level firewalling Action: Ask your carrier in writing whether your APN is shared, whether devices on it can route to one another, and what isolation is available. Until you have the answer, treat every cellular-attached machine, gateway and OEM diagnostic box as internet-exposed: put a firewall you control between the modem and the plant network, disable inbound management on the modem, and use per-site credentials instead of a fleet password. Then verify your remote sites cannot reach each other laterally, which is the specific failure in this case.

The NCSC published a formal alert on 27 August stating it has seen increased targeting of operational technology across multiple sectors globally, including in the UK, by a range of threat actors, with some limited real-world disruption already resulting. No victims, actors or techniques are named. In discrete and process manufacturing that usually means a specific short list: the machine an OEM ships with its own 4G router for remote support, the line acquired with a business unit and never brought under the standard build, the engineering workstation dual-homed for convenience, and the cell that still speaks Modbus or unauthenticated S7comm because the migration project was deferred. High Threat Intel — Ransomware tradecraft, AI-assisted intrusion

A ransomware affiliate ran an AI coding agent inside victim networks — and the exposed server shows exactly how

CloudSEK published analysis on 27 August of a misconfigured server belonging to a Russian-speaking Aurora ransomware affiliate, exposing tooling, command history, credential material and the operator’s Cursor chat transcripts. The campaign ran from April to July 2026 across nine countries; the operator reached domain-level or interactive access at 17 organisations, and four appeared on Aurora’s leak site. Manufacturing, food, agriculture and professional-services organisations were among those affected. For a manufacturer the practical question is whether the identity used by IT administrators can reach the engineering workstations and the MES, because this operator’s route was domain privilege, not an ICS exploit.

Critical Threat intel — Joint federal advisory naming critical manufacturing

Five agencies warn AI-written scripts are reading Siemens S7 controllers, with critical manufacturing named

Joint advisory AA26-231A from NSA, CISA, FBI, DOE and EPA describes actors using AI assistance to turn a well-documented open-source S7 library into custom tooling that queries internet-exposed Siemens S7 controllers in traffic resembling legitimate monitoring. Critical manufacturing is among the named sectors, and the agencies assess the activity as reconnaissance and pre-positioning rather than manipulation. The change worth internalising is not a new exploit. Writing a working S7 query used to require someone who knew the protocol; it no longer does, which enlarges the population capable of interrogating an exposed SIMATIC controller and makes obscurity a much weaker control than it was.

Active reconnaissance confirmed by five federal agencies against internet-exposed S7 controllers; no process manipulation attributed in this advisory No CVE — the advisory describes tooling and tradecraft against exposed controllers

Remove the exposure now rather than scheduling it. Enable access protection with a non-default password on S7-1200 and S7-1500; firewall S7-300 and S7-400 so only the HMI and engineering station can reach them. Then review the historian and engineering workstation logs for read traffic you cannot account for, and ask your machine builders directly whether any of their remote-support arrangements expose a controller to the internet.

Critical Advisory — Actively exploited, CISA KEV, historian and MES databases

A seven-year-old SQL Server flaw is now confirmed exploited — and it is the database under your historian

CISA added six vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 26 August, and the one that matters most on the plant floor is CVE-2019-1068, a remote code execution flaw in Microsoft SQL Server patched in July 2019 and only now confirmed as exploited in the wild. Successful exploitation runs code under the SQL Server Database Engine service account; where that account is over-privileged — which is common on systems installed by an integrator years ago — the attacker moves off the database and onto the Windows host. On a plant network this is the database under the historian, the MES, the batch or recipe store and the quality system — frequently an instance a machine builder or integrator installed as part of a line and left at the build it shipped with, sitting on the same flat network as the engineering workstations. No public write-up of the in-the-wild technique has appeared, so detection guidance is thin; the federal remediation deadline is 29 August. Citrix NetScaler CVE-2026-8452 was added in the same batch.

Critical Advisory — Backup and monitoring infrastructure, unauthenticated

Two unauthenticated flaws in Veeam ONE 13 — the monitoring layer that watches the backups you would restore from

Veeam disclosed vulnerabilities in Veeam ONE 13 in knowledge-base articles published on 25 August. CVE-2026-64633 is rated CVSS v4.0 10.0 and allows remote, unauthenticated code execution on the Veeam ONE agent host; CVE-2026-65641 is rated 9.3 and lets a remote unauthenticated attacker coerce SMB authentication out of the Veeam ONE service account, which is the first step in relaying that account somewhere more useful. In a plant Veeam ONE usually watches the backups of the MES, the historian and the engineering workstations that hold your PLC project files — the recovery capability that decides whether a ransomware event costs you a shift or a fortnight. There is no report of exploitation yet, but backup and monitoring servers are a standing target precisely because compromising them removes the recovery option before the ransomware runs.

Info Policy — EU Cyber Resilience Act reporting obligation

From 11 September 2026 manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities within 24 hours

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s reporting obligations begin on 11 September 2026. From that date, manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must notify an actively exploited vulnerability in their product, or a severe incident affecting its security, within 24 hours of becoming aware — with a fuller report following. The obligation lands on the manufacturer, so it applies to industrial firms that build machinery, instruments or connected equipment as well as to software vendors. The practical difficulty is rarely the report itself; it is that a 24-hour clock requires a detection and decision path that most engineering organisations have never had to build.

Not applicable — regulatory obligation with a forward-looking effective date No CVE
No patch applies — this is a product-compliance obligation under EU Regulation 2024/2847 Action: If your company places connected products on the EU market, decide now who receives a vulnerability report out of hours, who judges whether it is actively exploited, and who is authorised to notify within 24 hours. Publish a security contact and a disclosure route so researchers can reach you at all — many manufacturers currently have none, which makes the clock unmeasurable. Run one dry run against a hypothetical report before September, because the first real one is a poor place to discover the decision path does not exist.
