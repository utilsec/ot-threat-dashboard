Ivanti Connect Secure zero-day chain gave attackers root on fully patched VPNs, and one link is still unpatched

Ivanti disclosed on 13 October that a chain of three flaws was used against a limited number of Connect Secure customers to run commands as root on fully patched appliances. High Campaign — FortiGate firewall and SSL VPN credential theft; no CVE; access feeds ransomware

FBI and Secret Service: FortiBleed credential theft against FortiGate gateways is still running, with targets in all 16 critical-infrastructure sectors

The FBI and US Secret Service warned on 6 October, in joint advisory JCSA-20261006-01, that the FortiBleed campaign against internet-facing FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPNs remains an active threat. There is no CVE: the agencies describe abuse of reused or leaked credentials and legacy SHA-256 password storage, and researchers describe password spraying and stuffing, offline cracking of extracted hashes and a sniffer that captures authentication traffic. Attackers have added admin accounts and deleted or reset legitimate ones, locking owners out. The agencies say targets span all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors, critical manufacturing among them, and the FBI ties the access to Payload ransomware; SOCRadar, which reports overlaps with INC and Lynx, says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments from this access and counted 86,644 working device credentials across 194 countries as of 19 June. No OT system compromise has been described. FortiGate often sits at the IT/OT boundary in plants, so a stolen admin login there is a path toward production networks (editorial assessment).

Medium Cross-sector lesson — confirmed OT attack on French supermarket refrigeration controllers (food retail, not manufacturing)

Attackers took over refrigeration controllers in about 700 French supermarkets; plants run similar web-managed controllers

Over the nights of 11–13 October, attackers remotely changed settings on web-managed refrigeration controllers in French supermarkets, stretching defrost cycles so that some cabinets reached about 50°C, spoiling stock and, according to the Paris prosecutor, starting fires at some stores. Finance Minister Roland Lescure put the toll at about 700 stores on 13 October, across Carrefour, Coopérative U, E.Leclerc, Intermarché/Netto and Monoprix. CERT-FR’s alert CERTFR-2026-ALE-012 named Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC controllers on 12 October, added Copeland xweb and Dixell xweb on 13 October, and on 16 October said attack campaigns are probably still ongoing. Danfoss says most affected units are end-of-life or on outdated firmware, and Copeland points to a discontinued, end-of-life generation. No group has claimed it, no ransom was demanded and the entry route is not established. This is a food-retail incident, not a manufacturing one; we carry it because plants run cold rooms, chillers and building systems from similar web-managed controllers, and any that are reachable from the internet face the same risk (editorial assessment).

Medium Research — AI agents against OT: Booz Allen lab tests and Claroty Team82 exploit reproduction; no new CVE

In lab tests, an AI model found and moved a robotic arm within minutes, and an AI coding agent rebuilt a published controller exploit for about $6

Booz Allen ran frontier AI models in an isolated OT lab with no source code, engineering documents or advanced OT guidance. Across eight scenarios the models met every test objective, chaining asset discovery, device research and attack planning; one went from a perimeter compromise to actions inside the industrial control network in a little over 16 minutes, and another located a robotic arm, mapped its protection zones and motion limits and moved it within minutes. Defenses blocked or slowed some steps, but no single safeguard consistently stopped the models (SecurityWeek, 14 October). Separately, Claroty’s Team82 reported on 13 October that an AI coding agent, redirected by a researcher and connected to the Ghidra reverse-engineering tool, reproduced three previously disclosed Danfoss AK-SM 800A flaws and chained them to root on a lab device in about 28 minutes for roughly $6 of usage, working from publicly downloadable firmware. Team82 stresses this was not autonomous discovery, does not link the flaws to the French supermarket attacks, and says the window between disclosure and a working exploit may be shrinking. Both are lab results, not real-world attacks.

Lab research — no real-world AI-driven OT attack reported No new CVE — Team82 reused published Danfoss flaws (CVE-2025-41450, -41451, -41452)

Critical Exploited edge appliance — Citrix NetScaler ADC/Gateway: three KEV-listed zero-days plus a fourth critical SAML flaw (CVE-2026-107406)

Citrix patches a fourth critical NetScaler flaw, CVE-2026-107406, while the campaign behind three exploited zero-days continues

Citrix published bulletin CTX697191 on 8 October for CVE-2026-107406 (CVSS v4.0 9.5), a memory overflow in NetScaler ADC and Gateway set up as a SAML service provider or identity provider that Citrix says may lead to code execution or denial of service. It is not reported exploited and is not in KEV as of CISA’s 15 October catalog, but the builds that fixed the September zero-days (14.1-73.37 to 14.1-73.41 and 13.1-64.23 to 13.1-64.28) stay vulnerable when used as a SAML identity provider, so those appliances need another upgrade. The campaign itself runs on the three earlier flaws: CVE-2026-88771 and CVE-2026-88772 (KEV 27 September) and CVE-2026-88779 (KEV 4 October), which Citrix and watchTowr describe as a denial-of-service bug; CISA says attackers use it to force reboots so that code planted through CVE-2026-88771 runs. Mandiant counts dozens of victim organizations and describes suspected state-nexus actors without naming a group; Arctic Wolf counts at least 78 targeted organizations, and Shadowserver sees more than 20,000 exposed instances. No manufacturer has been named. NetScaler Gateway commonly fronts contractor and remote-engineer access into plant DMZs (editorial assessment).

Critical Exploited edge flaws — Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager and Fortinet FortiMail, both in CISA KEV

Cisco SD-WAN Manager authentication bypass and a FortiMail file-write zero-day are both exploited and KEV-listed, each at CVSS 9.8

Cisco disclosed on 30 September that CVE-2026-76504 in Catalyst SD-WAN Manager, an improper handling of URI encoding, lets an unauthenticated remote attacker bypass an authentication rule on an API endpoint and gain administrator-level access. Cisco says its PSIRT became aware of active exploitation in September, scores the flaw CVSS 9.8, lists no workarounds and fixes it in 20.9.10.1, 20.12.8.2, 20.15.6.1, 20.18.4.1, 26.1.2.1 and 26.2.1; releases older than 20.9 must migrate. It revised the advisory on 2 October, and CISA added the flaw to KEV on 30 September with a 3 October deadline. BleepingComputer counts it as the fifth exploited SD-WAN zero-day of 2026. Separately, Fortinet’s FG-IR-26-175 (1 October) covers CVE-2026-104286 in FortiMail, a path traversal that lets an unauthenticated attacker write arbitrary files through crafted HTTP or HTTPS requests (CVSS 9.8). Fortinet says it has been reported as exploited in the wild, and CISA listed it in KEV the same day with a 4 October deadline. Fortinet’s 5 October update lists fixed releases 7.4.9, 7.6.7 and 8.0.2, with 7.2 users told to move to 7.4 or later. Neither vendor names an actor or victims. For multi-site manufacturers, SD-WAN Manager is the controller that configures links between plants, warehouses and head office, so admin access there reaches every site at once (editorial assessment).

High Advisory — CISA ICSA-26-281-01, Red Lion N-Tron 700 Series managed industrial switches; seven CVEs; fix firmware 3.11.1

CISA: Red Lion N-Tron 700 switches keep factory admin logins active and accept unauthenticated SNMP changes; the fix is firmware 3.11.1

CISA’s ICSA-26-281-01 (8 October; Update A on 15 October revised the CVSS v4.0 scores) covers seven flaws in Red Lion (HMS Networks) N-Tron 700 Series industrial Ethernet switches, reported by Idaho National Laboratory. The factory default admin credentials stay active after other administrators are configured; SNMP can change configuration, accounts and firmware without authentication and can trigger a TFTP export of plaintext credentials; firmware and bootloader updates are neither signed nor authenticated; and a URL can reboot the switch. CISA lists critical manufacturing among the affected sectors and knows of no public exploitation. A compromised switch can expose or reroute traffic between controllers and HMIs (editorial assessment).

Medium Guidance — Third-party ICS integrators; NIST OT security guide draft

CISA and the FBI tell operators to rein in third-party ICS integrators after a 2025 integrator breach exposed customer SCADA files

CISA and the FBI published joint guidance on 24 September for critical infrastructure operators working with third-party ICS integrators. It draws on a March–April 2025 compromise of a US industrial automation company in which a foreign actor searched for “customers” and “SCADA” and staged about 800 files of customer SCADA information, ICS device details and schematics in nine archives. The agencies urge contract security requirements, least privilege, monitored and on-demand remote access, a full inventory of integrator-supplied equipment, offline backups and the ability to operate manually if the integrator is compromised. The victim’s named customers were power and transportation entities, not manufacturers, but plants routinely give OEMs, machine builders and integrators standing access to PLCs, robots and MES interfaces. Separately, NIST released the initial public draft of SP 800-82 Rev. 4, its OT security guide, on 21 September, restructured around CSF 2.0; comments are due 30 November.

Medium Regulation — CIRCIA final rule under White House review; text not yet public

CISA’s final CIRCIA incident-reporting rule has reached White House review; who it covers will not be known until it is published

The Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs logged CISA’s final rule under the Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act (RIN 1670-AA04) for review on 1 October. The law sets 72-hour reporting for covered cyber incidents and 24-hour reporting for ransom payments by covered entities in critical-infrastructure sectors, which include critical manufacturing, but those duties start only on dates the final rule sets, and the text, including which entities are covered, is not public. The statutory deadline passed in October 2025 and CISA’s later targets of May and September 2026 slipped; HIPAA Journal reports publication is expected before year-end. In a report released 28 September from a July roundtable, GAO found that industry participants flagged the forthcoming CIRCIA rule and the SEC’s disclosure requirement as problems, citing differing thresholds, timelines and definitions. OIRA review is procedural, and the text can still change.
