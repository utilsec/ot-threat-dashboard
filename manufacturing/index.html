Coca-Cola’s Fairlife suspended all US dairy production after a ransomware attack

Coca-Cola’s Fairlife dairy subsidiary suspended all US production on 16 July 2026 following a ransomware attack on production-related systems, disclosed via SEC filing. Critical Incident — OT disruption

Attackers took hands-on control of water-utility PLCs across a dozen states — locking out operators and knocking a treatment plant offline

Between 26–27 July 2026 a coordinated campaign disrupted automated controls at more than 30 Minnesota community water systems, and confirmed intrusions have since been reported across roughly a dozen states including Georgia, Michigan, South Dakota, Alabama and New Jersey. This is hands-on manipulation of control equipment, not a data breach: CISA says the actors changed PLC passwords and altered device IP addresses to lock operators out of their own equipment. In Braham, Minnesota a pump failure forced a treatment facility offline and prompted a resident conservation notice; Clayton County, Georgia issued a precautionary boil-water advisory after a pump-station disruption, later lifted. Dragos’s Rob Lee told NPR he could not recall “this many targets at once with operational impact.” Every affected utility reported that drinking water stayed safe, with staff falling back to manual operation. Federal officials have pointed to Iran-linked activity — a TLP:AMBER WaterISAC assessment reportedly found the evidence aligned with campaigns previously attributed to Iran — but no formal public attribution has been made, so treat the actor as suspected rather than confirmed.

Confirmed OT-disruptive incident — operator lockout, pump failure, plant offline across multiple utilities No single CVE — abuse of internet-exposed PLCs, default/absent credentials and weak remote access
No patch — architectural: remove OT from the internet, credential and remote-access hygiene Action: Today, not this quarter: find every controller, HMI and cellular router of yours reachable from the internet and take it off. Then change every default and shared PLC password, put remote access behind MFA and a broker, back up and hash your PLC project files and logic so you can prove and restore known-good state, and rehearse running the plant manually with the SCADA layer assumed hostile.

Critical Advisory — CVSS 10.0

A CVSS 10.0 command injection in Haiwell’s cloud HMI gateway hands an unauthenticated attacker root

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-02 covers CVE-2026-19188, an OS command injection (CWE-78) in the Haiwell IoT Cloud HMI Gateway that scores the full CVSS 10.0. The flaw sits in the device’s Net Check function: an unauthenticated attacker can reach the cmdPing Socket.io event and execute arbitrary commands as root. CISA flags the product’s use in energy, critical manufacturing and water/wastewater environments. Affected firmware is 3.40.1.12; the fix is 3.50.1.19. No in-the-wild exploitation had been reported at publication, which is the good news — a maximum-severity, unauthenticated, internet-reachable root execution on a gateway product is exactly the profile that gets weaponised quickly once someone bothers.

No confirmed exploitation reported at publication CVE-2026-19188 — not in CISA KEV as of this edition
Available — firmware 3.50.1.19 Action: Inventory for Haiwell gateways — they arrive through machine builders and integrators more often than through your own procurement, so an asset-register search may miss them. Patch to 3.50.1.19, and in the meantime confirm none is reachable from the internet or from your business network.

High Advisory — SCADA

AVEVA Enterprise SCADA deserialisation flaw could let tampered data become code execution

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-01 covers CVE-2025-7639, a deserialisation-of-untrusted-data vulnerability in AVEVA Enterprise SCADA carrying CVSS v3.1 7.1. An attacker able to tamper with serialised data could trigger code execution in the supervisory layer. Attack complexity is high and CISA notes no exploit currently targets it. Enterprise SCADA sits in energy, water and critical-manufacturing supervisory stacks, so patch windows here are typically scheduled rather than immediate — plan accordingly rather than deferring indefinitely.

CISA added two N-able N-central authentication-bypass flaws to KEV within two days: CVE-2026-18556 and CVE-2026-18577, both CVSS 8.2, the second being an incomplete-fix follow-on to the first. High Advisory — Automotive

A hardcoded key in Acrisure KARR anti-theft units means compromising one device exposes the fleet

CISA advisory ICSA-26-216-01 covers a hard-coded cryptographic key (CWE-321) in Acrisure’s KARR BT and DR-100 vehicle anti-theft and telematics units, tracked as CVE-2026-18411 with CVSS 8.1 per corroborating reporting. An attacker within Bluetooth range can unlock doors or engage immobilisation without authenticating. Because the key is shared across units rather than per-device, compromising one unit exposes every unit that ships with it — the defining property of hardcoded-secret flaws and the reason they are worth more attention than their individual scores suggest. Firmware dated 20 July 2026 or later resolves it. No public exploitation had been reported at publication.

No public exploitation confirmed at publication CVE-2026-18411 — not in CISA KEV
Available — firmware dated 2026-07-20 or later Action: For fleet operators, get the firmware update scheduled through your aftermarket installer. More broadly, treat this as a design-review prompt: ask any embedded-device supplier whether cryptographic keys are per-device or shared across the product line, because the answer determines whether one compromise is one problem or a fleet-wide one.

High Research — Black Hat

A 2024 truck-brake recall quietly fixed remote code execution — and none of it got a CVE

Research presented at Black Hat USA 2026 showed that a 2024 safety recall of Bendix EC80 heavy-truck brake electronic control units, covering roughly 450,000 units across three OEMs, quietly fixed buffer-handling flaws enabling remote code execution and denial-of-service, a hardcoded password that disabled traction control, and memory-corruption crashes. None of the issues received CVE identifiers. Researchers demonstrated triggering the bugs via software-defined radio through diagnostic ports, halting CAN bus traffic and disabling ABS and steering assist. The security-relevant finding is the disclosure gap: a safety recall was used as the remediation channel, so the vulnerabilities never entered any vulnerability database, and no fleet operator scanning CVE feeds would ever have learned about them.

Proof-of-concept demonstrated by researchers via SDR through diagnostic ports No CVEs assigned — remediated through a safety recall instead
Available — addressed in the 2024 recall; recall completion still being tracked Action: If you operate a vehicle or mobile-equipment fleet, cross-check outstanding safety recalls against your units — a recall may be your only notification channel for a security fix. For anyone specifying embedded equipment, put CVE assignment for security-relevant defects into the contract, not just a recall obligation.

High Research — Exposure

Researchers found 4,407 internet-facing Rockwell controllers — and 19 of 22 in already-attacked cities ran firmware vulnerable to a 2017 bug

A Cloud Security Alliance scan on 3 August 2026 identified 4,407 internet-facing Rockwell / Allen-Bradley controllers worldwide, 2,844 of them in the United States. The more uncomfortable finding is local: of 22 exposed controllers located in cities already hit by the July campaign, 19 were still running firmware vulnerable to CVE-2017-16740, a buffer-overflow flaw patched back in 2017. Separately, CISA Acting Director Nick Andersen said federal responders assisting with the recovery are still finding water-system PLCs reachable from the internet with factory-default credentials or no password at all. Broader counts cited during the response put roughly 10,000 Rockwell, Siemens and Schneider devices on the public internet sector-wide. The takeaway is not a novel zero-day; it is that a decade of unpatched, unauthenticated exposure is doing the work for the attacker.

No confirmed exploitation of this specific CVE in the campaign — but the exposure it measures is being actively abused CVE-2017-16740 — not in CISA KEV
Available — patched in 2017; MicroLogix 1400 firmware v21.003+ recommended Action: Do not assume this is someone else’s problem. Search your own public IP ranges and your cellular/LTE router estate with Shodan or Censys, from outside your network, and treat anything that answers on an industrial protocol as an incident. Then check controller firmware age — multi-year patch lag is the actual finding here.

High Advisory — Nation-state

The federal PLC advisory grew beyond Rockwell — Schneider Modicon and Siemens S7-1200 are now named too

CISA, the FBI, EPA and NSA updated joint advisory AA26-097A on Iran-affiliated exploitation of internet-exposed programmable logic controllers, widening the documented target set from Rockwell MicroLogix and CompactLogix to also include Schneider Electric Modicon M340 / BMX P34 and Siemens S7-1200 devices. The update adds July 2026 indicators of compromise, the MITRE ATT&CK exfiltration technique T1041, and detection guidance for malicious modification of reusable Rockwell Add-On Instruction code blocks — the logic building blocks that water-treatment and other process programs are assembled from. The advisory names energy and government facilities alongside water and wastewater as targeted sectors. It is the foundational document behind the multistate water-utility incidents, and the detection guidance is worth reading even if you run none of the three named vendors.

Active — exploitation of exposed PLCs documented by four federal agencies No CVE named in the advisory — exposure and weak authentication, not a single product flaw
Mitigations only — no universal patch; segmentation, credential and exposure controls Action: Pull the AA26-097A indicators into your monitoring and hunt against them. Specifically check whether any Add-On Instruction or reusable logic block in your controllers has been modified outside a change window, and compare running logic against your source-of-truth repository — most sites have never done this comparison and cannot currently tell.
