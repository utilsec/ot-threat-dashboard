Critical Incident — Law-enforcement action against state-sponsored infrastructure

FBI seizes the Chinese scanning and relay platforms built out of other people’s IoT devices

The Justice Department and FBI announced court-authorised seizures on 26 August of QScan and QTRouter, two linked platforms operated by a PRC state-sponsored group the government calls QTFY and ties to a Nanjing contractor. QScan handled reconnaissance against US critical infrastructure; QTRouter relayed intrusions through compromised routers and IoT devices so traffic appeared to come from ordinary domestic addresses. Both are now inoperable because the seized domains were hard-coded into the malware. Named victims are federal, but the relay design is the manufacturing lesson: unmanaged internet-connected devices in a plant — cameras, badge readers, HVAC controllers, machine-monitoring gateways — are exactly what these platforms recruited, and your firewall logs never showed who was really there.

Critical Incident — PLM supply-chain campaign with named victims

Clop has named more than 40 PTC Windchill victims, and what it took is how your products are built

The Clop extortion group has listed over 40 organisations as victims of a campaign against internet-facing PTC Windchill product lifecycle management deployments, with manufacturing, aerospace and industrial suppliers among the confirmed names. Windchill is where design files, bills of material, supplier documentation, tolerances and change history live. Losing it is not a privacy incident. It is the disclosure of how a product is made, to whom it is sourced, and where the process is sensitive — and for firms whose competitive position rests on process knowledge rather than a patent, that is the whole asset. The campaign also demonstrates the pattern: attackers now go after the engineering record rather than the shop floor.

High Incident — Lateral movement across a shared carrier network

An attacker crossed a shared private cellular network from one operator’s site into another’s plant

CERT Polska’s follow-up report describes an intrusion that started at a wind farm and ended at an unrelated combined heat and power plant, where a turbine was taken out of service. The bridge was a private APN both organisations bought from the same carrier. A private APN is an addressing and billing arrangement, not a security boundary: if the devices on it can reach one another, another subscriber’s compromised router becomes your ingress. Manufacturers should read this as a warning about a common convenience — the cellular links used for remote machine monitoring, OEM diagnostics, outbuildings, tank farms and satellite sites are frequently on exactly this kind of shared arrangement.

Confirmed — CERT Polska documents intrusion and a turbine taken out of service; incident occurred 29 Dec 2025, disclosed 8 Aug 2026 No CVE — the finding is a network design and carrier-isolation failure
No patch applies — remediation is carrier routing policy, per-site isolation and device-level firewalling Action: Ask your carrier in writing whether your APN is shared, whether devices on it can route to one another, and what isolation is available. Until you have the answer, treat every cellular-attached machine, gateway and OEM diagnostic box as internet-exposed: put a firewall you control between the modem and the plant network, disable inbound management on the modem, and use per-site credentials instead of a fleet password. Then verify your remote sites cannot reach each other laterally, which is the specific failure in this case.

Critical Threat intel — Manufacturing share of industrial ransomware

Manufacturing took 747 of the 1,140 industrial ransomware incidents in the second quarter

Dragos counted 1,140 ransomware incidents at industrial organisations in Q2 2026, up around 12 percent on Q1, with manufacturing absorbing the clear majority. The analysis is blunt about mechanism: attackers did not need control-system access to stop production. Encrypting ERP, MES, quality systems, label printing, shipping and scheduling halts a line just as effectively as touching a PLC, and it is far easier. Manufacturers who scope OT security around the control network alone are defending the part attackers are not aiming at.

Critical Threat intel — Joint federal advisory naming critical manufacturing

Five agencies warn AI-written scripts are reading Siemens S7 controllers, with critical manufacturing named

Joint advisory AA26-231A from NSA, CISA, FBI, DOE and EPA describes actors using AI assistance to turn a well-documented open-source S7 library into custom tooling that queries internet-exposed Siemens S7 controllers in traffic resembling legitimate monitoring. Critical manufacturing is among the named sectors, and the agencies assess the activity as reconnaissance and pre-positioning rather than manipulation. The change worth internalising is not a new exploit. Writing a working S7 query used to require someone who knew the protocol; it no longer does, which enlarges the population capable of interrogating an exposed SIMATIC controller and makes obscurity a much weaker control than it was.

High Threat intel — Federal red-team assessment findings

CISA’s red team took a domain undetected — the tooling was fine, the monitoring was not

CISA advisory AA26-237A publishes paired red-team results from two critical-infrastructure organisations assessed with comparable tradecraft. One detected and responded; the other never noticed a domain compromise. The advisory is unusually direct that the difference was not product spend — both had capable tooling, but only one reviewed the output, correlated identity events and had practised the response. Manufacturers recognise the pattern because engineering workstations, MES servers and corporate laptops so often share one Active Directory, and because plant-floor systems are frequently excluded from whatever monitoring exists on the grounds that alerts would be noisy.

The NetScaler flaw is now confirmed exploited and in KEV — federal deadline 29 August

CISA added CVE-2026-8452 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 26 August, moving the Citrix NetScaler ADC and Gateway issue from a patching recommendation to confirmed exploitation in the wild. It is a heap overflow in SAML single sign-on parsing, rated CVSS 8.8, requiring no authentication, and reachable when the appliance runs as a Gateway or AAA virtual server. In manufacturing, NetScaler routinely fronts the remote access used by engineers, third-shift support and the machine builders who dial in to diagnose equipment.

Critical Advisory — Actively exploited, CISA KEV, engineering toolchain

The Git server holding your PLC projects just went into KEV — Gitea is being exploited in the wild

CISA added CVE-2026-60004 to the KEV catalogue on 25 August after reports of active exploitation. Rated CVSS 9.8, it lets anyone with ordinary repository write access plant an executable Git hook and run shell commands as the Gitea service account. Gitea 1.27.1 fixed it on 27 July; exploitation followed within a month. Self-hosted Gitea has become common in controls engineering because it is trivial to stand up inside a plant network for PLC projects, HMI screens, robot programs and configuration baselines. That convenience is the risk: a low-privilege contractor account converts into code execution on the machine holding the authoritative copy of your production logic. Federal remediation was due 28 August.

Upgrade to 1.27.1 or later, audit repository write permissions, and remove contractor and former-employee accounts. Review server logs for hook creation and unexpected process execution since late July. Then confirm that the authoritative copies of PLC, robot and HMI programs exist somewhere other than this server, offline and verified; if the repository is the only copy, that dependency is the more serious finding.

CVSS 10.0 on a Siemens edge gateway — unauthenticated code execution on a dual-homed device

Siemens’ August advisory set includes a maximum-severity missing-authentication flaw in SIMATIC IoT2050 Advanced devices, allowing a remote unauthenticated attacker to execute code with elevated privileges. High Advisory — Serial-to-Ethernet gateway, no vendor patch

No login required and no patch coming — the Ebyte gateway advisory is a replacement decision

CISA advisory ICSA-26-237-06 covers the Ebyte NE2-D11 serial-to-Ethernet gateway, whose web management interface does not consistently enforce authentication before granting administrative functionality. An unauthenticated remote attacker can read configuration, change settings, hijack sessions or disrupt device operation. CISA notes the vendor acknowledged the reports and said a patch was in development, then stopped responding, so no fix status exists. Cheap serial converters like this are endemic in manufacturing: they are what got a legacy press, oven, torque controller, scale or test rig onto the network, and they are almost never in a patch programme or an asset register.

High Advisory — Exploited Windows IKE flaw in KEV

An exploited Windows IKE flaw — pre-authentication SYSTEM on the box terminating your site-to-site VPN

CISA added four exploited vulnerabilities to KEV on 18 August, including a Windows Internet Key Exchange flaw permitting pre-authentication code execution as SYSTEM. Placement is what makes it relevant on a plant floor: IKE runs on the hosts terminating site-to-site VPN tunnels, and those tunnels are how a corporate data centre reaches a plant, how a plant reaches a warehouse or a satellite facility, and how a machine builder reaches equipment through a shoreside concentrator. Compromise there puts an attacker precisely at the junction of both networks.

Info Policy — EU Cyber Resilience Act reporting obligation

From 11 September 2026 manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities within 24 hours

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s reporting obligations begin on 11 September 2026. From that date, manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must notify an actively exploited vulnerability in their product, or a severe incident affecting its security, within 24 hours of becoming aware — with a fuller report following. The obligation lands on the manufacturer, so it applies to industrial firms that build machinery, instruments or connected equipment as well as to software vendors. The practical difficulty is rarely the report itself; it is that a 24-hour clock requires a detection and decision path that most engineering organisations have never had to build.

Not applicable — regulatory obligation with a forward-looking effective date No CVE
No patch applies — this is a product-compliance obligation under EU Regulation 2024/2847 Action: If your company places connected products on the EU market, decide now who receives a vulnerability report out of hours, who judges whether it is actively exploited, and who is authorised to notify within 24 hours. Publish a security contact and a disclosure route so researchers can reach you at all — many manufacturers currently have none, which makes the clock unmeasurable. Run one dry run against a hypothetical report before September, because the first real one is a poor place to discover the decision path does not exist.
