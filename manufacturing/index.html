High Incident — Cross-sector read-across

A small UK generator lost four days to a cyberattack — and plant-site self-generation sits in exactly that blind spot

The Sunday Telegraph reported on 22 August that Iran-linked hackers took a British power plant offline for four days. The UK Department for Energy Security and Net Zero has since confirmed on the record that an incident occurred at a small-scale energy generator, and that the wider energy system was never at risk; the four-day outage, the attribution to Iran and the “unprecedented” label remain Telegraph sourcing that the NCSC would not comment on. The reason it belongs on a manufacturing page is the detail underneath: the NCSC is understood to have logged no outage reports from regulated power station operators, meaning the affected generator sat below the regulated reporting threshold. That describes a very large amount of industrial infrastructure. On-site CHP, cogeneration and steam plant, standby and peaking generators, plant battery storage and behind-the-meter renewables are generating assets that manufacturers own, that fall outside energy-sector regulation, and that are almost always commissioned and remotely supported by the OEM or an integrator rather than run by the site’s own controls team. They frequently sit on their own PLC and HMI stack, reached over a vendor VPN or a cellular router that never appears in the plant network diagram. A four-day loss of one of those assets is not a grid event — it is a production event, and it is yours. Read alongside AA26-231A naming Siemens S7 controllers an active target across critical manufacturing, the pattern is consistent: pressure is landing on the reachable, lightly-governed edge of the plant.

Critical Incident — Nation-state / PLCs

Five federal agencies warn AI-written scripts are reading Siemens S7 controllers, critical manufacturing named first

The NSA, CISA, FBI, Department of Energy and EPA issued a joint advisory on 19 August warning that threat actors are combining the open-source snap7 and python-snap7 automation libraries with AI-assisted scripting to build custom tools that mimic legitimate OT monitoring solutions. Those tools provide read and write access to Siemens S7 controller memory, configuration data and ladder logic programs over S7comm. Critical manufacturing is named first among the most-targeted sectors, ahead of energy, water, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities. The agencies state that this is an active threat rather than a theoretical one, and that AI-generated exploitation scripts dramatically reduce the expertise and time needed. Actors find exposed or poorly segmented devices with internet scanning services and gain access through default or weakly configured credentials. Affected lines span S7-200, S7-300 including 314, 315 and 317, S7-400, S7-1200 CPUs 1211C through 1217C, and S7-1500 including F-series safety controllers. The agencies assess current activity as reconnaissance and capability development, with read access used to prepare for later write operations.

High Incident — OT pivot / Connectivity

Attackers crossed from a wind farm into an unrelated plant over a shared private cellular APN

CERT Polska disclosed on 9 August that attackers reached the industrial control network of a Polish combined heat and power plant serving roughly 50,000 residents by pivoting through a private cellular data network. The intrusion started at an entirely different organisation: an internet-exposed Fortinet VPN and firewall device at a wind farm sharing the same carrier APN. Because the APN was misconfigured to allow arbitrary devices to communicate with one another, the attacker crossed from one operator’s network into another’s controllers. CERT Polska describes this as the first observed real-world use of a private APN as an OT attack vector. The incident occurred on 29 December 2025 and analysis took over three months. The relevance to manufacturers is architectural: private APN and private 5G are being sold into plants for machine connectivity and remote-site telemetry on the premise that they are safer than the public internet, and a flat one is a shared broadcast domain with other subscribers.

Confirmed OT intrusion — control network reached via carrier network pivot No new CVE — exposed edge firewall plus a flat private cellular network
No patch — architectural: segment the APN, treat carrier networks as untrusted Action: Ask your carrier for the routing and device-isolation policy on every private APN or private 5G deployment serving your plants, and get written confirmation devices cannot address each other. Inventory cellular routers installed by machine vendors for remote diagnostics, and put a firewall with an explicit allow-list between each one and the cell it serves.

High Research — Detection and response

CISA’s red team took a domain undetected — the tooling was fine, the monitoring was not

CISA published AA26-237A on 25 August comparing two red-team assessments run with near-identical tradecraft: one against a government services and facilities organisation, one against a water and wastewater systems entity. Both opened with phishing and escalated through Active Directory weaknesses that are ordinary rather than exotic — a Machine Account Quota left at its default of 10, and misconfigured Active Directory Certificate Services templates. The outcomes diverged sharply. At the organisation CISA labels A, the team obtained elevated domain privileges and reached sensitive business systems and cloud resources without ever being detected, despite the organisation running multiple security operations centres and multiple EDR products. CISA’s stated reason deserves reading twice: the SOCs did not communicate with one another, and thousands of false positives from routine business activity buried the real signal. For a manufacturer the relevance is direct and uncomfortable. Engineering workstations, historian servers and MES application hosts are almost always joined to the corporate Active Directory that this tradecraft targets, which means a domain compromise is one trust relationship away from the production estate. Both weaknesses CISA names cost nothing to fix. The detection finding is the one to take to a plant leadership meeting: having more tooling did not help, because nobody was correlating across consoles and routine business noise buried the signal — a familiar shape in multi-site manufacturers where each plant, and often each acquisition, brought its own monitoring arrangement.

No exploitation — this is CISA’s own assessment activity, published as defensive findings No CVE — the findings are configuration and detection failures, not product flaws
No patch — remediation is Active Directory hardening and SOC process Action: Set your domain Machine Account Quota to 0 and grant machine-join rights explicitly. Audit AD Certificate Services templates for the enrolment misconfigurations that allow certificate-based impersonation. Then answer the organisational question: across your sites, who correlates alerts between corporate IT, any MSSP and the OT monitoring platform, and what is the measured false-positive rate on the consoles watching engineering workstations? Finally, verify that authentication for engineering workstations, the historian and MES does not silently inherit corporate domain trust — if a domain admin is implicitly a plant admin, that is the finding to fix first.

Critical Advisory — Exploited web tier, CVSS 10.0

CISA sets a three-day federal deadline on a CVSS 10.0 Oracle web-tier flaw now under active attack

CISA added CVE-2026-21962 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 24 August with a remediation date of 27 August — the tightest window Binding Operational Directive 26-04 permits. The flaw is an improper access control issue in Oracle HTTP Server and in the Oracle WebLogic Server Proxy Plug-in for Apache HTTP Server and Microsoft IIS, scored CVSS 10.0. It is reachable over HTTP by an unauthenticated attacker with no user interaction and no existing privileges, and successful exploitation allows access to the instance or modification of the data it fronts. Oracle shipped the fix in its January 2026 critical patch update; reporting on 25 August describes broad exploitation against WebLogic servers. A three-day federal deadline is the signal worth reading — CISA reserves that window for exploitation it judges both live and consequential. This is not an ICS product and it will not sit on the plant floor, but Oracle’s web tier is a very common reverse proxy in front of the business systems that keep production moving: ERP and MES web front-ends, supplier and customer portals, quality and document management, and the reporting layers that pull from the historian. Dragos’ second-quarter analysis made the point that industrial production stops without anyone writing to a controller — ERP, scheduling and identity are enough. This is exactly that class of exposure, now with confirmed exploitation and a three-day federal clock.

Fire-alarm management software stores its credentials in cleartext in memory, CISA warns

CISA published ICSA-26-232-01 on 20 August covering Johnson Controls Simplex Incident Manager, the software operators use to monitor and manage Simplex fire alarm panels across a property portfolio. Versions up to and including V2.01 keep user passwords and authentication tokens unencrypted in system memory while the application runs, tracked as CVE-2026-27875 and classified as CWE-316. Plants run Simplex fire detection across production halls and warehousing, and the management workstation usually lives with facilities on the business network — shared login, physically accessible, rarely inventoried by the OT team. That is the foothold this flaw converts into working credentials.

High Advisory — SCADA platform / deserialisation

AVEVA Enterprise SCADA can be pushed into executing code through tampered serialised data

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-01, published 13 August as part of a 15-advisory batch, covers a deserialisation flaw in AVEVA Enterprise SCADA. An attacker who already holds the “DNA Authority — Operator” privilege can tamper with serialised data and have it execute as code when the server deserialises it. The 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025 release families are affected, along with certain Enterprise SCADA HMI versions. CISA reports no known public exploitation targeting this flaw. AVEVA’s guidance is to restrict operator rights to trusted users, disallow BLT test clients in production, and audit devices, network topology and perimeter defences; the vendor also points to KB117814 on removing the binary formatter. Enterprise SCADA sits above the line-level controls in process plants and across multi-site operations, which makes it a high-value pivot: code execution on the SCADA server puts an attacker in the same trust zone as the historians, alarm servers and the operator HMIs. The operator-privilege requirement is real mitigation, but shared control-room logins erode it quickly.

Medium Regulation — EU Cyber Resilience Act

From 11 September 2026 manufacturers must report exploited vulnerabilities within 24 hours

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s reporting obligations take effect on 11 September 2026. From that date, manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents affecting the security of those products. The timelines are tight: an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware, a full notification within 72 hours, and a final report within 14 days of a corrective measure being available for an actively exploited vulnerability, or within a month for a severe incident. Reporting goes through a single reporting platform to the relevant national CSIRT, with information made available to ENISA at the same time. Two points are routinely missed. The obligations cover products already on the EU market, not just new launches, so legacy product lines are in scope. And if your machines, controllers or embedded software reach EU customers, you are the manufacturer here — this is not only a supplier concern.

Low Policy — Joint guidance

US and Australian agencies publish guidance on isolating OT and running that way for an extended period

A joint publication from US and Australian agencies sets out how critical infrastructure operators should prepare to isolate vital operational technology and supporting systems from external connectivity, and to keep operating in that isolated state for an extended period rather than the few hours most continuity plans assume. It is guidance and creates no obligation. Its value for a manufacturer is that it converts a claim most plants make confidently — that the line can keep running if corporate is compromised — into a dependency checklist. The items that break first are rarely on the network diagram as control-system dependencies: time synchronisation, Active Directory authentication for HMI logins, licence servers for HMI and historian software, MES order release, and label printing. Several manufacturers have discovered during a real incident that a licence check phoning home was the thing that stopped the line.
