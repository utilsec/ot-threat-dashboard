High Incident — Lateral movement across a shared carrier network

An attacker crossed a shared private cellular network from one operator’s site into another’s plant

CERT Polska’s follow-up report describes an intrusion that started at a wind farm and ended at an unrelated combined heat and power plant, where a turbine was taken out of service. The bridge was a private APN both organisations bought from the same carrier. A private APN is an addressing and billing arrangement, not a security boundary: if the devices on it can reach one another, another subscriber’s compromised router becomes your ingress. Manufacturers should read this as a warning about a common convenience — the cellular links used for remote machine monitoring, OEM diagnostics, outbuildings, tank farms and satellite sites are frequently on exactly this kind of shared arrangement.

Confirmed — CERT Polska documents intrusion and a turbine taken out of service; incident occurred 29 Dec 2025, disclosed 8 Aug 2026 No CVE — the finding is a network design and carrier-isolation failure
No patch applies — remediation is carrier routing policy, per-site isolation and device-level firewalling Action: Ask your carrier in writing whether your APN is shared, whether devices on it can route to one another, and what isolation is available. Until you have the answer, treat every cellular-attached machine, gateway and OEM diagnostic box as internet-exposed: put a firewall you control between the modem and the plant network, disable inbound management on the modem, and use per-site credentials instead of a fleet password. Then verify your remote sites cannot reach each other laterally, which is the specific failure in this case.

Critical Threat Intelligence — Nation-state compromise of network and authentication infrastructure

Fire Ant hijacks the routers and authentication servers sitting between the plant and everything else

Sygnia’s 30 August research puts the China-nexus actor it calls Fire Ant inside network infrastructure rather than on top of it: Cisco IOS XR routers, TACACS authentication servers and Linux management hosts across a large interconnected estate. Router implants suppressed selected syslog messages, opened outbound sessions and served interactive shells that produced no records; on TACACS the actor injected a library into the running daemon and copied administrator credentials as logins were approved. Sygnia reports scanning and connection attempts toward linked critical-infrastructure networks but does not confirm compromise there. Most plants do not run IOS XR. Most plants do depend on a corporate network team, an integrator or an MSP for the routers and the authentication service between the enterprise and the cell — and on those same devices’ logs to prove what happened.

Confirmed — Sygnia documents active compromise of Cisco IOS XR routers, TACACS servers and Linux management hosts; activity against connected critical-infrastructure networks is described as scanning and connection attempts, not confirmed compromise No CVE — the actor abused legitimate administrative functions and deployed custom implants, including a TACACS credential-theft toolset Sygnia calls TacTap; no product vulnerability has been assigned
No patch applies — the exposure is the network, authentication and management tier itself, and the on-box logging that would normally reveal tampering Action: Ask your network owner — corporate IT, the integrator, or the MSP — to evidence three things in writing for every device between the enterprise network and the plant floor. One: that running configurations and firmware are compared against a baseline held off the device, with a date attached. Two: that device, VPN and authentication logs are forwarded to a store the network administrators cannot edit. Three: when network-administrator credentials and any TACACS or RADIUS shared secrets were last rotated. Then look at your own side. If the cell or line network authenticates engineers against the same directory and the same authentication service as the corporate estate, an administrator credential lifted at that layer reaches your engineering workstations without a single vulnerability being exploited.

High Threat Intel — Ransomware tradecraft, AI-assisted intrusion

A ransomware affiliate ran an AI coding agent inside victim networks — and the exposed server shows exactly how

CloudSEK published analysis on 27 August of a misconfigured server belonging to a Russian-speaking Aurora ransomware affiliate, exposing tooling, command history, credential material and the operator’s Cursor chat transcripts. The campaign ran from April to July 2026 across nine countries; the operator reached domain-level or interactive access at 17 organisations, and four appeared on Aurora’s leak site. Critical Threat intel — Joint federal advisory naming critical manufacturing

Critical Critical Advisory — Actively exploited, KEV-listed

A print server chained to unauthenticated code execution — PaperCut’s zero-day pair lands in KEV

PaperCut confirmed on 27 August that it was investigating active exploitation of its NG and MF print-management products, and assigned CVE-2026-81578 and CVE-2026-82078 the following day. Print management almost never appears on an OT asset register. Medium Advisory — Autonomous mobile robot fleet management

Rockwell fixes a weak password-hashing work factor in OTTO Fleet Manager — low drama, but it sits on the AMR control path

Rockwell has patched CVE-2026-75112 in OTTO Fleet Manager, the console that dispatches and monitors autonomous mobile robots on plant floors. The flaw is an insufficient work factor in the bcrypt implementation used to hash stored passwords — it does not hand anyone access on its own, but it makes offline brute-forcing of a stolen hash database materially cheaper. CISA scores it 6.8 (CVSSv3) / 6.9 (CVSSv4) and states it is not remotely exploitable, so this is a housekeeping item rather than an emergency. It earns a place on a manufacturing page for a different reason: fleet managers are a category of system that has quietly become operational. Info Policy — EU Cyber Resilience Act reporting obligation

From 11 September 2026 manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities within 24 hours

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s reporting obligations begin on 11 September 2026. From that date, manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must notify an actively exploited vulnerability in their product, or a severe incident affecting its security, within 24 hours of becoming aware — with a fuller report following. The obligation lands on the manufacturer, so it applies to industrial firms that build machinery, instruments or connected equipment as well as to software vendors. The practical difficulty is rarely the report itself; it is that a 24-hour clock requires a detection and decision path that most engineering organisations have never had to build.
