Iran-linked hackers locked operators out of control panels at small Midwest water stations, the Wall Street Journal reports, as DHS tells Congress that water is increasingly in Iran-backed hackers’ sights

The Wall Street Journal reported on 13 November, citing unnamed US and Israeli officials, that since the summer Iran-linked hackers have locked operators out of control panels at water stations in small Midwestern towns, breached networks aboard two Texas-bound energy tankers and sabotaged supermarket refrigeration in Europe. No utility, state or date has been named for the water cases. Check Point’s Sergey Shykevich linked the activity mainly to CyberAv3ngers, which works under Iran’s cyber command, and acting CISA head Nick Andersen named programmable logic controllers as the equipment Iranian actors threaten. On 18 November Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin’s written testimony to the House Homeland Security Committee said Iran-backed actors have targeted US critical infrastructure since February, with water among the sectors increasingly in their sights, and described the attacks so far as mostly localized disruptions of poorly secured control systems. Iran’s ambassador to France denies that Iran carries out cyberattacks abroad. Separately, Illinois says an attempt on the Algonquin Dam on 16 November was blocked (see Incidents & Attacks). It leads because it is the clearest official signal yet that small, internet-reachable water controllers are the target (editorial assessment).

High Research — China-linked relay network and management-platform exploitation; water among the named sectors; no OT intrusion described

Lumen: Volt Typhoon’s “Silent Train” network has reached targets since 2023 through hijacked routers and flaws in management platforms, and lists water among the sectors hit

Lumen’s Black Lotus Labs reported on 28 October on Silent Train, a framework it attributes to Volt Typhoon, the group US agencies link to the Chinese state, and has seen in consistent use since November 2023. For each operation the operators build a single-use chain, from virtual private servers outside the US through hijacked home-office routers and IoT devices abroad and then in the US, sometimes in under two minutes, and take it apart afterwards, which makes the traffic hard to trace or block. The chains are used to reach targets and exploit IT and network management platforms: Lumen ties the group to flaws in Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN (CVE-2026-20127, CVSS v3.1 10.0, Cisco), VeloCloud Orchestrator (CVE-2026-16812), N-able N-central (CVE-2025-8875 and CVE-2025-8876) and Versa Director, and likely an undisclosed Quest KACE flaw. Its key findings list water among the critical-infrastructure sectors affected, alongside energy, transportation, maritime, rail, aviation and emergency services; Lumen says it has notified hundreds of victims over three years, and Hawaii, California and Texas were the most targeted states. No victim is named and no intrusion into OT is described. These are different flaws from the VeloCloud and Cisco SD-WAN Manager bugs on other cards on this page. In our assessment, these platforms are what a utility’s IT or network provider uses to manage every site at once, so a compromise there reaches all of them.

Confirmed exploitation — Lumen research; the Volt Typhoon attribution is Lumen’s; no victim named and no OT intrusion described Named flaws in CISA KEV — CVE-2026-20127 (added 25 Feb 2026), CVE-2026-16812 (27 Jul 2026) and CVE-2025-8875/-8876 (13 Aug 2025), as of CISA’s 30 Oct catalog; the Versa and Quest KACE flaws have no CVE in the report
Fixes exist for the named CVEs — check each vendor’s advisory; the relay devices are mostly home-office routers, so detection and access control matter most Action: Ask your IT or managed-network provider whether it runs Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN, VeloCloud Orchestrator, N-able N-central, Versa Director or Quest KACE for you, confirm each is patched and that its management interface is not reachable from the internet, and review administrator logins for short sessions from unfamiliar addresses. Replace end-of-life routers at remote sites, change default credentials, and use CISA’s Volt Typhoon guidance (AA24-038A), which Lumen cites, for hunting.

Claroty finds 65,000 game consoles, smart speakers and cars on critical-infrastructure networks, most of them on production segments
