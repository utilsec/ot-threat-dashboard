Water-utility PLC campaign now spans at least 12 states — and federal agencies formally assess who is behind it

A campaign that began around 26–27 July 2026 has now touched water and wastewater utilities in at least 12 states, and federal agencies have formally assessed who is behind it. Actors reached internet-exposed Rockwell Automation and Allen-Bradley MicroLogix 1100 and 1400 controllers, changed device IP addresses and set unauthorised passwords, locking operators out of monitoring and control of their own equipment. More than 30 community water systems in Minnesota were affected, including a brief state of emergency in Maple Plain and a pump failure and conservation order in Braham; Clayton County, Georgia issued a boil-water advisory after a pump-station failure; nine systems in Michigan and a wastewater lift station in Rapid City, South Dakota were also hit. That is an official assessment, not proven attribution, and it does not individually cover the newest New Jersey and Alabama incidents; press coverage describes those as Iran-linked rather than formally attributed. No CVE was exploited: the advisory describes actors using leased third-party infrastructure and the manufacturers’ own PLC programming software to reach misconfigured, internet-accessible controllers that lack adequate access control. Officials say drinking-water safety itself has not been compromised at any affected utility.

High Incident — Water utility

New Jersey and Alabama become the newest states hit in the water-utility PLC campaign

SecurityWeek reports that New Jersey and Alabama have joined the multistate campaign against water and wastewater operational technology, bringing the confirmed count to at least 12 states. In New Jersey, systems in Cape May and Woodbine were affected; in Alabama, the city of Childersburg’s water, sewer and gas systems were targeted. As in earlier incidents, the pattern is consistent with actors reaching internet-exposed Rockwell/Allen-Bradley MicroLogix controllers and changing passwords or IP addresses to lock operators out. Neither incident has been separately and formally attributed by federal agencies; press coverage describes them as Iran-linked, consistent with the broader campaign CISA and the FBI have tied to Iranian-affiliated actors in advisory AA26-097A, but that advisory does not name these two incidents individually. Officials have not reported any compromise of drinking-water safety in either state.

High Incident — Water utility

Multistate campaign produced real operational consequences — a state of emergency, a pump failure, a boil-water notice

Beyond locked-out controllers, the water campaign has produced confirmed physical and operational effects in several states. Maple Plain, Minnesota declared a brief state of emergency, and Braham, Minnesota experienced a pump failure that triggered a water-conservation order; more than 30 Minnesota community water systems were affected in total. Clayton County, Georgia issued a boil-water advisory after a pump-station failure tied to the intrusion. Michigan confirmed incidents at nine systems, and Rapid City, South Dakota reported an incident at a wastewater lift station. Despite these effects, officials at the state and federal level say drinking-water safety itself was not compromised — the consequences documented so far are operational disruption and loss of operator visibility and control, not contamination. This is the confirmed physical-consequence evidence behind the sector’s High threat rating.

Critical Advisory — Nation-state

Federal agencies formally assess Iranian-affiliated CyberAv3ngers is behind the water-utility PLC campaign

The FBI, CISA, NSA, EPA, the Department of Energy, USCYBERCOM’s Cyber National Mission Force and the Treasury Department jointly issued Cybersecurity Advisory AA26-097A, “Iranian-Affiliated Cyber Actors Exploit Programmable Logic Controllers Across US Critical Infrastructure.” Originally published 7 April and updated 22 July 2026, it states the agencies assess a group of Iranian-affiliated APT actors — CyberAv3ngers, also known as the Shahid Kaveh Group and affiliated with the IRGC’s Cyber Electronic Command — is conducting this activity. The advisory names no CVE: it describes actors using leased third-party infrastructure and the manufacturers’ own PLC programming software to reach misconfigured, internet-accessible controllers that lack adequate access control, rather than exploiting a software vulnerability. This is an agency assessment, short of formal proof, and it predates and does not individually cover the newest New Jersey and Alabama incidents. It remains the foundational document behind the multistate campaign and the basis for treating this as nation-state hands-on manipulation of US water-sector OT.

Active — exploitation of exposed controllers documented and ongoing No CVE named — exposure and weak/absent credentials across affected controllers
Mitigations only — remove PLCs from the internet, strong unique credentials, MFA, validate project files Action: Read AA26-097A’s mitigation list against your own environment line by line, not just the headline advice — it specifically calls for validating PLC project files for unauthorised modification, which most utilities have never done and cannot currently do quickly.

High Advisory — Rockwell

Rockwell updates MicroLogix hardening guidance after actors were found hijacking exposed controllers

Rockwell Automation updated security advisory SD1790 on 10 August 2026 with recovery and hardening steps for MicroLogix 1400 and 1100 controllers, after actors were observed remotely changing IP addresses and setting unauthorised passwords on internet-exposed units — the same device families at the center of the multistate water campaign. Rockwell explicitly warns that a password change alone is not sufficient mitigation; the advisory calls for placing these legacy controllers behind a firewall or VPN, restricting network access, and validating that current device configuration and logic match known-good baselines. MicroLogix 1400/1100 are discontinued, widely deployed legacy product lines, which is part of why they remain reachable from the internet at scale across the water sector.

Active — devices being reconfigured directly by attackers, not via a software flaw No CVE — exposure and credential hijacking of legacy controllers

Confirm the device sits behind a firewall or VPN, is not reachable from the open internet, and that its IP address and logic match your documented baseline.

Critical Advisory — Haiwell

Maximum-severity command-injection flaw in a Haiwell HMI gateway used in water and energy environments

A CVSS 10.0 vulnerability, CVE-2026-19188, affects the Haiwell IoT Cloud HMI Gateway: OS command injection in the Net Check feature’s cmdPing Socket.io event, reachable through the /setting endpoint, gives an unauthenticated attacker root-level remote code execution. NVD published the flaw 14 August 2026; version 3.40.1.12 is vulnerable, and Haiwell has fixed it in 3.50.1.19 or later. These gateways are deployed in energy and water/wastewater environments as remote HMI and monitoring access points — the same role played by other internet-facing devices implicated in the current water campaign. No public exploitation has been confirmed at this writing, but a maximum-severity, unauthenticated, root-level flaw on internet-facing HMI gateways deserves urgent attention given how this campaign has unfolded.

High Advisory — AVEVA

AVEVA patches a deserialization flaw in Enterprise SCADA and HMI exploitable by a logged-in operator

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-01 covers CVE-2025-7639 in AVEVA Enterprise SCADA and HMI, an untrusted-deserialization vulnerability, CVSS 7.1, that lets an authenticated user with operator-level privileges execute arbitrary code. AVEVA has shipped fixes across its supported branches: Enterprise SCADA 2025 P1 and later, 2024 SP1 P2 and later, 2023 SP1 P1 and later, 2022 SP2 P3 and later, and 2021 SP2 P6 and later, plus HMI 2024 R2 HF7 and later. Because exploitation requires an authenticated operator session rather than an anonymous network attacker, the practical risk concentrates in environments with shared or loosely managed operator accounts, or where an attacker has already obtained low-privilege access — exactly the kind of account-hygiene gap the current water-sector campaign has exploited elsewhere.

No confirmed exploitation reported at publication Not in CISA KEV as of this edition
Available — patched in the version ranges above Action: Patch AVEVA Enterprise SCADA/HMI to the fixed release for your branch, and while you’re in there, audit operator accounts — shared logins and stale accounts are what turn an ‘authenticated user’ bug like this into a real risk.

High Research — Exposure

Researchers count thousands of exposed Rockwell PLCs still online, and CISA keeps finding more

Forescout’s Vedere Labs identified 4,407 internet-exposed Rockwell Automation/Allen-Bradley controllers worldwide, 2,844 of them in the United States, dominated by the discontinued legacy MicroLogix 1400/1100 families — including 22 devices in cities already confirmed hit by the water attacks. Forescout notes the intruders reconfigured devices directly rather than exploiting a new software flaw, consistent with AA26-097A’s account. Separately, CISA says it continues to find exposed water-sector control devices while assisting affected utilities, even as the agency and vendors publicize the exposure. Dragos has separately flagged CVE-2021-22681, an existing Rockwell Logix authentication-bypass flaw, as a vulnerability available to attackers given this level of exposure — not as something confirmed exploited in this campaign, but as a reminder that exposure compounds with known unpatched flaws already sitting on these networks.

Dragos: the water sector was warned about exactly this for a decade, and the same gaps are still open

In “Water Under Attack: A Decade of Warnings, and the Same Gaps Still Open,” published 13 August 2026, Dragos’s Michael Rothschild traces a decade of ICS security warnings about internet-exposed water-sector PLCs and default credentials, and argues the current campaign realizes risks the sector has been told about repeatedly rather than anything genuinely novel. Low Research — Analysis

StateScoop: expedient connectivity decisions and a staffing gap, not sophistication, explain the exposure

A StateScoop analysis of the 12-state water campaign quotes cybersecurity experts attributing the scale of exposure to years of expedient internet-connectivity decisions at small utilities — remote access set up for convenience during staffing shortages — combined with a persistent shortage of dedicated cybersecurity expertise at those same utilities. The piece frames the attackers’ method as opportunistic rather than sophisticated: scanning for exposed, poorly secured controllers rather than developing novel exploits. It reinforces the same conclusion running through the sector’s own advisories and Dragos’s retrospective — the fix is exposure reduction and staffing, not a patch.

Medium Policy — Water & wastewater

A Senate bill and a new Water Watch Center both target the small-utility cybersecurity gap the campaign exposed

Senators Amy Klobuchar and Adam Schiff introduced the Water Cyber Shield Act on 13 August 2026, which would give the EPA explicit authority to conduct mandatory cybersecurity assessments of water systems, authorize $300 million a year through state revolving funds, and require a cyber-risk component in resilience planning for large water systems. Separately, the newly launched Water Watch Center is intended to help small, resource-constrained utilities detect and respond to incidents they currently lack the staff to handle on their own. Both arrive directly in response to the multistate campaign and target the same underlying problem: the roughly 50,000 US community water systems are mostly small and cannot independently fund a security program. This is reported as a factual summary of proposals and launches, not an endorsement.

Low Policy — Funding

New York awards more than $9 million to strengthen cybersecurity at 153 water systems

New York State announced over $9 million in SECURE grants to 153 drinking-water and wastewater systems, with up to $50,000 available per system for cybersecurity assessments and up to $100,000 for improvements. The awards follow the state’s March 2026 cybersecurity mandates for water utilities and arrive as the multistate campaign against water-sector OT continues to unfold; New York has not said whether any of its own utilities were directly affected. Programs like this are one of the few practical mechanisms getting a first cybersecurity assessment funded at small systems that otherwise could not afford one.
