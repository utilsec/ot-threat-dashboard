Info Guidance — CISA detection guidance for critical infrastructure

CISA’s new detection guidance starts from the assumption that the attacker gets in — then makes them trip over something

CISA published “Using Cyber Decoys to Strengthen Detection and Response” on 17 September. It defines decoys as assets that look like legitimate systems, accounts or data but exist to distract an adversary, reveal their presence or collect intelligence on them, and it recommends tripwires, breadcrumbs and honeytokens alongside Zero Trust on the explicit assumption that an attacker will eventually obtain some network access. The guide organises planning around the three MITRE Engage goals — expose, affect, elicit — and maps the resulting detections to ATT&CK, with particular attention to the living-off-the-land activity that conventional tooling misses. It sets out three phases, preparation, execution and analysis, and advises iterating continuously with red-team exercises. For water and wastewater the appeal is the economics rather than the novelty: a decoy account, a fake historian export or a bogus engineering-workstation credential costs nothing to run, changes nothing about production systems, and produces an alert that is almost never a false positive — which is a very different proposition from adding monitoring to a plant network that cannot be taken down. We have seen a secondhand report that the guide contains a water-sector case study; the CISA document itself could not be retrieved to confirm that, so it is not asserted here.

Info Threat Intelligence & Research — Analysis, engineering practice

Cyber-informed engineering argues the useful question is not how to keep attackers out, but which consequences you can design away

An analysis published 15 September sets out the case for cyber-informed engineering in critical infrastructure: that industrial systems operate under a constraint IT does not share, because a compromised network does not automatically have to become a catastrophic physical event. The argument is to move the security question upstream into design — identifying the physical consequences that actually matter, then engineering them out with controls that do not depend on the digital system remaining trustworthy. For a water utility with two operators and no security budget, this is the most useful framing available: you may not be able to keep an attacker out of a network, but you can design the plant so that a compromise cannot produce an unsafe dose or an overflow. Mechanical limits and analogue interlocks are cybersecurity controls. This is practitioner analysis rather than incident reporting; it names no victim and describes no attack, but it is the most useful lens available for operators whose detection budget will never match their consequence exposure.

It is a different flaw from CVE-2026-91843 (see the Check Point management card). The fourth, Check Point’s VPN flaw CVE-2026-85102, has its own card. No attribution has been published for any of them, and no water utility has been named as a victim.
