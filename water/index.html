Top Story

The multistate water-utility PLC campaign has reached at least 12 states, and federal agencies name Iranian-affiliated actors

Water and wastewater utilities in at least 12 states have reported cyberattacks on their operational technology since late July. Actors reached internet-exposed Rockwell and Allen-Bradley MicroLogix controllers, changed device IP addresses and set unauthorised passwords, locking operators out of monitoring and control. More than 30 Minnesota community water systems were affected, Clayton County, Georgia issued a boil-water advisory after a pump-station failure, and Michigan, South Dakota, New Jersey and Alabama have all confirmed incidents. Joint advisory AA26-097A assesses that Iranian-affiliated CyberAv3ngers, tied to the IRGC Cyber Electronic Command, is behind the activity. No CVE was exploited: the entry path is controllers reachable from the internet with default, shared or absent credentials. Officials say drinking-water safety has not been compromised at any affected utility.

The method behind the 12-state campaign is not a software vulnerability. Critical Incident — Water & wastewater

The campaign produced real operational consequences: a pressure drop, a boil-water advisory, a lift-station incident

Beyond locked-out controllers, the campaign has caused documented physical effects. Clayton County Water Authority in Georgia, which serves about 300,000 customers in the Atlanta area, reported cyber activity that caused a water-pressure drop and forced a boil-water advisory. Minnesota IT Services reported coordinated activity against more than 30 community water systems statewide beginning 28 July, with Plymouth, South St. Paul, Maple Plain and Braham confirming operational impacts. A wastewater lift station in Rapid City, South Dakota was also affected, and Michigan and New Jersey have reported incidents. In several cases utilities lost remote control of pumps, valves and pressure and reverted to manual operation. Officials maintain that treated-water safety was not compromised.

High Incident — Attribution

AA26-097A now covers Schneider and Siemens PLCs too, and adds detection guidance for tampered code modules

The joint advisory behind this campaign, AA26-097A, was issued on 7 April by the FBI, CISA, NSA, EPA, DOE, US Cyber Command’s Cyber National Mission Force and Treasury, and updated on 22 July. The update matters for water operators in two ways. First, the observed manufacturer scope expanded beyond Rockwell Automation to include Schneider Electric and Siemens PLCs, so a utility that replaced its MicroLogix fleet is not automatically out of scope. Second, the agencies added guidance for detecting malicious changes inside reusable code modules used within Rockwell PLC programs — a reminder that the risk is not only lockout but altered logic. Reported techniques include Dropbear SSH remote access, project-file exfiltration and HMI or SCADA data manipulation that disables shutdown and alarm logic.

Confirmed active campaign — federal assessment of Iranian-affiliated actors No CVE exploited
No patch — logic integrity verification and access control Action: Pull a current copy of every PLC program and diff it against your last known-good baseline, paying particular attention to reusable add-on instructions and routines that implement alarms or interlocks. If you have no baseline, capturing one this month is the single highest-value task on this page.
Suggested SLA: Controller logic baseline capture and comparison — 30 days Owner:

High Research — Exposure

4,407 Rockwell controllers are still reachable from the internet — 22 of them in cities already hit

A Shodan-based scan by Forescout Vedere Labs on 3 August found 4,407 internet-facing Rockwell Automation and Allen-Bradley programmable logic controllers worldwide, 2,844 of them in the United States. Twenty-two sit in cities where water and wastewater utilities have reported attacks since late July, and 19 of those 22 appear vulnerable to CVE-2017-16740, a buffer overflow in MicroLogix 1400 controllers that Rockwell patched nine years ago. Forescout attributes much of the exposure to small and mid-sized utilities connecting controllers to cellular routers for remote monitoring, which keeps staffing costs down but frequently leaves EtherNet/IP or Modbus TCP answering from the open internet. Total exposure is down 47 percent from a 2020 peak of 7,814, which is progress and still far too many.

Directly linked to active campaign — exposed devices in affected cities CVE-2017-16740 not in CISA KEV; exposure is the primary risk
Patch available since 2017 for CVE-2017-16740; exposure removal is the priority Action: Run your own external scan this week rather than assuming: check every public IP your utility owns, plus the addresses your cellular carrier assigns to field routers, for EtherNet/IP (TCP 44818), Modbus (TCP 502) and web interfaces. Anything that answers goes behind a firewall or VPN before the end of the month.
Suggested SLA: External exposure scan and remediation — 7 days (Critical) Owner:

High Threat Intelligence — Ransomware

Gunra ransomware is exploiting known VPN and firewall flaws, and utilities are named among its victims

The FBI, CISA, DoD Cyber Crime Center, NSA, US Secret Service and the Republic of Korea’s National Police Agency published #StopRansomware advisory AA26-222A on Gunra, a double-extortion operation derived from leaked Conti source that moved to an affiliate model in early 2026. Its victim base explicitly includes utilities, alongside healthcare, finance, manufacturing, transport and government. Initial access comes from exposed edge infrastructure — VPN gateways, firewalls and RDP — including the Fortinet FortiOS and FortiProxy authentication-bypass flaws CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472. For a water utility, this is a different failure mode from the PLC campaign: it will not lock you out of a controller, but it will encrypt billing, SCADA historians and the engineering workstations you need to recover.

Separately, confirm your SCADA and historian backups are offline or immutable and that you have restored from one within the last twelve months.

High Threat Intelligence — Virtualisation

A suspected China-nexus actor is mass-exploiting a patched vCenter flaw, with ransomware behind it

Exploitation of CVE-2026-59310, a directory-traversal flaw in Broadcom VMware vCenter rated CVSS 9.8 and patched on 29 July, has been attributed to a suspected China-nexus actor. German incident-response firm QUIRSO counted roughly 361 victim IP addresses across 47 countries, 343 of them appearing between 3 and 5 August, and assesses with moderate confidence that a Chinese-speaking operator is behind it. The chain establishes persistence through cron jobs, systemd units and reverse_ssh, drops a Linux backdoor, and in some cases deploys Babuk-derived ransomware. Mid-sized utilities that consolidated SCADA servers, historians and HMI virtual machines onto a single VMware cluster should treat this as an availability risk to operations, not just an IT patching item.

Actively exploited at scale — ~361 victim IPs across 47 countries Not listed in CISA KEV at generation time
Patch available — Broadcom fix released 29 Jul 2026 Action: Patch vCenter immediately; if it was exposed and unpatched after 3 August, hunt for unexpected cron and systemd entries, reverse_ssh binaries and unfamiliar SSH keys before assuming you are clean. Then check whether any SCADA-supporting VM shares a cluster with general enterprise workloads.

Medium Research — Ransomware

Dragos logged 1,140 industrial ransomware incidents in Q2 — none of them touched a control system

Dragos recorded 1,140 ransomware incidents affecting industrial organisations in the second quarter of 2026, up 12 percent on Q1, with 514 in North America. Manufacturing took 747; organisations supporting ICS environments took 117. Notably, Dragos observed no ransomware operator directly manipulating an industrial control system during the quarter. Disruption came from encrypted enterprise IT and precautionary shutdowns of virtualisation and shared infrastructure. For water utilities this is a useful counterweight to the PLC campaign: the nation-state activity in the headlines does reach controllers, but the far more common ransomware scenario stops at the business network and still takes the plant down, because billing, procurement, lab systems and remote support all live there.

Critical Advisory — HMI gateway

Haiwell IoT Cloud HMI Gateway has an unauthenticated root command-injection flaw rated CVSS 10.0, with water named in scope

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-02 describes CVE-2026-19188, an operating-system command-injection vulnerability in the Haiwell IoT Cloud HMI Gateway rated CVSS 10.0. The Net Check feature reachable via the /setting endpoint passes unsanitised input to the cmdPing Socket.io event, allowing arbitrary commands to run as root. CISA lists Water and Wastewater Systems, Energy and Critical Manufacturing among the affected sectors, and Haiwell has released fixed firmware. This class of low-cost cloud HMI gateway is exactly what small utilities and their contractors deploy on lift stations, booster stations and remote wells so the system can be checked from a phone — the same deployment pattern Forescout blames for most PLC exposure. No public exploitation has been reported to CISA.

No known public exploitation reported to CISA at generation time Not in CISA KEV
Patch available — Haiwell Scada-v3.50.1.19 Action: Ask your integrator and any contractor who maintains remote sites whether Haiwell gateways are installed anywhere in your system, because they often will not appear in your own asset register. Take any internet-reachable unit offline today and apply the vendor firmware.

Regulation & Policy
Info Regulation — US legislation

The Water Cyber Shield Act would give EPA assessment authority and $300 million a year

Senators Amy Klobuchar and Adam Schiff introduced the Water Cyber Shield Act of 2026 on 12 August, directly in response to the attacks on water systems across roughly 12 states. The bill amends the Safe Drinking Water Act and the Clean Water Act to give EPA the authorities it needs as sector risk management agency: expanded power to conduct cybersecurity assessments and to require corrective action when significant vulnerabilities are found. It would also authorise an additional $300 million a year through the Drinking Water and Clean Water State Revolving Funds for cybersecurity improvements. It is an introduced bill, not law, and previous attempts to give EPA mandatory authority over water cybersecurity have not survived the legislative process — but the funding line is the part small utilities should track.
