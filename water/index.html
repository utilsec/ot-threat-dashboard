Five agencies warn AI-written scripts are reading Siemens S7 controllers, with water named a top target sector

The NSA, CISA, FBI, Department of Energy and EPA issued a joint advisory on 19 August warning that threat actors are pairing the open-source snap7 and python-snap7 automation libraries with AI-assisted scripting to build custom tools that mimic legitimate OT monitoring software. Those tools provide read and write access to Siemens S7 controller memory, configuration data and ladder logic over the S7comm protocol. Water and wastewater is named among the most-targeted sectors alongside critical manufacturing, energy, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities. Affected lines run from the legacy S7-200 and S7-300 through the S7-1200 and S7-1500, including F-series safety controllers. The agencies assess the current activity as reconnaissance and capability development, with read access being used to understand target environments and prepare for later write operations.

High Incident — Operational impact

What the attacks actually did: lost view, altered ladder logic, pressure loss and flooding

The FBI and EPA public service announcement, issued 30 July and last updated 6 August, describes the observed technique in enough detail to be useful operationally. Actors targeted internet-exposed Rockwell Automation and Allen-Bradley MicroLogix 1100 and 1400 controllers, remotely tampering with device configuration by changing IP addresses and enabling and setting passwords. The result was loss of view, and in some cases loss of function, over connected equipment. At least one organisation reported modified PLC project files after noticing ladder-logic discrepancies across several of its sites. The agencies also note that similarities in network setups provided by third parties may let an actor repeat a success across multiple customers of the same integrator. Reported operational effects included loss of pressure and flooding, with the agencies pointing out that pressure loss can allow untreated ground water to seep into distribution pipes. How badly a utility was affected depended on whether the device monitored or controlled equipment, and on whether staff could switch to manual.

High Threat intel — Ransomware / edge exploitation

Six agencies warn the Gunra ransomware crew is walking into critical infrastructure through two 2025 Fortinet bugs

CISA, the FBI, the Defense Cyber Crime Center, the NSA, the Secret Service and the Korean National Police Agency published joint advisory AA26-222A on 10 August on Gunra, a ransomware operation that has hit organisations across critical infrastructure sectors since April 2025. The advisory’s operational point is unglamorous: Gunra’s initial access is overwhelmingly through internet-facing edge devices, and specifically two Fortinet flaws — CVE-2024-21762 and CVE-2024-55591 — both patched in 2025 and both still reachable on unpatched appliances. Once inside, the group steals data before encrypting, and runs a double-extortion leak site. The agencies published detection guidance, indicators and a set of mitigations centred on patching edge appliances, enforcing phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication and segmenting recovery infrastructure. Water utilities are squarely in the target set, and the initial-access route matters more than the ransom note. A small system that bought a Fortinet firewall five years ago, had it configured once, and has never had a patch conversation since is exactly the exposure this advisory describes — and unlike the PLC campaign, this one ends with your billing, SCADA historian and backups encrypted.

Critical Advisory — Remote access edge

Citrix is telling admins to patch NetScaler now — an unauthenticated bypass on the appliance that fronts most OT remote access

Citrix published bulletin CTX696939 on 20 August covering two NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway flaws. The serious one, CVE-2026-19490, is an authentication bypass carrying a CVSS score of 9.3: on Gateway and AAA virtual-server configurations an unauthenticated attacker can skip the login step entirely. The second, CVE-2026-19489, is a memory overflow that can produce unpredictable behaviour or a denial of service, and only applies where SIP ALG is enabled on a Large Scale NAT group. Fixed builds are 14.1-73.32 and 13.1-63.21 or later. Citrix, Rapid7 and Finland’s national cyber authority all said they were not aware of in-the-wild exploitation at publication, while urging immediate patching — NetScaler has a long record of exploited edge flaws. Small and mid-size utilities lean on NetScaler-fronted remote access more than they realise, because it usually arrived with a managed-services contract or an integrator rather than a deliberate architecture decision. If an unauthenticated attacker can skip the login on that appliance, the SCADA jump host behind it is the next hop.

Rockwell advisory on MicroLogix tampering includes a recovery procedure that erases the program

Rockwell Automation issued an advisory warning customers about actors targeting its MicroLogix 1400 series devices, published alongside the federal alerts. The company confirmed that attackers have been able to change IP addresses and modify passwords, blocking water system operators from monitoring their own devices. Its recovery guidance for a tampered device is blunt: cycle power, then remove and reinstall the battery. Critical Advisory — Exploited edge / KEV

Cisco ASA and FTD VPN zero-day exploited to reload firewalls, added to CISA KEV

Cisco has patched CVE-2026-20349, a flaw in the remote-access SSL VPN service of Secure Firewall ASA and Secure Firewall Threat Defense software that attackers are exploiting to force appliances offline. A specially crafted HTTP request causes an unexpected reload and a denial-of-service condition, with no authentication and no user interaction required. Cisco’s PSIRT confirmed active exploitation in August, and CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue with a federal remediation deadline of 14 August. Hot fixes cover ASA 9.16, 9.18, 9.20, 9.22, 9.23 and 9.24 and FTD 7.0, 7.2, 7.4, 7.6, 7.7 and 10.0. For a water utility the significance is availability: if this appliance is how your SCADA vendor, integrator or on-call operator reaches the plant, an attacker can remove that path at will — and the current PLC campaign is precisely the situation in which you would want remote visibility to work.

High Advisory — SCADA platform / deserialisation

AVEVA Enterprise SCADA can be pushed into executing code through tampered serialised data

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-01, published 13 August as part of a 15-advisory batch, covers a deserialisation flaw in AVEVA Enterprise SCADA. An attacker who already holds the “DNA Authority — Operator” privilege can tamper with serialised data and have it execute as code when the server deserialises it. The 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025 release families are affected, along with certain Enterprise SCADA HMI versions. CISA reports no known public exploitation targeting this flaw. AVEVA’s guidance is to restrict operator rights to trusted users, disallow BLT test clients in production, and audit devices, network topology and perimeter defences; the vendor also points to KB117814 on removing the binary formatter. Larger water and wastewater authorities run Enterprise SCADA across treatment, distribution and lift-station telemetry. The privilege requirement blunts the risk, but only if operator accounts are actually individual, actually revoked when a contractor leaves, and actually distinct from the vendor support account — three assumptions that regularly fail an audit at a mid-size utility.

Medium Policy — Federal legislation (proposed)

A Senate bill would give EPA mandatory assessment authority and $300 million a year for water utility cybersecurity

Senators Amy Klobuchar and Adam Schiff introduced the Water Cyber Shield Act on 13 August, in the middle of the PLC campaign now reported by utilities in twelve states. The bill would authorise roughly $300 million a year in grants and technical assistance for drinking-water and wastewater cybersecurity, and would give the Environmental Protection Agency authority to require cybersecurity assessments — the gap left when the courts vacated EPA’s 2023 attempt to fold cyber into sanitary surveys. Industry groups have separately pressed Congress for a water-sector reliability organisation modelled on NERC. The bill is at introduction stage only; nothing about it is in force, and past water cyber bills have not advanced. For utility managers the practical value today is planning signal, not compliance obligation.

Medium Policy — Sector oversight

Industry groups press Congress for a water reliability organisation modelled on NERC

In the wake of the attacks, federal authorities and industry groups are pushing together for greater oversight and more resources. The National Association of Water Companies is urging Congress to pass bipartisan legislation creating a water risk and resilience organisation that would advise the EPA on developing uniform cybersecurity standards, operating along the lines of the North American Electric Reliability Corporation. Its chief executive told Cybersecurity Dive that no one should be exempt from meeting compliance standards. The American Water Works Association has written to House and Senate leaders seeking additional funding, wider eligibility for cybersecurity training and improved information sharing. The EPA held a call with hundreds of water industry stakeholders in late July. None of this creates an obligation today, but a sector historically resistant to mandatory cyber standards asking for them is a meaningful shift in posture.

Low Policy — State funding

New York awards nine million dollars to strengthen cybersecurity at 153 water systems

New York has announced roughly nine million dollars in grants to help local governments assess and improve cyber defences at 153 water systems, alongside the state’s cybersecurity regulations for water utilities. The grants are structured in two tiers: Tier 1 covers assessment work such as penetration testing and risk assessments, and Tier 2 covers implementation including firewalls, network segmentation and incident response planning. A source familiar with the programme said New York was not among the states affected by the recent attacks. The relevance beyond New York is the structure rather than the sum. Assessment-only funding tends to produce reports that sit unimplemented; separating an implementation tier acknowledges that the binding constraint at small utilities is usually the capital to act on findings, not the findings themselves. Operators in other states can reasonably point to this design when arguing for state or revolving-fund support.
