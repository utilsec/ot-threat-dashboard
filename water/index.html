Info Guidance — CISA detection guidance for critical infrastructure

CISA’s new detection guidance starts from the assumption that the attacker gets in — then makes them trip over something

CISA published “Using Cyber Decoys to Strengthen Detection and Response” on 17 September. It defines decoys as assets that look like legitimate systems, accounts or data but exist to distract an adversary, reveal their presence or collect intelligence on them, and it recommends tripwires, breadcrumbs and honeytokens alongside Zero Trust on the explicit assumption that an attacker will eventually obtain some network access. The guide organises planning around the three MITRE Engage goals — expose, affect, elicit — and maps the resulting detections to ATT&CK, with particular attention to the living-off-the-land activity that conventional tooling misses. It sets out three phases, preparation, execution and analysis, and advises iterating continuously with red-team exercises. For water and wastewater the appeal is the economics rather than the novelty: a decoy account, a fake historian export or a bogus engineering-workstation credential costs nothing to run, changes nothing about production systems, and produces an alert that is almost never a false positive — which is a very different proposition from adding monitoring to a plant network that cannot be taken down. We have seen a secondhand report that the guide contains a water-sector case study; the CISA document itself could not be retrieved to confirm that, so it is not asserted here.

Info Threat Intelligence & Research — Analysis, engineering practice

Cyber-informed engineering argues the useful question is not how to keep attackers out, but which consequences you can design away

An analysis published 15 September sets out the case for cyber-informed engineering in critical infrastructure: that industrial systems operate under a constraint IT does not share, because a compromised network does not automatically have to become a catastrophic physical event. The argument is to move the security question upstream into design — identifying the physical consequences that actually matter, then engineering them out with controls that do not depend on the digital system remaining trustworthy. For a water utility with two operators and no security budget, this is the most useful framing available: you may not be able to keep an attacker out of a network, but you can design the plant so that a compromise cannot produce an unsafe dose or an overflow. Mechanical limits and analogue interlocks are cybersecurity controls. This is practitioner analysis rather than incident reporting; it names no victim and describes no attack, but it is the most useful lens available for operators whose detection budget will never match their consequence exposure.

Critical Advisory — Actively exploited, CISA KEV, small-site network switch

A small-office managed switch goes into KEV after nearly a thousand were quietly stripped of their configurations and root password hashes — the class of switch that ends up in a pump-house cabinet

CISA added CVE-2026-7273 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 21 September with a federal remediation deadline of 24 September. It is a stack-based buffer overflow in the CGI program of Zyxel GS1900-series managed switches (GS1900-8, 8HP, 10HP, 16, 24, 24E, 24EP, 24HPv2, 48 and 48HPv2) that lets an unauthenticated attacker on the LAN execute operating-system commands with a crafted HTTP request. Zyxel fixed it on 16 June in firmware 2.90(AAHH.2)C0 and the equivalent build for each model; Zyxel’s advisory gives no CVSS and we do not invent one. The KEV listing says LAN-based, which means the exposure is management-plane reachability rather than a raw internet-facing service. What made it exploited is described in a GreyNoise report published the same day: a single actor, active since 7 May and assessed by GreyNoise as a Chinese speaker, exploited 996 GS1900 switches in 48 countries, 129 of them in the United States, using an obfuscated Python tool that pulled device configurations and root-level hashed credentials over TFTP. GreyNoise counts 564 of the victims as running factory-default credentials. The same actor stole records from an unnamed western government organisation through two WordPress flaws in July and has probed Gitea (CVE-2026-60004, the flaw in the Red Heron card), UniFi, Proxmox and PAN-OS GlobalProtect among others; GreyNoise infers overlap with Red Heron from shared command-and-control, which is an inference rather than an observation. The water relevance is editorial: the GS1900 is the low-cost managed switch an integrator specifies for a small plant, a booster station or a well house, and a stolen configuration from one of them contains VLAN layout, SNMP strings and the credential hashes an attacker needs to move from the office network to the controls.

Info Policy — Federal incident reporting

117 federal reporting rules across 27 agencies — a problem sharpest at utilities with one person on call

The McCrary Institute and the US Chamber of Commerce published analysis on 8 September, drawing on Government Accountability Office work, counting 117 separate federal cyber incident reporting regulations across 27 agencies, 48 of which apply to private industry. The argument is operational rather than ideological: duplicative notifications with different thresholds, formats and clocks pull people away from containment in the hours when containment still helps. Water and wastewater utilities feel this more acutely than most sectors, not because their obligations are heavier but because the same person is often the operator, the incident commander and the one filing to the state primacy agency, WaterISAC, the EPA and, potentially, CISA. At a system serving a few thousand people that is not a coordination problem, it is a single point of failure. The analysis arrives as CIRCIA rules are expected to be finalised this autumn.
