AI Threats AI-assisted intrusion is now an agency finding — and still has not touched a control loop

The argument has moved past whether AI-assisted intrusion is real and onto what it actually changes. The anchor is AA26-231A, in which five US agencies describe AI-generated tooling built on public industrial automation libraries, dressed up to resemble legitimate OT monitoring software, aimed at Siemens S7 controllers located through Censys and ZoomEye scanning. The agencies were blunt that this is an active threat rather than a theoretical one. Note what it is not: an AI system manipulating a control system. The practitioner reading that keeps surviving contact is that the ceiling has not moved — an experienced ICS engineer could already do the S7 work by hand — but the floor has dropped, and with it the supervision an attacker needs. A lower skill floor plus ready-made target lists is precisely how opportunistic actors find the controllers nobody knew were exposed. That is also why the countermeasure is unglamorous and unchanged: remove the exposure, kill the default credentials, and stop relying on obscurity that a scanning service already erased.

No patch applies — this is attack-surface reduction, and CISA has published the method it wants you to use Action: Do the half of this that most OT programmes skip: scan yourself from the outside. Critical Joint advisory — Active threat to Siemens S7 controllers, six sectors

Five agencies declare Siemens S7 PLCs an active target — with AI-written tooling disguised as monitoring software

On 19 August the NSA, CISA, FBI, Department of Energy and EPA jointly published AA26-231A, describing active threat activity against internet-exposed Siemens S7 Series programmable logic controllers. Actors combine publicly available industrial automation libraries with AI-assisted scripting to produce custom exploitation tools that masquerade as legitimate OT monitoring software, locating targets through internet-scanning services such as Censys and ZoomEye and favouring devices running outdated firmware or default passwords. The agencies report read and write access to PLC memory, configuration data and ladder logic, and name critical manufacturing, energy, water and wastewater, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities as the targeted sectors. Their framing was deliberate: this is an active threat, not a theoretical one, and the use of AI lowers the OT expertise an attacker needs.

Confirmed by five US agencies — the joint advisory describes observed reconnaissance and capability development against US-based Siemens S7 installations, with read and write access achieved to PLC memory, configuration and logic. Potential impacts named include disruption of critical industrial processes, safety incidents, downtime and equipment damage. No specific victim organisations are named and no actor attribution is made in the advisory No CVE — the advisory concerns exposure, default credentials and outdated firmware, not a single named vulnerability
Firmware currency matters, but exposure removal is the primary control the advisory asks for Action: Find every S7 in your estate and establish, per device, whether it answers from the internet — then check the same for every other controller family, because the technique generalises. Query Censys or Shodan against your own address space and your cellular carrier ranges rather than trusting the network diagram. Where a controller must stay reachable, front it with a brokered gateway requiring MFA, restrict source addresses, and turn on the protection level and password features S7 firmware supports but most sites leave at default. Enable and forward controller-side logging so a logic write or mode change generates an alert, since the tooling described here mimics monitoring traffic and will not look anomalous at the network layer. Finally, capture a known-good copy of each PLC program now, so you can prove whether logic has been altered.

High Red team findings — Government services and water/wastewater

CISA ran the same playbook against two utilities: one SOC caught it in minutes, the other never saw it

CISA’s advisory AA26-237A, published 25 August, reports two red-team assessments run against consenting critical-infrastructure organisations using near-identical tradecraft — a government services and facilities entity and a water and wastewater utility. The team reached full domain compromise at both. The difference was detection. The water utility caught and contained the initial activity three times, forcing the exercise to continue under an assume-breach model. At the other organisation nothing was detected at all, and the red team ended up operating on the SOC analysts’ own workstations, taking screenshots, running keyloggers and reading the team’s messages and email. The route in was mundane throughout: default web credentials for the foothold, then Active Directory enumeration, an ESC1 certificate-template misconfiguration, Machine Account Quota abuse, SCCM lateral movement and credentials recovered from administrative systems.

Authorised assessment, not an intrusion — both engagements were consented red-team exercises run by CISA and reported by CISA. The findings are directly applicable to operators, but no real adversary activity is described and neither organisation is named No CVE — every technique used is a configuration or credential weakness, not a patchable flaw
No patch applies — this is an argument about detection engineering and AD hygiene Action: Take the four techniques named and check them directly rather than waiting for a pen test. Audit certificate templates for ESC1 — requester-supplied subject with a client-authentication EKU is the single highest-value fix in this list. Set Machine Account Quota to zero unless a documented process needs it. Review who can push software through SCCM and how those accounts are protected. High Law enforcement — PRC state-sponsored scanning and relay infrastructure seized

The FBI took down the scanner and the relay network — and the victim list runs through critical infrastructure

The Justice Department and FBI announced court-authorised seizures of the domains behind two linked hacking platforms, QScan and QTRouter, built and run by a PRC state-sponsored group tracked as QTFY and operated out of a Nanjing contractor firm. The pairing is what makes this an OT story rather than an espionage footnote. QScan performed mass vulnerability scanning and automatically compromised large numbers of IoT and embedded devices; QTRouter then relayed attacker traffic through those compromised devices, so intrusion attempts arrived at victims from addresses that looked local and legitimate. Investigators say QTFY stole data from more than 300 US organisations, with named victims including the Department of Energy, NASA, the Federal Reserve and the US Senate, and that its customers included Chinese state intelligence and military organs. Activity is traced back to at least 2018.

Confirmed by DOJ and the FBI — the seizures were announced 26 Aug 2026 in a Justice Department press release describing court-authorised domain seizures against the QScan and QTRouter platforms, with a joint NSA/FBI advisory characterising QTFY targeting of military, telecommunications, government, education and critical-infrastructure networks since at least 2018. Note the limits: a domain seizure disrupts the platform’s command channel; it does not evict the actor from networks already compromised, and it does not clean up the intermediate devices that were conscripted as relays No single CVE — the platform scanned opportunistically for known flaws in internet-facing and embedded devices
Patching your edge is the control — the takedown removes infrastructure, not the exposure it fed on Action: Treat this as confirmation of a defensive assumption rather than as good news. Two things follow for an OT operator. First, the relay model means hostile traffic reaching your perimeter can originate from a residential or small-business address in your own region, so geographic and reputation-based blocking is a weak control and source-address allow-listing on remote access is a strong one. Second, the conscripted devices were routers, cameras and other embedded kit — the same class of equipment that sits unmanaged on OT-adjacent networks in most plants. Inventory the embedded devices on your networks that nobody patches: remote-access routers, cellular gateways, cameras, printers, UPS and building-automation controllers. Confirm each is on supported firmware, has no default credentials and cannot initiate outbound connections it does not need. Then review outbound egress logs from OT-adjacent segments for persistent connections to unfamiliar destinations.

High Executive Order 14420 — Bulk-power system supply chain, US

A national emergency over foreign grid equipment — and it reaches the firmware, not just the transformer

On 26 August the President signed Executive Order 14420, declaring a national emergency under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act with respect to foreign-produced bulk-power system electric equipment. The order covers high-voltage infrastructure at or above 69 kV — transformers, inverters, generators and battery energy storage — and explicitly extends to associated software, firmware and remote-access capability. It prohibits transactions initiated after 26 August involving acquisition, importation, transfer or installation of covered equipment tied to designated foreign entities where the transaction poses a sabotage or disruption risk, and directs the Department of Energy to issue implementing rules within 120 days. Those rules may condition the continued operation of equipment already energised, and potentially require removal. For utilities this becomes a supply-chain compliance layer on top of NERC CIP-013, and it lands on a provenance record most operators do not have.

Policy action, not an incident — the order is signed and in force as of 26 Aug 2026, but its operative prohibitions depend on DOE rulemaking due within 120 days, and the list of covered foreign entities and covered equipment will be defined through that process. Practical obligations on individual utilities are therefore not yet fixed No CVE — this is supply-chain and procurement policy
No patch applies — the deliverable is equipment provenance and procurement control Action: Start the inventory now rather than when the rule lands, because the lead time is in the data, not the decision.
