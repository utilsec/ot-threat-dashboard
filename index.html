CISA added a third MikroTik RouterOS flaw, CVE-2026-67279, to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 25 September, with a 28 September federal deadline, joining CVE-2026-86060 and CVE-2026-67277 from 10 September. CERT Polska has tracked in-the-wild exploitation since roughly 2 September of a two-flaw SSH chain it calls “MikroTrick,” and its 22 September write-up shows CVE-2026-67279 chained with CVE-2026-86060 handing an unauthenticated attacker full administrative control. Shadowserver separately counted more than 122,500 MikroTik devices with SSH reachable from the internet in early September — an exposure figure, not a confirmed-unpatched count. These routers commonly front remote-access and cellular-telemetry links into field sites and small, unattended facilities that rarely get timely firmware updates.

CISA KEV now lists three MikroTik flaws: CVE-2026-86060 (argument injection), CVE-2026-67277 (missing authentication for a critical function in the bandwidth-test service) and CVE-2026-67279 (SSH workflow bypass that lets an unauthenticated client open a session channel and send an exec request; CVSS 6.9, version not stated). Confirmed vs claimed: exploitation is confirmed by CERT Polska and CISA KEV; the 122,500 figure is internet-reachable SSH exposure, not compromise. FBI and CISA: your ICS integrator’s access to your plant is your risk too, not just theirs

The FBI and CISA published a joint fact sheet, “Considerations for Critical Infrastructure Operators Working With Third-Party ICS Integrators,” prompted by a 2025 compromise of a US industrial automation solutions company that provided system integration, engineering consulting and SCADA programming. Between March and April 2025, the agencies say, foreign intruders searched the company’s systems for terms including “customers” and “SCADA” and staged nine archives of roughly 800 files of customer SCADA information, ICS device details and schematics for exfiltration; the downstream customers named are power utilities and transportation entities. The fact sheet recommends least-privilege access for integrators, on-demand and auditable remote access, a full inventory of supplied hardware and software, security requirements in contracts, and the ability to operate manually if the integrator is compromised.

Confirmed guidance referencing a 2025 case — the fact sheet is dated 23 Sep 2026 and was released publicly on 24 Sep. Confirmed vs claimed: the guidance and the underlying 2025 case are confirmed by the FBI and CISA; the affected integrator, its customers and the actors’ nationality are not named, and no downstream operator breach is disclosed No CVE — guidance and a supply-chain case study, not a product vulnerability
No patch applies — the work is contractual, access-control and inventory discipline with every third-party integrator Action: Inventory which integrators, VARs and system houses currently hold remote access or credentials into your OT environment, and confirm each connection is on-demand and operator-authorized rather than standing and integrator-initiated. Ask what data each integrator holds about your systems and where it is stored. Update contracts to require least-privilege access, prompt breach notification, and a documented offline-backup and manual-operation fallback that doesn’t depend on the integrator being reachable.

NIST’s core OT security guide gets a major rewrite — and the comment window is open until 30 November

NIST released the initial public draft of SP 800-82 Revision 4, “Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security,” on 21 September. The draft expands coverage beyond classic ICS to include building automation, water and wastewater, food and agriculture, freight rail, maritime vessels and industrial IoT and cloud convergence; restructures the document around NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, with the risk section refocused on the Govern function; and expands guidance on asset management, network monitoring and zero-trust architecture for OT. Public comments are open through 30 November 2026. It landed the same week as the FBI and CISA fact sheet on third-party ICS integrator risk (above).

Confirmed publication, draft stage — NIST released the SP 800-82r4 initial public draft on 21 Sep 2026 with comments due 30 Nov 2026. Confirmed vs claimed: the publication, scope and comment deadline are NIST-confirmed; the document is a draft, so specific control language may change before publication No CVE — guidance document, not a product vulnerability
No patch applies — the work is reviewing the draft and submitting comments where your sector’s specifics aren’t well captured Action: If you operate in one of the newly explicit sectors — building automation, water, food and agriculture, freight rail or maritime — read the draft’s scope section and flag gaps specific to your environment during the comment period. Everyone else: use the CSF 2.0 restructuring and the zero-trust guidance as a preview of how NIST expects OT programs to map their controls going forward.
Suggested SLA: Initial read of draft scope relevant to your sector — 14 days; submit comments if gaps identified — by 30 Nov 2026; begin CSF 2.0 control-mapping exercise — 60 days Owner:
