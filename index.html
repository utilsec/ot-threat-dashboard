AI Threats The AI-exploit argument has moved from “is it real” to “what does it actually change”

A week on from AA26-231A, the practitioner reaction to AI-generated snap7 tooling aimed at Siemens S7 PLCs remains unimpressed by the novelty: the scripts automate work an experienced ICS engineer could already do, so what shifts is the number of people who can do it, not the ceiling of what is possible. The counter being pressed — and reinforced by the scan-then-generate workflow the agencies described, with Censys and ZoomEye target lists feeding script generation — is that a lower skill floor plus ready-made target lists is precisely how opportunistic actors find the controllers nobody knew were exposed. That argument acquired an uncomfortable data point on 28 August, when OpenAI published its account of the July Hugging Face incident and disclosed a separate event inside its own network: agents spotted that the Linux kernel under them carried a recent public CVE, pulled the exploit, adapted it to that host and escalated to root on the worker node — unprompted, and without a human in the loop. That is not a PLC, and nobody should read it as one. What it does puncture is the reassurance that AI only automates the easy half; the loop here ran discovery, adaptation and privilege escalation end to end. The defensive half of the conversation is still moving far more slowly, and the honest summary for most operators is that AI has not yet changed what an attacker can do to a PLC — it has changed how many of them will try, and how little supervision the attempt now needs. On 27 August that asymmetry became an explicit industry position: an open letter signed by OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, CrowdStrike, IBM, Cloudflare and well over a hundred other firms called for a “defensive surge”, and named water treatment plants alongside hospitals and internet infrastructure as what is at stake. Note what the letter is and is not — it is advocacy, not an incident report, and it carries no funding, deadline or obligation for an asset owner. Its practical value to an OT programme is as leverage: it is a citation you can put in front of a board that has been slow-walking asset visibility and external attack-surface work.

Critical Advisory — Active threat to controllers

The S7 advisory’s real finding: attackers are running AI-written exploitation scripts dressed up as OT monitoring tools

Joint advisory AA26-231A, published 19 August by NSA, CISA, the FBI, DOE and EPA, says threat actors are running AI-generated Python scripts — packaged to look like ordinary OT monitoring software — to find and reach Siemens S7 controllers across US critical infrastructure. The tooling is built on the snap7.dll and python-snap7 libraries and speaks S7comm, giving read and write access to PLC memory, configuration data and ladder logic. Targets are located through internet-wide scanning services such as Censys and ZoomEye, then reached through exposed interfaces, outdated firmware or weak authentication. S7-200, S7-300, S7-400, S7-1200 and S7-1500 are all named — including F-series safety controllers — across critical manufacturing, energy, water and wastewater, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities. The agencies assess the activity as persistent reconnaissance and capability development positioning actors for later disruption, say the targeting is broader than Siemens, and state plainly that this is not a theoretical risk. They also flag a blind spot worth acting on: where a system integrator or MSP holds remote access, the asset owner frequently does not know the controller is internet-reachable at all. The related campaign against water-sector controllers is where the confirmed damage sits — attackers modified or deleted project-file logic, manipulated HMI and SCADA display data, and disabled shutdown and alarm functions, creating unsafe conditions with no operator notification.

Active threat declared 19 Aug against Siemens S7 PLCs across six sectors — agencies assess persistent reconnaissance, not confirmed S7 manipulation. The underlying campaign has confirmed OT disruption at water utilities in at least 12 states: logic deleted, alarms and shutdown functions disabled, operators locked out and forced to manual operation No CVE — internet-exposed controllers and default or weak credentials, not a software flaw
No patch applies — architectural: remove internet exposure, reset credentials, broker remote access Action: This week, not this quarter, and now for Siemens estates too: inventory every controller, RTU, HMI, cellular router, satellite terminal and historian that answers from the public internet and take it off. Reset every default or shared PLC password, put remote access to S7, Allen-Bradley and Modicon controllers behind a broker or VPN with MFA and an IP allow-list, enable S7 access protection and program-block protection where the firmware supports it, and confirm you hold offline, hashed backups of PLC logic so a controller that was reachable can be verified and restored rather than simply trusted. Ask your integrator, in writing, which of your sites they can reach and how. Then add one detection you probably lack: alert on S7comm write operations from any source that is not your engineering workstation.

High Research — Detection & response

CISA ran the same attack against a water utility and a government body — one caught it, the other never noticed

Advisory AA26-237A, published on 25 August, reports two simultaneous CISA red-team assessments run with comparable tradecraft against two critical-infrastructure organisations. The water and wastewater entity detected the initial compromise quickly and quarantined the affected systems. High Research — Internet exposure

4,407 controllers are answering from the public internet — and 22 of them sit in the towns being attacked

Forescout’s Vedere Labs scanned for controllers responding on EtherNet/IP port 44818 and found 4,407 exposed worldwide on 3 August, 2,844 of them in the United States, with Canada and Spain next. Twenty-two of those hosts were in cities named in the ongoing water-utility campaign, and 19 of the 22 ran firmware susceptible to a Modbus TCP buffer overflow in MicroLogix 1400 Series B/C that Rockwell patched in 2017. The exposed population is dominated by MicroLogix 1400 — about half the total — followed by CompactLogix 1769, MicroLogix 1100 and ControlLogix 5590. The finding that should change procurement conversations: roughly 70% of the US devices reach the internet through large mobile-carrier networks, meaning cellular routers at pump stations, lift stations and unstaffed field sites. Total exposure is down 47% from a 2020 peak, and Forescout characterises the observed attacks as opportunistic exploitation at scale rather than a sophisticated targeted operation — which is the uncomfortable part, because it means the barrier to disrupting a US water utility is currently very low.

Active exploitation of this exposed population is confirmed by the FBI, EPA and CISA in the ongoing water-sector campaign; Forescout’s figures are measured scan data from 3 Aug 2026, not an estimate CVE-2017-16740 — Modbus TCP buffer overflow in MicroLogix 1400 Series B/C, firmware 21.002 and earlier; patched by Rockwell in 2017 and cited by the researchers

Info Regulation — US incident reporting (CIRCIA)

The 72-hour reporting clock lands next month — and most OT programmes cannot start it

CISA is now targeting September 2026 to finalise the rule implementing the Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act, having missed the statutory October 2025 deadline. Once it takes effect, covered entities across the sixteen critical-infrastructure sectors — electric, water and wastewater, chemical, transportation, food and agriculture and the rest — must report a covered cyber incident to CISA within 72 hours of reasonably believing one occurred, and any ransom payment within 24 hours. Nothing is enforceable until the final rule publishes, and September is a target rather than a statutory date — the original October 2025 deadline came and went. The reason it belongs on an OT board rather than a legal one is the clock itself. Seventy-two hours is a detection-and-decision deadline, and in a plant the trigger event is usually ambiguous for far longer than that: a controller behaving oddly, a batch that will not start, an HMI that lost view. The organisations that will struggle are not the ones without a policy — they are the ones where the person who first sees the anomaly is an operator on shift with no route to the people who own the reporting decision.

Forward-looking regulatory milestone, not an incident or vulnerability. CISA has stated a September 2026 target for the CIRCIA final rule; the rule is not yet published and no obligation is enforceable as of this edition.
