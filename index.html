Critical Incident — Engineering data theft

Clop’s PLM extortion campaign now names Shell, GE and Philips — the stolen material is plant drawings and blueprints

Clop has listed 43 new victims tied to its exploitation of internet-exposed PTC Windchill and FlexPLM, the product lifecycle management platforms used across aerospace, defence, automotive, heavy machinery, medtech and retail. On 13 August Shell confirmed it is investigating a “potential incident” after the gang claimed 89 GB of data; on 17 August GE said it is assessing the claim and Philips confirmed a contained compromise of one enterprise server with no customer impact. The gang advertises backups, project plans, facility photographs, drawings, diagrams and blueprints. The entry point is CVE-2026-12569, an input-validation flaw in Windchill and FlexPLM exploited as a zero-day from early June for unauthenticated remote code execution and JSP web-shell deployment. On 18 August ReliaQuest published analysis of the implant itself, and it is not commodity tooling: a Java/JSP web shell written against Windchill’s own internal APIs, database schema, keystore and file-vault layout, with functions for credential access and decryption, repository enumeration, bulk file retrieval and arbitrary Java execution. It was engineered for PLM estates specifically. The detection problem is the part worth carrying into your own environment — the web shell runs as the application’s own service identity, so database telemetry can misattribute the activity as normal application behaviour, and query or file-access logs alone will not separate the theft from ordinary use. PTC began patching on 17 June; CISA added the flaw to KEV on 25 June with a three-day federal remediation deadline. No OT or control-system impact has been reported at any victim — this is confidentiality loss, not disruption. The OT relevance is what was taken: PLM holds the as-designed record of plants, machines and safety systems, and it is rarely inventoried as an OT-adjacent asset or covered by an OT threat model.

Critical Incident — Confirmed OT sabotage

An attacker crossed from a wind farm to a district heating plant through a shared private APN — and stopped a steam turbine

CERT Polska’s follow-up on the December 2025 Polish energy-sector intrusion, disclosed at DEF CON on 9 August and published on 11 August, describes what it calls the first observed case of an attacker reaching an OT network through a private cellular APN. The intruder compromised a FortiGate firewall at a wind farm, used a Teltonika cellular router on that network to enter a private APN operated by the distribution system operator, and from there reached a combined heat and power plant supplying roughly 50,000 residents — an entirely unrelated organisation. A steam turbine and the process-water treatment system were shut down, interrupting cogeneration. Operator response kept it to a short outage with no loss of heat supply. CERT Polska attributes the activity to a Russia-linked actor and notes the root cause was configuration, not exploitation: devices on the shared APN could reach each other freely, a pattern it found was common across Poland.

CISA put the mass-exploited vCenter flaw in KEV today — and virtualisation is where OT lives now

High Incident — Ports & logistics

All three North Carolina ports ran their gates by hand after a cyberattack — and the Coast Guard is still watching

The North Carolina State Ports Authority discovered an intrusion on 4 August and began recovery the following morning, activating its cybersecurity contingency plan across the Port of Wilmington, the Port of Morehead City and the Charlotte Inland Port. Gate openings were delayed and truck processing dropped to manual while the authority contained the intrusion; by 7 August a normal operating schedule had resumed with forensics continuing. The US Coast Guard confirmed it is monitoring the aftermath and coordinating with the North Carolina Department of Transportation, the state IT department and other federal partners. The authority has not disclosed the nature of the attack, which systems were hit, or whether vessel operations, cargo-handling equipment or rail interchange were touched, and no actor has been named — there is no reporting tying this to the Iranian-affiliated PLC campaign elsewhere on this page. Treat the OT question as open rather than answered: what is confirmed is enterprise and gate-management disruption, not control-system manipulation. Wilmington and Morehead City together move roughly 4.4 million short tons of bulk and breakbulk a year, much of it agricultural export, so the downstream exposure runs into food and agriculture supply chains as well as retail and raw materials. The transferable lesson is the one the authority got right: the fallback to manual gate processing existed, was documented and was exercised, which is why a multi-site IT compromise became a slow day instead of a closed port.

High Threat intel — AI-assisted intrusion

Commercial AI models did the OT reconnaissance for an attacker who had never touched a control system

Dragos, working with Gambit Security, published its analysis of an intrusion at a municipal water and drainage utility serving the Monterrey metropolitan area in Mexico, in which an IT compromise escalated into an attempted OT breach in January 2026. What makes the case worth reading is the tooling rather than the outcome. The adversary used commercial large language models as an operational engine: Anthropic’s Claude wrote and iterated a roughly 17,000-line Python framework and OpenAI models handled data processing and Spanish-language output. Dragos reports the model autonomously identified an OT-adjacent industrial gateway from network data, generated candidate credential lists and launched an automated password-spray attempt against it — work the operator apparently could not have scoped or scripted unaided. The attempt failed. Dragos found no evidence the OT environment was breached and no novel ICS malware or specialised industrial tradecraft was involved; the models simply operationalised publicly documented techniques quickly. That is the point defenders should take from it, and it lines up directly with this edition’s lead: the joint S7 advisory describes the same pattern from the other side, AI-written Python built on snap7 and dressed as OT monitoring software. The barrier that used to protect industrial estates — that an attacker had to understand your process and your protocols before they could do anything useful — is thinning, and the compensating controls are the unglamorous ones: no OT-adjacent gateway reachable from IT without a broker, no shared or default credentials, and lockout and alerting on authentication failures against engineering assets.

Critical Advisory — SCADA/HMI remote code execution, scanned in the wild

A CVSS 9.5 flaw in the FUXA SCADA/HMI platform lets an unauthenticated attacker write files to the server — scanning began within a day

CVE-2026-25895 is a missing-authentication-plus-path-traversal flaw in FUXA, the open-source web-based SCADA/HMI package used for small automation projects, machine HMIs, remote monitoring and lab or pilot lines. In versions up to and including 1.2.9, a remote attacker with no credentials can write arbitrary files to the server file system and reach remote code execution — and because FUXA typically runs as the process front end, that is code execution on the box operators watch the plant through. VulnCheck reported broad internet scanning for the flaw beginning 18 August, from a single source address attempting to overwrite main.js with junk data; no working RCE payloads have been observed dropped yet, so treat this as reconnaissance ahead of exploitation rather than a confirmed compromise. Roughly 60 FUXA instances answer directly on the public internet. The uncomfortable part of the FUXA story is where these installs live: open-source HMI software rarely appears on an OT asset register, and the people who stood it up are often not the people who patch.

CISA, the FBI, DC3, NSA, the US Secret Service and the Republic of Korea National Police Agency published joint advisory AA26-222A on 10 August, covering Gunra ransomware-as-a-service. The initial-access route is the part OT owners should read twice: Gunra affiliates are exploiting CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472, two authentication-bypass flaws in Fortinet FortiOS and FortiProxy that grant super-admin privileges on the firewall. Both were patched in early 2025 and both are already in CISA’s KEV catalogue. Gunra emerged in April 2025, is written in C/C++ and is reportedly derived from leaked Conti source; it moved to a structured affiliate programme in early 2026 with a management panel, configurable builders and cross-platform payloads. The agencies count at least 51 victims across the Americas, Europe, the Middle East, Africa and Asia-Pacific, including hospitals, government bodies, financial institutions and critical manufacturing, with demands frequently above $10 million. Post-access tradecraft reported in the advisory includes abusing an SSL-VPN administrative account with default credentials and no lockout policy, OpenSSH tunnelling out to attacker infrastructure, and modifying authentication-processing files on a corporate VDI portal to permanently bypass MFA. Reconnaissance and lateral movement are deliberately scheduled between 22:00 and 06:00 local time. One victim lost tens of terabytes, staged through OneDrive and SharePoint before transfer to Mega.

Confirm the FortiOS and FortiProxy version on every perimeter, DMZ and site-to-site appliance — including the ones an integrator installed and still administers — and if any sat unpatched on these CVEs, treat the device as compromised, not merely vulnerable: export and review the configuration for unfamiliar admin accounts, VPN profiles, SSH keys and outbound tunnels, then rotate every credential the device holds. The 22:00–06:00 pattern also tells you where to look: if nobody reviews OT-boundary firewall logs outside working hours, that is the window this actor is deliberately using.

High Advisory — OT monitoring tooling

Six flaws in Malcolm, CISA’s own OT traffic-analysis suite — the sensor that watches your whole plant network

ICSA-26-230-01, published 18 August, covers six vulnerabilities in Malcolm, the free open-source network-traffic-analysis suite CISA develops and recommends for OT visibility. The headline issue is CVE-2026-55676 at CVSS 8.8: an unrestricted file upload that accepts a PHP file and leads to arbitrary code execution. Behind it sit CVE-2026-63177 (7.1), a path traversal during archive extraction; CVE-2026-63133 and CVE-2026-19671 (6.5 each); CVE-2026-63134 (5.4), an RBAC bypass using unnormalised URI paths; and CVE-2026-19670 (5.4), an authorisation bypass via percent-encoding — plus resource-exhaustion and compression-bomb conditions that can deny service. A monitoring sensor with full network visibility is a high-value foothold, and it is rarely in anyone’s patch schedule.

High Advisory — Remote access

Citrix is telling admins to patch NetScaler now — an unauthenticated bypass on the appliance that fronts most OT remote access

Citrix published a bulletin on 19 August covering two flaws in NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway, and followed it with an unusually direct instruction to upgrade immediately. The serious one is CVE-2026-19490, an authentication bypass a remote attacker can reach without credentials where the appliance runs as an AAA virtual server or as a Gateway — SSL VPN, ICA Proxy, CVPN or RDP Proxy — with exposure depending on firmware version and whether a SAML action is configured. The second, CVE-2026-19489, is a memory-overflow denial-of-service reachable when SIP ALG is enabled on a large-scale NAT group. Citrix has published configuration strings that let you check both preconditions from your own running config rather than guessing from version numbers: look for add authentication samlAction together with add authentication vserver or add vpn vserver for the first, and add lsn group.*sipalg for the second. Fixed builds are 14.1-73.32, 13.1-63.21, 14.1-73.32 FIPS and 13.1-37.277 FIPS/NDcPP. SecurAccess ZTNA Hybrid deployments running customer-managed NetScaler instances are in scope too. Neither flaw is reported as exploited yet, and neither is in KEV as of this edition — but the precedent is close and recent: Citrix issued the same kind of warning for CVE-2026-3055 on 23 March, attackers were abusing it within days, and CISA added it to KEV on 30 March with a three-day federal deadline. NetScaler has accumulated 22 KEV entries in five years, six of them used in ransomware. ShadowServer currently sees more than 22,000 NetScaler ADC and roughly 1,800 Gateway instances answering from the internet. For OT owners this is not an ICS advisory and it will not appear in an ICS feed, but it is the box that terminates integrator VPNs, vendor support sessions and engineering-workstation access into the plant — the same class of edge device that carried the intrusions in the Gunra and Polish district-heating cases elsewhere on this page.

Info Policy — Transit & transportation

NIST’s transit cybersecurity profile is final — and it is written for agencies whose business and control systems already share a network

NIST’s National Cybersecurity Center of Excellence published the final NIST IR 8576, the Transit Cybersecurity Framework Community Profile, on 5 August. Built on CSF 2.0 and developed with transit operators and the federal agencies that oversee them, it translates transit mission needs — keeping service running safely — into a prioritised baseline of cybersecurity outcomes that a small agency and a large one can each scale to fit. Its framing is the useful part for OT people outside transit: it starts from the premise that business and operational technology now share digital, network-based communication, and it asks agencies to manage cyber risk alongside safety and operations rather than as a separate IT programme. It is voluntary and non-regulatory, but it is the kind of document that tends to become the reference point when a regulator or an insurer later asks what “reasonable” looked like.

Medium Policy — Information sharing & funding

The information-sharing law that lets vendors warn you about OT threats expires at the end of September

The Operational Technology Cybersecurity Coalition — an industry group whose members include Forescout, Claroty, Dragos, Honeywell and Schneider Electric — issued a statement calling the water-sector attacks a wake-up call and pressing Congress and CISA on three items. The one with a date attached is the Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015, whose liability protections are set to lapse at the end of September 2026. Those protections are the legal basis on which vendors, ISACs and operators pass indicators to CISA and on which CISA and the FBI push warnings back out to a wider set of potential victims — the mechanism that carried the PLC-campaign indicators to utilities that had not yet been hit. The coalition also asked CISA to issue a Binding Operational Directive covering operational technology at federal facilities (heating and cooling, power, physical access control), and asked Congress to reauthorise and fund the State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program, the main funding route for the small municipal utilities in this campaign’s path. Coalition chair Alison King notes roughly 148,000 public water systems in the US, with only a small fraction participating directly in threat-intelligence sharing.
