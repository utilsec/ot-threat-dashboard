High Incident — confirmed OT compromise of internet-exposed PLCs across more than 100 US water systems

More than a hundred water systems, one campaign, and the only thing the attackers needed was a PLC facing the internet

CISA has confirmed that over 100 US water and wastewater systems were targeted during July, in a campaign federal agencies attribute to Iran-affiliated actors and characterise as opportunistic rather than selective. The technique was not sophisticated: find a programmable logic controller reachable from the public internet, change its password and its network settings, and lock the operator out of their own equipment. Some utilities reported flooding and drops in water pressure afterwards. Rockwell Automation and Allen-Bradley MicroLogix 1100 and 1400 controllers were named first; the joint advisory has since been widened to Schneider Electric and Siemens devices, with the actors also using vendors’ own engineering software — Studio 5000, EcoStruxure Control Expert, TIA Portal — to pull project files out of victim environments. This is the clearest confirmed OT-disruptive campaign in the current window, and none of it required a vulnerability.

Confirmed — CISA confirmed in late Aug 2026 that more than 100 US water systems were targeted during July 2026, and has described the activity as opportunistic in nature rather than aimed at particular utilities. The FBI and EPA warned from 30 Jul 2026 that malicious cyber actors were attacking OT devices at water and wastewater utilities, with water sector organisations in at least seven states reporting incidents to the FBI from 27 Jul 2026; more than 30 community water systems in Minnesota were affected in the initial wave. Reported operator-visible effects include changed PLC passwords and altered network settings including IP addresses, preventing remote access and management by utility staff, and in some cases flooding and reductions in water pressure. The joint advisory AA26-097A, originally scoped to Rockwell Automation and Allen-Bradley MicroLogix 1100 and 1400 controllers, was updated on 22 Jul 2026 by CISA, the FBI, the NSA, the EPA and other partners to add Schneider Electric and Siemens devices, and to document exfiltration of PLC project files using the vendors’ own legitimate engineering software. No patch applies — the fix is removing controllers from the public internet and replacing default credentials Action: The uncomfortable part of this campaign is that it is entirely within reach of any small utility to prevent, and that is also why it keeps working. Start by scanning your own public IP ranges for anything answering on 44818, 502, 20000, 102 or a web interface on a controller — and then check what your cellular carrier or ISP has assigned you, because a surprising number of exposed PLCs sit behind a modem nobody documented. Where a controller must be reachable for remote operations, put it behind a brokered remote-access path with multi-factor authentication rather than a port forward. Second, treat the project-file exfiltration as the more durable risk: a stolen Studio 5000, Control Expert or TIA Portal project hands an attacker your logic, your tag names, your setpoints and your alarm thresholds, and no password change takes that back — so restrict engineering workstations, keep project repositories off general file shares, and know which projects have been on machines that were exposed. Finally, join and use WaterISAC — the state-by-state pattern in July was only visible because utilities reported.

Info Guidance — CISA, “Using Cyber Decoys to Strengthen Detection and Response”

CISA wants critical-infrastructure operators to plant tripwires — and to build them from tools they already own

CISA published guidance on deploying cyber decoys inside critical-infrastructure networks, aimed explicitly at teams that cannot detect an adversary using valid credentials and native administrative tooling. It describes three constructs: honeytokens, which are data elements or logical objects with no legitimate business purpose — fake records, credentials or files — whose use strongly implies unauthorised activity; breadcrumbs, planted references in scripts, documentation, file shares or configuration files that lead an intruder toward a monitored decoy; and honeypots, deliberately vulnerable systems. The instruction that makes this usable is the procurement one: repurpose existing EDR, identity and data-loss-prevention tooling to deploy and monitor decoys rather than buying a deception platform. The stated goal is reduced mean time to detection through high-fidelity alerts.

Guidance, not a threat — CISA released “Using Cyber Decoys to Strengthen Detection and Response” on 16 Sep 2026, with sector coverage following on 17 Sep 2026. The guidance is framed as making cyber deception accessible to smaller security teams as well as well-resourced ones, and defines honeytokens (data elements or logical objects with no legitimate business use, such as fake records, credentials or files, planted to detect unauthorised access or exfiltration, where any interaction strongly suggests malicious or otherwise unauthorised activity), breadcrumbs (planted clues in scripts, documentation, file shares or configuration files that guide an intruder toward a monitored decoy resource) and honeypots (systems with deliberate vulnerabilities operating at system level). CISA states the core problem being addressed is that many organisations cannot detect adversaries using legitimate credentials, built-in administrative utilities and living-off-the-land techniques, and explicitly instructs organisations to repurpose tools they already own — EDR platforms, identity and access management systems and data loss prevention tooling — to deploy and monitor decoys without new purchases. The stated benefit is reduced mean time to detection through high-fidelity alerting. Confirmed vs claimed: the guidance and its contents are confirmed from the CISA publication; the OT-specific application below is editorial No CVE — detection guidance
No patch applies — detection engineering Action: Deception is unusually well suited to OT, and under-used there, for one reason: baseline behaviour on a control network is narrow and well understood, so anything touching a decoy is almost certainly hostile. Start with the cheapest and most durable item — a decoy credential. Create a domain account named to look like an engineering or vendor service account, give it no rights and no logon, and alert on any authentication attempt. Nothing legitimate will ever touch it. Then add a decoy file share on a server in the plant DMZ named to suggest PLC projects or drawings, containing plausible but worthless files, with read alerting enabled. Third, plant breadcrumbs pointing at both — a reference in an operations runbook, a stale entry in a scripts folder, a line in a configuration file on a jump host. Fourth, if you run passive OT monitoring already, a decoy host answering on a common industrial protocol on an unused address in the control VLAN costs nothing and catches scanning. The discipline that makes this work is documentation: write down every decoy, where it lives, who owns the alert and what the response is, and review it when people leave — an undocumented honeypot becomes an unpatched server in someone else’s inventory within two years. Avoid deliberately vulnerable honeypots inside a control network; the honeytoken and decoy-share pattern gives most of the detection value with none of the added attack surface, and it will survive an audit conversation.
Suggested SLA: Deploy a decoy credential with alerting — 14 days; deploy a decoy file share in the plant DMZ with read alerting — 30 days; plant breadcrumbs and document every decoy with a named alert owner and response — 45 days; review decoy inventory as part of the standard access review cycle — ongoing Owner:

Info Regulation — FERC order approving NERC Reliability Standard CIP-014-4; effective 1 October 2028

The substation risk assessment just got a method, a 36-month clock and a 1,500-foot neighbour test

FERC issued an order on 10 September approving NERC Reliability Standard CIP-014-4, with the Federal Register notice published on 15 September and an effective date of 1 October 2028. The revisions target the part of CIP-014 that NERC concluded lacked rigour and consistency: the R1 risk assessment. Transmission owners must now identify applicable facilities existing or planned to be in service within a 36-month window, aligning the cycle with transmission planning; use a documented risk assessment methodology performing both steady-state and dynamic simulations on at least one system peak load case and one off-peak case; and identify proximate bulk electric system stations and substations within 1,500 feet, fence line to fence line, to evaluate the effect of an attack on adjacent equipment. It is a physical security standard, not a cyber one — but the assessment work, the ownership questions and the budget cycle all land on the same teams.

Regulatory action, not a threat — confirmed: FERC issued its order approving Reliability Standard CIP-014-4 on 10 Sep 2026, with the Federal Register notice published 15 Sep 2026 and an effective date of 1 Oct 2028. NERC filed the petition for approval on 16 Jul 2026 under Project 2023-06, itself a response to FERC’s December 2022 directive following the Moore County, North Carolina and Pacific Northwest substation attacks. The standard provides a consistent approach for identifying and assessing transmission stations, transmission substations and primary control centres critical to bulk-power system operation. Key changes: the risk assessment cycle aligns to a 36-month timeframe covering facilities existing or planned to be in service; a documented risk assessment methodology is mandated, specifying both steady-state and dynamic simulations using at least one system peak load case and one system off-peak load case; and a new provision requires identification of proximate existing bulk electric system transmission stations and substations within 1,500 feet (457 metres) measured fence line to fence line, to evaluate the impact of a physical attack on adjacent equipment. Note: this is a physical security standard sitting alongside CIP-002 through CIP-015 rather than inside them, and nothing here changes cyber compliance obligations No CVE — reliability standard approval, not a vulnerability
No patch applies — compliance planning against a 1 Oct 2028 effective date Action: Two years sounds generous until you work out that the initial risk assessment must be complete by the effective date and now requires dynamic simulation work your planning group may not currently perform for physical security scoping. Start by asking the planning engineers, not the security team, whether the existing CIP-014 study would survive the new methodology test — documented thresholds with technical justification, steady-state and dynamic runs on both peak and off-peak cases, stated assumptions on loss of communications and loss of protection systems. Where it would not, the gap is study methodology and modelling capacity, which is a budget and resourcing conversation for the coming planning cycle rather than a 2028 one. Second, run the 1,500-foot proximity screen early: it captures neighbouring bulk electric system stations you do not own, which means data requests and coordination with other entities, and those take longer than the analysis. Third, for jointly owned facilities, get responsibility allocation into the joint-ownership agreements now rather than discovering in 2028 that both owners assumed the other was doing the assessment. Finally, treat the alignment with the transmission planning cycle as the practical gift in this standard — if your physical security assessment and your transmission planning assessment run on the same 36-month clock and the same models, one team can feed the other instead of maintaining two divergent views of the system.

Medium Regulation — EU Cyber Resilience Act reporting obligations in force since 11 September 2026; ENISA Single Reporting Platform live

Your OT vendor now has 24 hours to tell Brussels a vulnerability in their product is being exploited — and the portal to do it opened the day the clock started

The Cyber Resilience Act’s reporting obligations became applicable on 11 September, and ENISA brought the initial operating capability of its Single Reporting Platform online the same day. Manufacturers placing products with digital elements on the EU market — which covers a great deal of the controller, gateway, HMI, drive and industrial software estate — must now notify actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents affecting product security through one portal, which fans the notification out to the coordinating CSIRT, other affected member states and ENISA. The CRA’s substantive security requirements do not bite until 11 December 2027, and the obligation for open-source stewards starts then too. This is a disclosure-timing change, not a product-security change — but disclosure timing is what determines when you find out.

Policy — Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, the Cyber Resilience Act, is the EU’s horizontal framework imposing mandatory cybersecurity requirements on products with digital elements across their lifecycle. Its Article 14 reporting obligations apply to manufacturers from 11 Sep 2026; the main cybersecurity requirements apply from 11 Dec 2027, and Article 24(3) extends reporting duties to open-source software stewards from that later date. ENISA deployed the initial operating capability of the Single Reporting Platform on 11 Sep 2026: manufacturers and stewards report once, and the designated coordinator CSIRT disseminates the notification to CSIRTs in other member states where the product is available, with ENISA receiving it simultaneously. The reporting cadence widely described by counsel advising on the regulation is an early warning within 24 hours of awareness, a fuller notification within 72 hours, and a final report once a corrective measure is available. ENISA has published an FAQ, user manuals, tutorial videos, a glossary and a factsheet in multiple EU languages, and runs a help desk. Confirmed vs claimed: the applicability date, the platform launch and the dissemination model are confirmed by ENISA and reported by Industrial Cyber; the specific penalty ceilings and the 24/72-hour cadence come from law-firm analysis of the regulation rather than from an ENISA statement, and the authoritative text is the regulation itself No CVE — this is a regulatory and disclosure item, not a vulnerability
No patch applies — the work is contract language, PSIRT-feed coverage and knowing which of your vendors are in scope Action: Asset owners outside the EU should not file this under “European compliance”. The practical effect is that a large share of the OT vendors you already buy from now face a 24-hour clock on actively exploited vulnerabilities, and the notifications land with EU CSIRTs first. Three things follow. First, work out which of your vendors are in scope — if they sell the same controller, gateway, RTU, drive or SCADA package into the EU, they are, regardless of where your plant sits — and ask them plainly how a CRA notification will reach their customers outside the EU and on what timeline relative to the regulator. Several will not have an answer yet; that is the useful part of asking now. Second, check your PSIRT subscriptions actually cover the European entity that manufactures your equipment rather than a regional sales arm, and make sure someone owns reading them. Third, put this into the next round of procurement and support-contract language: notification to you within a defined window of the vendor becoming aware, notification of actively exploited vulnerabilities specifically, and the vendor’s coordinated disclosure policy as an attachment rather than a URL. If you operate in the EU and manufacture or integrate anything you place on the market — skid builders and OEMs frequently do without realising it — confirm whether you are a manufacturer under the CRA and register with the Single Reporting Platform before you need it, not during an incident.
