Critical Incident — Confirmed OT disruption, energy; first documented private-APN pivot

Attackers walked from a wind farm’s firewall to another plant’s PLCs across a shared cellular APN — and stopped a turbine

CERT Polska’s follow-up report on attacks against Poland’s energy sector documents an intrusion path that maps almost exactly onto the exposure CISA and the NCSC are now warning about, and it is the reason this older item belongs on the page. Attackers compromised a FortiGate firewall at a wind farm, moved to a Teltonika cellular router, and then crossed a private cellular APN that had been provisioned without client isolation — meaning every SIM on that APN could reach every other. On the far side sat a WAGO PFC200 at a separate combined heat and power plant, still carrying default administrator credentials, which the attackers used to build an SSH bridge into the OT network. From there they forced three Siemens PLCs into STOP and set password protection on them, shutting down a steam turbine and the process-water treatment serving a town of roughly 50,000 people. CERT Polska assesses this as the first documented real-world OT intrusion via lateral movement across a private APN, and notes the shared-APN-without-isolation configuration is common in Poland and likely well beyond it.

Confirmed by CERT Polska — the national CERT’s energy-sector incident follow-up report, published 10 Aug 2026, describes the full chain and the operational outcome, including the turbine and water-treatment shutdown at a second facility. The incident itself occurred in 2025 and is disclosed here for the first time in this detail. Not attributed: CERT Polska publishes the intrusion path and tooling without naming an actor No single CVE drives this — the chain ran on an internet-reachable edge firewall, an unisolated carrier APN and a default controller password
Patching alone would not have stopped it — the decisive weaknesses were architectural: APN client isolation and a default credential Action: Treat every private APN as an untrusted external network, not as an extension of your plant LAN. Ask your carrier, in writing, whether client isolation is enabled on your APN and whether any SIM on it can reach any other; if the answer is no or unclear, that is a finding, not a query. Then put the APN behind the same boundary you would give a vendor VPN — an allow-list of exactly which field devices may talk to exactly which OT hosts on exactly which ports, terminated on a firewall you control rather than on the carrier’s. Separately, run a credential sweep across every field controller and gateway reached over cellular: a WAGO, Teltonika or comparable device still holding its shipped administrator password is the single cheapest thing an attacker can find. Finally, note where the damage actually landed — at a facility the wind farm’s operators did not consider connected to them. Map which of your sites share a carrier, an integrator or a management platform, because that shared plane is the blast radius nobody drew.

High Forescout Vedere Labs — AI-assisted exploit development against PLCs

A frontier model ported a working PLC exploit to a second controller in eight hours — then bricked it

Forescout’s Vedere Labs published the result of a deliberately awkward experiment: point a frontier AI model at real industrial hardware and see how far it gets. Starting from the team’s own remote code execution exploit for a WAGO 750-852, built on CVE-2021-31886 — a pre-authentication overflow in the Nucleus FTP server — they asked Claude to port it to the related 750-831, giving the model a terminal, Ghidra and the physical device. Total cost of the final stage: over eight hours and more than $500 in API charges. A follow-up attempt at a command-and-control implant wrote to flash and permanently bricked the PLC.

Vendor research, conducted in a lab — published 1 Sep 2026 by Forescout Research – Vedere Labs and reported by SecurityWeek and Cybersecurity Dive. Vedere Labs research VP Daniel dos Santos states the AI did not construct the exploit autonomously and needed researcher direction on what to exploit and how. Two long-standing assumptions get weaker. First, “our controller model is obscure enough that no public exploit exists” — the marginal cost of porting a known exploit across sibling models in the same family is what this work is driving down, so treat a vulnerability in one model as applying to its whole family until you have evidence otherwise. Medium Guidance analysis — Five Eyes OT isolation readiness, all critical-infrastructure sectors

The Five Eyes isolation guidance, reduced to six steps — and the hard one is running islanded for three months

Nozomi Networks published a working read of the Five Eyes “CI Fortify” guidance on 2 September, condensing the July joint publication into six sequential steps: identify the vital systems and their dependencies; establish trust levels and map every connection; pre-build the separation points before you need them; write and test graduated isolation plans; keep visibility while isolated; and run a defined set of post-isolation checks before reconnecting. The step operators keep stumbling on is the planning assumption underneath: the guidance expects you to be able to sustain isolated operation for up to three months, not for an afternoon. That reframes isolation from an incident-response button into a design constraint, because a three-month island needs local authentication, local historian and licence servers, on-site spares, paper procedures and a supply of consumables ordered without the ERP that normally orders them. This is preparedness advice rather than a response to any new threat, but it lands against a month of confirmed OT disruption, which is why it is being read now.

Vendor analysis of official guidance — Nozomi Networks blog, 2 Sep 2026, summarising the joint “CI Fortify” publication issued 28–29 Jul 2026 by CISA, the NSA, the FBI and Australia’s ASD/ACSC with partner agencies. Not an advisory and not an incident: nothing here reports exploitation, and the six-step framing is Nozomi’s condensation rather than the agencies’ own wording — read the source publication before you cite it in a programme document No CVE — the subject is architecture, dependency and rehearsal, not a product flaw
No patch applies — the deliverable is a tested separation point and a dependency list, not a software update Action: Do not start with the plan; start with the bill of dependencies, because that is what decides whether the plan is fiction. Pick one plant and list every service the process cannot run a full shift without that lives outside the cell: domain authentication for HMI logins, DNS and NTP, the licence server, the historian, MES and recipe management, remote vendor support, and the ERP transaction that releases raw material. For each, record how long the plant runs without it and what the written manual fallback is — then fix the two that fail first, usually authentication and time. Second, build the separation point in advance and label it: a specific firewall rule set, a physical break, or a switch port, with a named person authorised to operate it and a documented decision threshold, so the choice to isolate is not being invented during an incident by someone waiting on legal. Third, solve the visibility problem the guidance flags, because most monitoring dies with the link — a local collector that keeps recording while isolated and reconciles later is worth more than any cloud dashboard here. Fourth, write the reconnection checklist now: what you verify on the isolated side before you re-open, in what order, and who signs it. Finally, calibrate honestly — if three months is unreachable for your site, say so in writing, state the number you can actually sustain, and put the gap in front of the executive who funds spares and stock, because that gap is a business decision, not an engineering failure.
