Medium Nozomi Networks Labs research — PROFINET DCP memory corruption in a Siemens OT/IT edge gateway

Four ways to knock a Siemens edge gateway off the PROFINET segment — and blind SCADA while you do it

Nozomi Networks Labs published research on 15 September detailing four memory-corruption flaws in the PROFINET Discovery and basic Configuration Protocol implementation on the Siemens SCALANCE LPE9403 industrial PC, found using emulation-driven snapshot fuzzing with the Unicorn CPU emulator and AFL++. The flaws are tracked as CVE-2025-40575 through CVE-2025-40578 — an uninitialised-value issue, a NULL pointer dereference and two out-of-bounds reads — each carrying a CVSS 4.0 score of 5.3, and affect firmware up to V4.0 HF0. The DCP daemon runs as root, requires no authentication and is bound to all three device interfaces, so an attacker already on the same Layer 2 segment can crash it with crafted Ethernet frames. Siemens has shipped fixed firmware.

Critical CVE-2026-5430 — WSO2 API Manager and gateway products; CVSS 9.8, exploitation attempts observed from 13 September

Forged admin tokens against WSO2 API gateways — a flaw patched in April is now being tried in the wild

watchTowr reported on 15 September that its honeypot network began catching JSON Web Tokens carrying baked-in administrator privileges on 13 September, aimed at CVE-2026-5430 in WSO2 API Manager. The flaw, rated CVSS 9.8 and patched by WSO2 in April, is an improper verification of a cryptographic signature: a token signed with an algorithm the product does not properly support is accepted anyway, letting an unauthenticated attacker present themselves as an administrator. WSO2 API Control Plane, Traffic Manager and Universal Gateway are affected alongside API Manager. Successful use gives access to the APIs behind the gateway along with stored credentials and the secrets of registered applications. For industrial operators the exposure is indirect but real: API gateways of this kind increasingly front historian, MES, asset-management and telemetry services, and the secrets they hold are frequently the same service accounts used on the plant side of the boundary.

Medium EU Cyber Resilience Act — Reporting obligations IN FORCE since 11 Sep 2026

The clock has started: anyone selling a connected product into the EU now owes a 24-hour warning on exploited vulnerabilities

The EU Cyber Resilience Act entered its reporting phase on 11 September 2026. As of yesterday, manufacturers of products with digital elements must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents affecting their products, with an early warning inside 24 hours and a fuller notification inside 72 hours. The scope reaches far past consumer IoT: PLCs, RTUs, drives, gateways, HMIs, protocol converters and the engineering software around them are products with digital elements, and the obligation follows the manufacturer regardless of where the affected device is installed. Legal analysts advise naming an owner now and building the assessment, escalation and customer-communication path in advance, coordinating it with existing NIS2 and GDPR duties rather than standing up a third parallel process.

In force — reporting obligations under the EU Cyber Resilience Act took effect 11 Sep 2026, confirmed by Industrial Cyber’s 11 Sep 2026 report and consistent with legal analysis published 1 Sep 2026. Info Regulation — FERC approves a revised NERC reliability standard; North American bulk-power system

FERC signs off on CIP-014-4 — a tighter, more consistent test for which substations count as critical

FERC issued an order on 10 September 2026 approving NERC Reliability Standard CIP-014-4, published in the Federal Register on 15 September. CIP-014 is the physical security standard for the bulk-power system, and this revision targets the part registered entities have found most contestable: how transmission stations, transmission substations and primary control centres are identified and assessed as critical to reliable operation. The revision pushes toward a more consistent, repeatable identification methodology across entities rather than adding a new control set. It is a physical-security standard, but OT security staff should not file it under someone else’s programme — the risk assessment that decides a station is critical is the same assessment that should be driving cyber scoping for the relays, RTUs, protection schemes and communications at that station, and at many utilities the two exercises are still run separately, years apart, on different criteria.

Regulatory action, not a threat — confirmed: FERC order approving CIP-014-4 issued 10 September 2026 and published in the Federal Register on 15 September 2026. Not fixed here: implementation timing and the compliance date follow the approved implementation plan, so read the order and NERC’s plan rather than assuming an effective date No CVE — reliability standard, not a vulnerability
No patch applies — compliance and assessment activity Action: For registered entities, pull the order and the associated implementation plan and hand the identification methodology to whoever runs your transmission planning assessments, because that is where the work sits. The part worth doing beyond the compliance minimum is the cross-check: line up your CIP-014 critical station list against your CIP-002 BES Cyber System impact ratings and see whether the same facilities appear in both. Where a station is physically critical but its cyber assets are rated low-impact, that mismatch is a defensible question to raise internally now rather than in an audit — it usually reflects two assessments run on different criteria years apart, not a deliberate decision. For utilities outside NERC’s footprint and for other sectors, the transferable idea is the methodology itself: a repeatable, documented basis for deciding which sites carry consequence, applied consistently across the estate, rather than a critical-site list inherited from whoever built it.
