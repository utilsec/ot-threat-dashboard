High Nation-State / Active Exploitation

NSA, CISA, FBI and 12 allied nations warn Russia’s FSB Center 16 is harvesting configs from critical-infrastructure routers

A joint advisory (AA26-194A, “Improve Router Hygiene”) attributes a long-running campaign to Russia’s FSB Center 16 — the actor also tracked as Berserk Bear / Dragonfly / Energetic Bear. The actors scan for internet-exposed networking devices running SNMP with default or weak community strings, then issue SNMP writes abusing the Cisco config-copy MIB to exfiltrate device configurations over TFTP; they also opportunistically exploit Cisco Smart Install and known device CVEs. Stolen configs expose network topology, credentials and VPN details — ideal reconnaissance for deeper access. Named sectors most at risk include Energy, Communications, Defense Industrial Base, Financial Services, Government (especially state/local) and Healthcare. For OT teams, the perimeter routers and firewalls fronting plant networks are exactly the under-audited devices in scope.

Active — agency-confirmed, opportunistic exploitation of exposed devices No single CVE — config/protocol abuse (SNMP, Smart Install) plus known CVEs
Mitigations available — disable Smart Install, move to SNMPv3 authPriv Action: Inventory routers/switches/firewalls fronting OT and remote sites. Disable Cisco Smart Install where unused, replace SNMPv1/v2c and default community strings with SNMPv3 (authPriv), and block SNMP/TFTP from untrusted networks. Pull device configs off internet reachability, review them for exposed credentials, and rotate any that were reachable.

Info Guidance / OT Resilience

CISA and Five Eyes partners publish “CI Fortify” — pre-engineer OT isolation before an attack, not during one

On 28 July CISA, Australia’s ASD/ACSC, the UK NCSC, the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security and the FBI released joint guidance, “CI Fortify — Advice for Isolating Vital Systems,” giving critical-infrastructure operators practical steps to cut vital OT and enabling systems off from all other networks during a crisis and keep delivering the essential service in isolation for an extended period. The core message is that isolation has to be identified, engineered and rehearsed in advance: first define the minimum systems needed to keep the service running, then document every connection those systems have to corporate networks, remote access, cloud, internet-facing infrastructure, vendors and other operators, and build tested separation points you can actually invoke. Landing the same week as the Minnesota water incident (see top story), it is the defensive counterpart to that scenario — a ready-made framework for the manual-fallback and disconnect decisions those utilities had to make live.

Defensive guidance — not a threat or vulnerability No CVE — Five Eyes joint OT-resilience guidance
Guidance item — no patch applicable Action: Use CI Fortify to build a tested isolation playbook: define the minimum systems and data needed to keep your critical service running, map every dependency and connection into and out of them, and pre-engineer the separation points and manual/degraded-ops procedures you would trigger in a crisis. Then exercise it — the guidance’s whole premise is that isolation improvised mid-attack fails.

Info Regulation / NERC CIP

NERC CIP-012-2 now in force — real-time data between control centers gets tighter protection

CIP-012-2 became effective 1 July 2026, strengthening requirements to protect real-time operational data exchanged between control centers against unauthorized disclosure, modification and loss of availability — closing gaps left by the earlier revision. It lands alongside CIP-003-9, whose enforcement began in April, which extends security-governance and vendor electronic-remote-access requirements into low-impact BES environments that historically saw minimal oversight. For registered entities the practical questions now are scope and evidence: which control-center links and low-impact assets are in play, and what documentation demonstrates compliance. Non-NERC operators can still borrow the intent — protect control-to-control communications and govern low-criticality remote access — as good practice.

If you are not NERC-regulated, treat both as a free maturity benchmark for protecting inter-site control traffic and governing low-criticality remote access.

Info Policy / Standards

OT Cyber Coalition pushes to make ISA/IEC 62443 the single global OT standard

The Operational Technology Cybersecurity Coalition released a position paper on 23 July urging US policymakers to formally recognize ISA/IEC 62443 as the global baseline for OT cybersecurity, arguing that an expanding patchwork of sector-specific mandates piles on compliance cost without improving real security. The paper points to the TSA’s pipeline security directives, NERC CIP’s “double-reporting” burden in the energy sector, and the EU’s overlapping NIS2, CER and Cyber Resilience Act requirements as cases where fragmented rules re-create controls already defined in 62443. For asset owners it is a useful signal of where harmonization pressure is building — and a reminder that a 62443-anchored program tends to map cleanly onto most of these regimes at once.

Policy position paper — not a threat or vulnerability No CVE — standards/regulatory advocacy item
Awareness item — no patch applicable Action: No operational action required. If you are choosing a control framework or rationalizing several compliance regimes at once, use ISA/IEC 62443 as the common backbone and map TSA, NERC CIP and NIS2 obligations to it — it reduces duplicate evidence work and positions you for whichever way harmonization lands.
