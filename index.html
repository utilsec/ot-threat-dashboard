Critical Advisory — Active threat to controllers

The S7 advisory’s real finding: attackers are running AI-written exploitation scripts dressed up as OT monitoring tools

Joint advisory AA26-231A, published 19 August by NSA, CISA, the FBI, DOE and EPA, says threat actors are running AI-generated Python scripts — packaged to look like ordinary OT monitoring software — to find and reach Siemens S7 controllers across US critical infrastructure. The tooling is built on the snap7.dll and python-snap7 libraries and speaks S7comm, giving read and write access to PLC memory, configuration data and ladder logic. Targets are located through internet-wide scanning services such as Censys and ZoomEye, then reached through exposed interfaces, outdated firmware or weak authentication. S7-200, S7-300, S7-400, S7-1200 and S7-1500 are all named — including F-series safety controllers — across critical manufacturing, energy, water and wastewater, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities. The related campaign against water-sector controllers is where the confirmed damage sits — attackers modified or deleted project-file logic, manipulated HMI and SCADA display data, and disabled shutdown and alarm functions, creating unsafe conditions with no operator notification.

High Incident post-mortem — Confirmed OT disruption, energy

A turbine was stopped through the utility’s own private cellular network — a path nobody was watching

Added this edition after a sweep of European CERT publications. CERT Polska’s technical write-up, published 8 August, is the most useful OT intrusion narrative of the month because the entry route is one almost nobody inventories. The attacker first compromised a FortiGate VPN appliance at a wind farm, then used a Teltonika cellular router on that network to reach a private APN — a carrier-managed closed cellular network operated by the distribution system operator and treated by everyone on it as trusted. From inside the APN the attacker scanned, found a WAGO PFC200 controller answering on its web administration interface with default credentials, and pivoted onward into the OT network of a combined heat and power plant. The result was physical: a steam turbine and the plant’s process-water treatment system were shut down, interrupting cogeneration. CERT Polska describes it as the first observed case of an OT network being reached through a private APN.

Confirmed by CERT Polska — national CERT technical report published 8 Aug 2026 documenting the intrusion chain and the resulting shutdown of a steam turbine and process-water treatment at a Polish CHP plant. The intrusion itself occurred in December 2025, on the same day as coordinated activity against roughly 30 renewable energy facilities and a second CHP plant; the report is the new development, not the attack. No attribution has been published No single CVE drives this — a compromised VPN appliance, an unrestricted private APN and a controller left on default credentials, chained
Patch the FortiGate, yes — but the finding here is architectural: the private APN was implicitly trusted and never segmented Action: Find out whether your organisation has a private APN or any carrier-managed closed network, and who owns it. In most utilities it was provisioned by the telecoms team or an integrator for remote sites, billed to operations, and never treated as a network in its own right — no segmentation, no monitoring, no asset register. Critical Enforcement — PRC intrusion infrastructure

US seizes the Chinese state-sponsored scanning and relay network built out of other people’s IoT devices

The Justice Department and FBI announced on 26 August that they had seized the domains behind QScan and QTRouter, two linked platforms operated by a PRC state-sponsored group DOJ tracks as QTFY and ties to a Nanjing technology company. QScan automatically scans for and infects internet-facing IoT devices worldwide; QTRouter then enrols those devices, alongside commercial proxy services and leased virtual private servers, into a network that hides the Chinese origin of intrusions. DOJ says the service was sold on to customers including China’s Ministry of State Security and the People’s Liberation Army. Named victims include NASA, the Federal Reserve, the Department of Energy, HHS and the US Senate, with power companies, hospitals, telecoms and defence contractors among private targets. Info Regulation — US incident reporting (CIRCIA)

The 72-hour reporting clock is due this month — and most OT programmes cannot start it

CISA is now targeting September 2026 to finalise the rule implementing the Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act, having missed the statutory October 2025 deadline. Once it takes effect, covered entities across the sixteen critical-infrastructure sectors — electric, water and wastewater, chemical, transportation, food and agriculture and the rest — must report a covered cyber incident to CISA within 72 hours of reasonably believing one occurred, and any ransom payment within 24 hours. Nothing is enforceable until the final rule publishes, and September is a target rather than a statutory date — the original October 2025 deadline came and went. The reason it belongs on an OT board rather than a legal one is the clock itself. Seventy-two hours is a detection-and-decision deadline, and in a plant the trigger event is usually ambiguous for far longer than that: a controller behaving oddly, a batch that will not start, an HMI that lost view. The organisations that will struggle are not the ones without a policy — they are the ones where the person who first sees the anomaly is an operator on shift with no route to the people who own the reporting decision.

Forward-looking regulatory milestone, not an incident or vulnerability. CISA has stated a September 2026 target for the CIRCIA final rule; the rule is not yet published and no obligation is enforceable as of this edition. Treat the date as a planning assumption — this deadline has already slipped once No CVE — incident-reporting regulation
No patch applies — this is detection, escalation and decision-rights work Action: Do the two things that take longer than reading the rule will. First, settle decision rights on paper now: name the single role that decides whether an OT event is a reportable incident, name the deputy for nights and weekends, and confirm both are reachable by an operator on shift without going through a ticket queue. Most OT escalation paths are built to reach a maintenance engineer, not a legal or compliance decision-maker, and the 72-hour clock starts at reasonable belief, not at confirmation. Second, run the clock as an exercise before it is real — take a plausible plant scenario, such as an HMI losing view of a skid, and time how long it actually takes from the operator noticing to someone senior enough to make a reporting call knowing about it. If that number is measured in shifts rather than hours, the gap is your finding, and it is fixable with a phone tree and a standing instruction rather than a project. Then make sure your OT logging retains enough to describe what happened inside three days, because a report you cannot substantiate is its own problem.
