Critical Advisory — Active threat to controllers

The S7 advisory’s real finding: attackers are running AI-written exploitation scripts dressed up as OT monitoring tools

Joint advisory AA26-231A, published 19 August by NSA, CISA, the FBI, DOE and EPA, says threat actors are running AI-generated Python scripts — packaged to look like ordinary OT monitoring software — to find and reach Siemens S7 controllers across US critical infrastructure. The tooling is built on the snap7.dll and python-snap7 libraries and speaks S7comm, giving read and write access to PLC memory, configuration data and ladder logic. Targets are located through internet-wide scanning services, then reached through exposed interfaces, outdated firmware or weak authentication. S7-200, S7-300, S7-400, S7-1200 and S7-1500 are all named, across critical manufacturing, energy, water and wastewater, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities. The agencies assess the activity as persistent reconnaissance and capability development positioning actors for later disruption, say the targeting is broader than Siemens, and state plainly that this is not a theoretical risk. The related campaign against water-sector controllers is where the confirmed damage sits: attackers modified or deleted project-file logic, manipulated HMI and SCADA display data, and disabled shutdown and alarm functions, creating unsafe conditions with no operator notification.

Critical Incident — Confirmed OT disruption

CERT Polska: an attacker crossed a shared private cellular network from a wind farm into a heating plant and stopped its turbine

After an investigation running more than three months, CERT Polska disclosed on 8 August what it assesses to be the first real-world case of an attacker reaching an OT network through a private APN. The intrusion began at a wind farm’s edge, moved onto a cellular router, and crossed a distribution system operator’s private access point name — a dedicated mobile network the DSO had provisioned with a carrier — into a controller at an entirely unrelated combined heat and power plant. The APN was configured to let arbitrary devices on it talk to one another, so what everyone involved treated as a segmentation boundary behaved as a flat shared LAN. At the CHP plant the attacker stopped a steam turbine and the water treatment system that produces process water, interrupting cogeneration at a facility supplying heat to roughly 50,000 residents. The incident itself occurred on 29 December 2025; the disclosure and the technical detail are new this month, and the design pattern it breaks is one agencies have actively recommended.

Critical Incident — Engineering data theft

Clop’s PLM extortion campaign names Shell, GE and Philips — and the stolen material is plant drawings and blueprints

Clop has listed more than 50 organisations tied to its exploitation of internet-exposed PTC Windchill and FlexPLM, the product lifecycle management platforms used across aerospace, defence, automotive, heavy machinery, medtech and process industry. Shell confirmed on 14 August that it is investigating a potential incident after the gang claimed 89 GB; GE said it is assessing a claim of 391 GB; Philips confirmed a contained compromise with no impact on customer environments against a claim of 13.5 GB. The entry point is CVE-2026-12569, an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw in Windchill and FlexPLM rated CVSS 9.8 and reached through deserialisation of untrusted data. No OT or control-system impact has been reported at any victim — this is confidentiality loss, not disruption. The OT relevance is what was taken: PLM holds the as-designed record of your plants, machines and safety systems, and in most organisations it is neither inventoried as an OT-adjacent asset nor covered by an OT threat model. An adversary who has your drawings does not need your network to plan against you.

High Research — Exposure measurement

Forescout counted 4,407 internet-facing Rockwell controllers — and found 22 of them in the cities that were attacked

Vedere Labs ran an internet-wide scan on 3 August and published on 5–6 August: 4,407 Rockwell Automation and Allen-Bradley PLCs answering on EtherNet/IP port 44818 worldwide, 2,844 of them in the United States. Twenty-two sat in cities hit by the water-utility attacks. Nineteen of those 22 appear to run firmware susceptible to CVE-2017-16740, a stack-based buffer overflow in MicroLogix 1400 Series B and C on revision 21.002 and earlier, fixed nine years ago in 21.003. The finding that should change how you hunt is the network path: nineteen of the 22 sat on the same mobile carrier network. This exposure is cellular, not corporate. These are field-deployed controllers at pump stations, lift stations and water towers, backhauled to SCADA over cellular modems that hand them public IP addresses — which means they never appear in a corporate firewall rule review, and your network team may have no record they exist. Forescout also found the usual companions around the same utilities: expired TLS certificates, abandoned remote-access hostnames and forgotten servers.

Measured exposure corroborating an active campaign — scan data published 5–6 Aug 2026; the water-sector attacks it maps to are confirmed. The specific 22 controllers are not confirmed as the compromised devices CVE-2017-16740 — Rockwell CVSS 8.6, Modbus TCP buffer overflow in MicroLogix 1400 Series B/C on firmware 21.002 and earlier; fixed in 21.003

High Threat intelligence — Control device security

Team82 chained 23 flaws in Copeland refrigeration controllers to root, then warmed a fridge while the display kept reading normal

Claroty’s Team82 disclosed 23 vulnerabilities in the Copeland XWEB Pro supervisory controller family — XWEB300D PRO and XWEB500D PRO — 21 of them rated high severity. The chain starts with a logic flaw in the device’s Lua-based authentication middleware: because any Lua table, including an empty one, evaluates as true, supplying an unrecognised auth_mode returns an empty-but-truthy object and the router waves the request through to the administrative API. A separate predictable password-generation routine yields a deterministic daily administrator credential, giving a second way in. From there Team82 found 19 OS command-injection issues across API and CGI endpoints, all running with elevated privileges, for root-level remote code execution. The part that should interest engineers is the physical demonstration: from a compromised XWEB the researchers drove a serially connected XR60CX field controller over Modbus, disabled the compressor and cooling fans, and simultaneously fed the display the probe’s legitimate reading — so the load spoiled while the temperature record, the same record kept for regulatory compliance, looked clean. Copeland worked with the researchers and shipped firmware 1.13.

No in-the-wild exploitation reported — coordinated disclosure, vendor-patched. The physical impact was demonstrated in Team82’s own test rig, not observed at a victim site Not in CISA KEV. Individual CVE IDs are not consistently published across the 23 findings — treat firmware version, not a CVE list, as the tracking key
Patched — Copeland XWEB Pro firmware 1.13 Action: Find your refrigeration and environmental supervisory controllers before you go looking for CVEs — on most sites they were installed by a refrigeration contractor, sit on a flat store or plant network, and appear in no asset register. Upgrade XWEB Pro units to 1.13, take their management interfaces off the internet, and put contractor access behind a brokered, logged path rather than a port forward. Then generalise the finding: a supervisory controller that both drives field devices and produces the compliance temperature record is a single point where product loss and record falsification happen together, so the integrity check that matters is an independent temperature source — a separate logger, or the field controller read directly — that an attacker on the supervisor cannot edit. This applies to cold chain in food, pharmacy and clinical storage, and to data-centre and building cooling built on the same architecture.

Critical Advisory — Identity platform

A CVSS 10.0 unauthenticated code-execution flaw in Entra ID was exploited before disclosure — and there is nothing for you to patch

CVE-2026-69836 is an unsafe-deserialisation flaw in Microsoft Entra ID that allowed an unauthenticated remote attacker to execute code against the service, scored CVSS 3.1 10.0 with no privileges and no user interaction required. Microsoft disclosed it on 20 August and has said it was exploited in the wild before the fix. Because Entra ID is a Microsoft-operated cloud service, the remediation was applied on Microsoft’s own infrastructure — there is no KB, no package and no configuration change for customers, and the advisory was published for transparency rather than action. That is precisely why it belongs on an OT board. Entra ID is the identity plane behind the VPN concentrators, remote-access portals, jump hosts and vendor accounts that reach most plant networks, so a service-side compromise window is a window against every downstream authentication decision made during it — and unlike an appliance CVE, you cannot close it, verify it or schedule it. Info Regulation — US information sharing

The legal cover for threat-intelligence sharing expires on 30 September, five weeks out

The Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015 provides the statutory framework, liability protections and legal safeguards under which private organisations share cyber threat indicators and defensive measures with federal agencies and one another. Info Regulation — EU Cyber Resilience Act

The Cyber Resilience Act’s vulnerability-management standards are due for adoption on 30 August — and IEC 62443 is the reference

The standardisation programme underpinning the EU Cyber Resilience Act sets 30 August 2026 as the latest date for adoption of the horizontal Type A standard and the Type B standards covering vulnerability management. For industrial equipment this is the point at which the CRA stops being an abstraction: IEC 62443 is emerging as the key technical reference for CRA compliance in industrial settings, which means the standard many operators have used as a voluntary maturity model becomes the yardstick their suppliers are measured against in a regulated conformity process. Asset owners are not the regulated party here, but they are the beneficiary, and the leverage arrives at procurement rather than in the plant.
