Refrigeration hack hit about 700 French supermarkets; Forescout now counts nearly 13,000 exposed refrigeration control services worldwide

On 22 October Forescout reported 12,976 internet-exposed refrigeration management services in 99 countries — the class of web-managed controller attackers forced into continuous defrost at French supermarkets on the nights of 11–13 October. France’s finance minister put the toll at about 700 stores, including Carrefour, E.Leclerc, Intermarché and Monoprix; the Paris prosecutor says fires also resulted and is investigating with ANSSI. CERT-FR names Danfoss AK-SC and AK-SM controllers plus Copeland and Dixell XWEB, and its 16 October revision says the campaigns are probably still under way. Most affected units are end-of-life, and the EU Cyber Resilience Act will not compel fixes for them. No actor, CVE or entry route has been named, and no new attack nights have been reported since 13 October.

Confirmed attack on refrigeration control systems — acknowledged by the retailers, by France’s finance minister and by ANSSI, and under investigation by the Paris prosecutor. Edge Devices Exploitation keeps clustering at the remote-access and WAN edge, not in control-loop devices

The exploited flaws this cycle sit where outside networks meet the plant. On 16 October CISA added the BeyondTrust Remote Support zero-day (exploited since late August) and the FortiOS flaw behind Unit 42’s Peerstitch case to KEV with three-day deadlines, and on 22 October Arista disclosed a third exploited VeloCloud Orchestrator zero-day in four months (CVE-2026-114625; not in KEV as of CISA’s 21 October catalog). They follow October’s Citrix NetScaler and Ivanti Connect Secure zero-days (see Incidents & Attacks and Critical Advisories). PHP2Shell showed the same speed in a software component: Amazon saw attempts four days before PHP shipped fixes on 21 October. None of these devices runs a control loop, but each is a path a vendor, integrator or technician uses to reach one.

High Advisory — joint advisory AA26-293A from CISA, the FBI, the US Secret Service and German and UK partners on SafePay ransomware, which the agencies say has hit at least 600 organizations across multiple critical-infrastructure sectors

CISA, the FBI and partners warn that SafePay ransomware has hit at least 600 organizations since August 2024

Advisory AA26-293A, issued on 20 October by CISA, the FBI and the US Secret Service with Germany’s LKA Rheinland-Pfalz, the UK National Crime Agency and the North West Regional Organised Crime Unit, says SafePay has hit at least 600 organizations from multiple critical-infrastructure sectors in North America, South America, Europe and Australia. Entry is usually through exposed or brute-forced credentials, phished accounts, VPN logins from Mullvad addresses and automated exploitation of edge-device flaws; the group then steals data, deletes shadow copies, resets network-attached storage that holds backups and moves with tools such as WinRM, PsExec and RDP. The advisory carries indicators and ATT&CK mappings for activity from November 2024 to August 2026. It names no victims, and reporting on it describes no OT, energy or water impact, so the OT risk here is the usual one: a ransomware crew reaching the IT network that plant systems depend on.

Citrix has confirmed a third NetScaler zero-day under attack, days after patching two others that were exploited for weeks before disclosure, and on 9 October it shipped a fourth fix. The exploited trio is CVE-2026-88771, CVE-2026-88772 and CVE-2026-88779; the federal KEV deadline for the newest passed on 7 October. Because NetScaler often fronts remote access into plant networks, an unhunted appliance is a possible foothold toward OT.

High Incident — Ivanti Connect Secure VPN zero-day chain exploited on fully patched appliances since early Oct; new actor UTA0576 tracked by Volexity; one flaw still unpatched; no OT victim named

Ivanti warns attackers chained three zero-day flaws, one still unpatched, to take root on fully patched Connect Secure VPNs

Ivanti disclosed on 13 October that a small number of customers were hit through a chain of three zero-day flaws that gave unauthenticated, root-level code execution on fully patched Connect Secure appliances. Volexity, which found it while investigating a customer incident, dates its case to 3 October (other researchers saw activity from 1 October) and tracks a previously undocumented actor, UTA0576. The intruders stole domain credentials stored on the appliance, searched Active Directory for LAPS passwords and BitLocker recovery keys, sprayed passwords and scanned internal networks, but left no web shell or backdoor. Volexity judges, with low confidence, that the group is financially motivated, and, with medium confidence, that it uses AI models for exploit development. Fixes cover two of the flaws (CVE-2026-104935); the third, a privilege-escalation bug, is still unpatched. VPN gateways like this commonly sit at the IT/OT boundary.

Vendor-confirmed exploitation in the wild against a limited number of customers. Info Research — Booz Allen Hamilton lab test of frontier AI models moving from an IT foothold into an industrial control environment

Booz Allen: frontier AI models went from a perimeter breach to a control-zone action in just over 16 minutes in a lab test

Booz Allen’s 8 October report, From Digital to Physical: The AI Threat Is Here, describes tests in its isolated OT lab in Chantilly, Virginia, modelled on a manufacturing facility. Frontier models from several unnamed vendors met all eight test objectives, going from a perimeter compromise to energising an output on a controller in just over 16 minutes, with a human required to approve every exploit and physical action. One agent found a robotic arm’s API, gained admin access and moved the arm, and a SCADA gateway holding live authenticated sessions to 14 devices gave the models a ready path. Some controls held: one controller rejected a stop command, and local-control mode blocked a remote motor start. Booz Allen calls the results preliminary; some known flaws were left unpatched on purpose and no zero-days were developed. Its Kyle Miller told Dark Reading that removing the approval step would likely have roughly halved the time.

Vendor lab research — Confirmed vs claimed: results are Booz Allen’s own controlled-environment findings, described as preliminary, with human approval required for every exploit and physical action; no real-world attack is reported No CVE — research finding; not in CISA KEV
No patch applies — the controls are segmentation, session hygiene and detection Action: Review which gateways, jump hosts and historians hold standing authenticated sessions into control zones, remove persistent sessions where possible, and test whether your monitoring would spot fast, automated movement between IT and OT.

High Advisory — Siemens Software Center uncontrolled search-path (DLL side-loading) flaw CVE-2026-110936 (CVSS v3.1 6.7 / v4.0 8.4) exploited in the “Romulus” ClickFix campaign; in CISA KEV since 20 Oct 2026

Siemens confirms a Software Center flaw is being exploited to deliver malware, and CISA adds it to the KEV catalog

Siemens first published CVE-2026-110936 on 15 October and on 19 October confirmed it is being exploited. Software Center, the tool engineers use to install and update Siemens software, did not restrict where it searches for helper DLLs, so attackers paired a genuine, validly signed copy with their own malicious libraries in an installer package. Siemens ties the activity to the “Romulus” multi-platform ClickFix campaign, which tricks users into running pasted commands, and names no final malware family. It says no Siemens system, product or update channel was breached, to its knowledge. Version 4.0.9.8 fixes the flaw, and installed copies update on next start. CISA added the flaw to KEV on 20 October with a 3 November deadline and has republished Siemens’ advisory as ICSA-26-295-03. Exploitation needs local access and a user who runs the lure, so engineering workstations running Siemens tooling are the exposure.

Coast Guard issues NVIC 02-26 on maritime cybersecurity plans and IT/OT risk assessments, with a 16 July 2027 deadline

The US Coast Guard released NVIC 02-26, a companion to its maritime cybersecurity rule, for operators of US-flagged vessels, port facilities and Outer Continental Shelf facilities regulated under the Maritime Transportation Security Act. It explains how to name a cybersecurity officer, complete a cyber risk assessment and write a Cybersecurity Plan for Coast Guard approval; all three are due by 16 July 2027. The approach is performance-based: operators choose how to meet each requirement, but must back claims with documentation and observable evidence, and a plan that only restates the regulation must also explain how it is met. Penetration testing is not needed for the first submission but is expected periodically over five-year renewal cycles, with attention to OT safety. Enclosures cover plan development, a sample officer letter, penetration-testing guidance and an optional NIST CSF method for scoping IT and OT systems.

Info Regulation — FERC staff report on CIP audit lessons learned flags late registration of new generation, aggregated low-impact systems and AI tools inside CIP networks; no CVE

FERC staff publish CIP audit lessons: unregistered new generation, stacked low-impact systems and AI tools in CIP networks

On 13 October FERC released a staff report drawing on its fiscal-2026 audits of NERC-registered entities. Most cybersecurity processes reviewed met the mandatory CIP standards, but auditors found three recurring problems. Some generator owners registered new resources late or never identified their BES Cyber Systems, and several inverter-based resources were running commercially without the required cyber evaluation, one more than a year past the threshold. Two entities treated systems as low impact that together met the medium-impact criteria once affiliate and contractor control was counted. Auditors also saw what the report calls “super intelligence” (SI) tools — chatbots, assistants and automated analytics — appearing inside CIP-networked environments, where they can mimic user behavior and open unauthorized access or lateral-movement paths faster than compliance programs adapt. The report is staff guidance, not a new requirement, and it suggests NERC may consider further guidance or standards changes.
