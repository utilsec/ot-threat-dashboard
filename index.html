Resilience Can plants really run without IT? Leaders and engineers disagree

SANS’s 2026 survey (see Threat Intelligence & Research) found every security leader expects plants to ride out a full IT compromise, while only 15% of practitioners say they could run safely by hand beyond 48 hours. Recent outages sharpened the point: Brazil’s Santos port moved automated procedures to manual handling after a cyberattack, and Sweden’s Kinnarps stopped three factories after an IT intrusion; the 13 November US ground stop, which officials now trace to a faulty air-traffic communications router with no sign of hacking; and a German hotel pool where a faulty chlorine doser injured guests because its email-only safety alert was stuck in the Microsoft 365 outage. In Chile, a 103 MW solar-and-battery plant is still off the grid nearly three months after a cyberattack (see Incidents & Attacks). On 17 November CISA’s acting director told CyberScoop that outages from bad patches or failed upgrades deserve the same resilience planning as attacks, citing water and grocery supply. Safety alarms need a path that does not depend on the cloud, and recovery plans need a realistic reconnection timeline.

Critical Incident — CISA/FBI/HHS/USDA/DOT joint warning (5 Nov 2026) and a second wave of refrigeration attacks across about 11 European countries (1–4 Nov); Belgium says its evidence points to Iran (12 Nov), no formal attribution

CISA and FBI warn of an ongoing threat to internet-connected refrigeration as European supermarket attacks reach at least 11 countries

Hackers are tampering with internet-connected refrigeration controllers in European supermarkets, and on 5 November CISA and the FBI, with HHS, USDA and DOT, warned the threat is ongoing and also reaches healthcare, chemical, transport and cold-storage operations. Hundreds of French stores were hit in October; a second wave on 1–2 November reached about 11 countries, forcing units into continuous heating or defrost, and three French blood-service sites had settings changed with no blood lost. On 10 November Sweden warned that more incidents are likely after refrigeration was tampered with at seven Coop stores and an undisclosed number of ICA stores on 2 November; its NCSC says the victims mainly ran older systems from one manufacturer protected by weak passwords. On 12 November Belgium’s interior minister said evidence on the Belgian attacks points to Iran but stopped short of an official attribution (see top story); a pro-Russian claim is unverified, and France has formally attributed only the separate 12 October attack, to Iran’s Revolutionary Guard. Exposure is falling but not gone: exposed Danfoss StoreView instances dropped from 3,004 to 708 between 9 October and 5 November (The Hacker News, 9 November). Sekoia reported on 10 November that the second wave used a non-public, unauthenticated flaw that renames the controller’s admin account, apparently a step towards resetting its password, and assesses that both waves may come from the same actor. If a controller is reachable from the internet, treat it as at risk today.

The pro-Russian claim (NoName057(16), Z-Pentest, 5 Nov) is unverified, and SentinelOne rates Russian involvement credible but unproven. France formally attributed its 12 October attack to Iran’s IRGC on 9 Nov (see top story); no government has formally attributed the November wave or the US commissary freezers, though on 12 Nov Belgium’s interior minister said Belgian evidence points to Iran (not an official attribution). No fix for end-of-life controllers — isolate or replace them; supported units should run current firmware Action: Take every web-managed refrigeration controller and cold-chain monitor off the internet tonight (Danfoss AK-SM/AK-SC, Copeland and Dixell xweb first). Allow remote access only through a VPN with multi-factor authentication, change default passwords, check defrost durations and setpoints on every case, and alarm on temperature with monitoring that does not depend on the controller. Ask your refrigeration contractor in writing which sites it reaches over the internet. Keep logs and configurations before any reset, and never re-expose an end-of-life unit.

Critical Incident — PHP2Shell plus Gaia root flaw CVE-2026-123039 used to backdoor Check Point gateways and wipe a VMware ESXi host (wave 1 from 30 Oct, Israel; wave 2 from 2 Nov, multiple countries); CVE-2026-123039 added to CISA KEV 13 Nov 2026

Attackers rooted unpatched Check Point gateways through PHP2Shell, planted backdoors and wiped a VMware host; the root flaw is now in KEV

Check Point Research said on 12 November that attackers exploited the PHP2Shell flaws on unpatched Check Point Mobile Access portals from 30 October, about a week after fixes shipped, then used a local flaw in the gateway’s DynamicCLI service (CVE-2026-123039) to become root. They planted web shells and backdoors named TSGA and TSProxy, scanned the internal network, moved over SSH to a VMware ESXi host and ran a wiper that overwrites and deletes files. The first wave hit Israeli organizations, some destructively; a second wave from 2 November reached gateways in several countries and sectors with no destruction seen. The backdoors resemble tooling linked to the China-nexus group UNC5221, but Check Point does not attribute the campaign with confidence. CISA added CVE-2026-123039 to KEV on 13 November. No OT victim is reported, but these firewalls often sit at the IT/OT boundary and ESXi hosts often run HMIs and historians (editorial assessment).

Exploited in the wild — Confirmed vs claimed: the attack chain, backdoors and ESXi wipe are Check Point Research’s own incident findings; Check Point confirms CVE-2026-123039 is exploited and CISA lists it in KEV; victims are unnamed and no count is given; the China-nexus link is a tooling resemblance, and Check Point raises false-flag and access-broker scenarios; no OT impact is reported In CISA KEV — CVE-2026-123039 added 13 Nov 2026; PHP2Shell CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619 added 26 Oct 2026

If a portal was exposed and unpatched after 21 October, assume compromise: look for zz*.tpl files in the portal template directory and unfamiliar daemons, rebuild rather than clean, rotate every credential the gateway held, and check any ESXi host it could reach over SSH. Confirm you have offline backups of virtualized HMIs, historians and engineering workstations.

A Chilean solar-and-battery plant is still off the grid nearly three months after a cyberattack, and lawmakers want answers

The 103 MW Huatacondo solar plant in Chile’s Tarapacá region, with a 98 MW / 312 MWh battery system still in commissioning, has been disconnected from the national grid since late August after a cyberattack. Chile’s National Electric Coordinator triggered its disconnection protocol on 27 August after the owner reported the attack, and its 14 November daily report still lists both assets at zero availability under forced outage. For renewables operators, the lesson is how long a plant stays dark once it is pulled off the grid for safety and cannot be restored with confidence (editorial assessment).

High Threat Intel — Lumen Black Lotus Labs report (28 Oct) on Volt Typhoon’s “Silent Train” relay network and zero-day campaigns against management platforms; water, energy, transportation, maritime, rail and aviation among victim sectors; no OT intrusion described

Volt Typhoon has used a rotating “Silent Train” relay network since 2023 to break into management platforms, with water, energy and transport among the victims, Lumen reports

Lumen’s Black Lotus Labs reported on 28 October that Volt Typhoon has used “Silent Train”, a covert network of rapidly rotating, single-use chains of compromised small-office routers, IoT and enterprise devices, since November 2023 to reach targets and keep access to management and control-plane systems. Lumen ties the group to four confirmed zero-day campaigns and one likely one: Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN (CVE-2026-20127, CVSS v3.1 10.0, Cisco), VeloCloud Orchestrator (CVE-2026-16812), N-able N-central (CVE-2025-8875 and CVE-2025-8876), Versa Director (no CVE given) and, with moderate confidence, Quest KACE (no CVE). These are older flaws, not the VeloCloud flaw CVE-2026-114625 that CISA added to KEV on 23 October. Victims include telecoms, government, defence contractors, IT firms and MSPs, and universities, and Lumen lists water, energy, transportation, maritime, rail, aviation and emergency services among the critical-infrastructure sectors hit; Hawaii, California and Texas recur as focal points (Zero Day).
