Top Story

A small UK generator sat offline for four days after a cyberattack — and it sat below the regulated reporting threshold

The Sunday Telegraph reported on 22 August that Iran-linked hackers took a British power plant offline for four days, calling it unprecedented. For anyone running generation, the useful version of this story is the one that survives verification. The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero has now confirmed on the record that an incident occurred, describing it as affecting a small-scale energy generator and stating that at no point was there a risk to the wider energy system. That is a government acknowledgement of a genuine cyber event at a UK generating asset — the first publicly acknowledged case of its kind. What remains unconfirmed is most of the headline: the four-day duration, the attribution to Iran and the IRGC, and the “unprecedented” characterisation all come from unnamed British and US officials via the Telegraph, and the NCSC declined to comment on the specific incident. The plant, operator, technology, initial access route and even the attack date — reported only as last month — remain unpublished, so there is no vector to defend against yet. The detail that carries the most operational weight is a quiet one: the NCSC is understood to have received no outage reports from regulated power station operators. That places the affected generator beneath the regulated reporting regime, and it reframes the whole story. Britain’s licensed generation estate is well governed and well watched; the population underneath it is not. Embedded generation, CHP and district energy schemes, standby and peaking sets, industrial self-generation, battery storage and small renewable sites are numerous, frequently unmanned, and commonly reached through a vendor’s remote-access path or a cellular router rather than a monitored corporate network. They rarely carry continuous monitoring, mandatory incident reporting, or a restoration plan anyone has exercised. A four-day recovery at a site whose loss never registered on the grid is precisely what that governance gap produces. If you operate generation, the question this raises is not about Iran. It is whether your own sub-threshold assets — the ones that fall outside your NIS or NERC CIP scope, the ones an integrator commissioned and still dials into — would take four days to bring back, and whether anyone would have told you.

Critical Advisory — Active threat to controllers

Five federal agencies warn AI-written scripts are reading Siemens S7 controllers across the energy sector

The NSA, CISA, FBI, Department of Energy and EPA issued a joint advisory on 19 August warning that threat actors are combining the open-source snap7 and python-snap7 automation libraries with AI-assisted scripting to build tools that mimic legitimate OT monitoring software. Those tools give read and write access to Siemens S7 controller memory, configuration data and ladder logic over S7comm. Energy is named among the most-targeted sectors, alongside critical manufacturing, water, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities. The agencies say actors locate exposed devices with internet scanning services and get in through default or weak credentials rather than any vulnerability. Affected lines run from the legacy S7-200 and S7-300 through the S7-1200 and S7-1500, including F-series safety controllers. The agencies assess the activity as positioning for later disruptive write operations.

High Incident — Nation-state / OT pivot

Attackers crossed from a wind farm into an unrelated Polish CHP plant over a shared private cellular APN

CERT Polska disclosed on 9 August, at DEF CON, that attackers reached the industrial control network of a Polish combined heat and power plant serving roughly 50,000 residents by pivoting through a private cellular data network. The intrusion began somewhere else entirely: an internet-exposed Fortinet VPN and firewall device at a wind farm on the same carrier APN. Because the APN was misconfigured to let arbitrary devices address one another, the attacker crossed from one generator’s network into another operator’s controllers. CERT Polska describes this as the first observed real-world use of a private APN as a route into an OT network. The incident occurred on 29 December 2025, the same day as coordinated attacks on 30 Polish renewable facilities and another CHP plant; ESET attributes that operation to the Russia-aligned Sandworm group with medium confidence. Analysis of the smaller plant took more than three months, which is why it was omitted from the original report.

Confirmed OT intrusion — control network reached at a heat and power plant No new CVE — exposed edge firewall plus a flat private cellular network
No patch — architectural: segment the APN, treat carrier networks as untrusted Action: Ask your carrier this week for the routing and device-isolation policy on every private APN your sites use, and get written confirmation that devices on it cannot address each other. Inventory cellular routers at remote generation, substation and DER sites and put a firewall with an explicit allow-list between each one and the control network it serves.

High Threat intel — Ransomware / edge exploitation

Six agencies warn the Gunra ransomware crew is walking into critical infrastructure through two 2025 Fortinet bugs

CISA, the FBI, the Defense Cyber Crime Center, the NSA, the Secret Service and the Korean National Police Agency published joint advisory AA26-222A on 10 August on Gunra, a ransomware operation that has hit organisations across critical infrastructure sectors since April 2025. The advisory’s operational point is unglamorous: Gunra’s initial access is overwhelmingly through internet-facing edge devices, and specifically two Fortinet flaws — CVE-2024-21762 and CVE-2024-55591 — both patched in 2025 and both still reachable on unpatched appliances. Once inside, the group steals data before encrypting, and runs a double-extortion leak site. The agencies published detection guidance, indicators and a set of mitigations centred on patching edge appliances, enforcing phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication and segmenting recovery infrastructure. For power operators the relevance is the access path rather than the ransomware. Fortinet appliances sit in front of a great many substation aggregation links, generation site DMZs and third-party maintenance connections, and the two CVEs Gunra relies on were fixed more than a year ago — meaning any operator still exposed is exposed to a documented, agency-confirmed campaign rather than a theoretical one.

Load the advisory’s indicators into your monitoring, and confirm that backup and recovery infrastructure is segmented from the domain that would be encrypted.

Team82 analysed more than 750,000 cyber-physical assets across some of the largest data centre facilities in the world and found nearly one in five sitting one hop away from a system making outbound internet connections. Citrix is telling admins to patch NetScaler now — an unauthenticated bypass on the appliance that fronts most OT remote access

Citrix published bulletin CTX696939 on 20 August covering two NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway flaws. The serious one, CVE-2026-19490, is an authentication bypass carrying a CVSS score of 9.3: on Gateway and AAA virtual-server configurations an unauthenticated attacker can skip the login step entirely. For a utility this is the appliance that fronts vendor and engineering access into EMS, SCADA and substation networks, and increasingly the one carrying operator access into DER and storage fleets. An authentication bypass there is not an IT problem — it is a door into the control-centre perimeter that does not need a stolen credential.

Fire-alarm management software stores its credentials in cleartext in memory, CISA warns

CISA published ICSA-26-232-01 on 20 August covering Johnson Controls Simplex Incident Manager, the software operators use to monitor and manage Simplex fire alarm panels across a property portfolio. Versions up to and including V2.01 keep user passwords and authentication tokens unencrypted in system memory while the application runs, tracked as CVE-2026-27875 and classified as CWE-316. That is the access level this flaw needs.

CISA publishes an advisory for flaws in Malcolm, its own free OT network monitoring suite

Advisory ICSA-26-230-01 covers vulnerabilities in Malcolm, the network traffic analysis suite CISA distributes free for OT and ICS environments. High Advisory — SCADA platform / deserialisation

AVEVA Enterprise SCADA can be pushed into executing code through tampered serialised data

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-01, published 13 August as part of a 15-advisory batch, covers a deserialisation flaw in AVEVA Enterprise SCADA. An attacker who already holds the “DNA Authority — Operator” privilege can tamper with serialised data and have it execute as code when the server deserialises it. The 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025 release families are affected, along with certain Enterprise SCADA HMI versions. CISA reports no known public exploitation targeting this flaw. AVEVA’s guidance is to restrict operator rights to trusted users, disallow BLT test clients in production, and audit devices, network topology and perimeter defences; the vendor also points to KB117814 on removing the binary formatter. Enterprise SCADA is deployed on transmission and distribution control estates and across midstream operations, so the population here is control-room grade rather than office grade. The saving grace is the privilege requirement — but “authenticated operator” is a low bar inside a control room where shared or long-lived accounts are still common, and it is exactly the account a contractor or an intruder with a stolen VPN credential ends up holding.

No known exploitation — CISA reports none targeting this flaw Not in CISA KEV
Vendor fix and mitigations available via bulletin AVEVA-2026-005 Action: Confirm which AVEVA Enterprise SCADA release each control centre is on and apply the fix per security bulletin AVEVA-2026-005. In the meantime, audit who actually holds “DNA Authority — Operator” rights, remove it from anyone who does not need it, and confirm BLT test clients are not present on production nodes.

Medium Regulation — NERC CIP

The 2026 CIP picture: CIP-003-9 and CIP-012-2 in force, CIP-015 internal monitoring ahead

Two standards changed day-to-day practice this year. CIP-003-9 extends security management control expectations to low-impact BES cyber systems, with explicit attention to vendor remote access and to detecting malicious communications at low-impact assets. CIP-012-2 tightens protection of real-time operational data exchanged between control centres, including confidentiality and data handling rather than integrity and availability alone. Looking forward, CIP-015 introduces internal network security monitoring for high and medium impact BES cyber systems with external routable connectivity, and FERC has directed that its scope be extended. A further final rule approves CIP-003-11 modifications improving baseline security for low-impact systems through new password protocols for remote users and intrusion detection. The through-line is consistent: low-impact sites and vendor access are where the next audit attention lands, and internal monitoring is no longer optional at the high and medium tier.

Low Policy — Joint guidance

US and Australian agencies publish guidance on isolating OT and running that way for an extended period

A joint publication from US and Australian agencies sets out how critical infrastructure operators should prepare to isolate vital operational technology and supporting systems from external connectivity, and to keep operating in that isolated state for an extended period rather than for the few hours most continuity plans assume. It is guidance, not a requirement, and creates no compliance obligation. The value for a utility is that it turns an assumption most operators state confidently — that they can drop to manual or islanded operation if the corporate network is compromised — into a checklist that names the dependencies people forget: time synchronisation, licence servers, authentication, historian writes, remote vendor support and the physical availability of local operating staff. The water sector incidents this summer showed several utilities reverting to manual control successfully; the ones that struggled generally discovered a dependency nobody had documented.

Not applicable — guidance Not applicable
Not applicable — continuity planning Action: Take the isolation dependency list and walk it against one generating station or one control centre. Identify what breaks after 24 hours of no corporate connectivity, then after a week. Time sync, authentication and licence servers are the usual first failures, and none of them appears on a network diagram as a control-system dependency.
