Cyber-informed engineering argues the useful question is not how to keep attackers out, but which consequences you can design away

An analysis published 15 September sets out the case for cyber-informed engineering in critical infrastructure: that industrial systems operate under a constraint IT does not share, because a compromised network does not automatically have to become a catastrophic physical event. The argument is to move the security question upstream into design — identifying the physical consequences that actually matter, then engineering them out with controls that do not depend on the digital system remaining trustworthy. Protection schemes, interlocks and non-digital trip paths are security controls. This is practitioner analysis rather than incident reporting; it names no victim and describes no attack, but it is the most useful lens available for operators whose detection budget will never match their consequence exposure.

CISA will stop publishing its weekly Vulnerability Summary bulletin from 28 September, SecurityWeek reported on 17 September, directing users instead to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue, alerts and advisories. The agency frames the change as consistent with Binding Operational Directive 26-04 of June 2026 and its shift towards risk-based prioritisation. Nothing in the reporting indicates that ICS advisories are affected. The practical question for a utility is procedural rather than technical. The weekly summary is a common input to vulnerability-management routines and, in more than a few programmes, it is the documented patch source named in the evidence trail kept for NERC CIP-007 patch-management obligations. If a procedure of yours points at that bulletin, the source needs changing before the end of the month, and the change itself needs recording. KEV and the ICS advisory feed are the obvious replacements, with one caveat worth stating in the procedure: KEV is deliberately narrower, listing only what is known to be exploited, so a programme that swaps one for the other without adjusting its criteria will quietly stop tracking a great deal it used to see.

No patch applies — the work is updating your documented patch sources before 28 Sep 2026 Action: Search your vulnerability-management and CIP-007 procedures for any reference to the CISA weekly Vulnerability Summary and replace it before 28 September, recording the change through your normal document-control route so the evidence trail stays continuous. Name the replacement sources explicitly — the KEV catalogue, CISA ICS advisories and your vendor PSIRT feeds — and state in the procedure how a vulnerability that is not in KEV still gets assessed, because that is the gap the change creates. If any automation ingests the bulletin, find it now rather than discovering the feed has gone quiet in October. And check with your ICS teams that nobody is relying on the summary for OT vendor coverage; the ICS advisory feed is unaffected, but the habit may need redirecting.

Info Regulation & Policy — EU Cyber Resilience Act

ENISA switches on the Single Reporting Platform as the Cyber Resilience Act’s vulnerability reporting duties take effect

The EU Agency for Cybersecurity deployed the initial operating capability of its Single Reporting Platform, announced 14 September, giving manufacturers and open-source software stewards the channel they need to meet Cyber Resilience Act reporting obligations that took effect this month. Under the CRA, makers of products with digital elements must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents on a defined clock, with an early warning followed by a fuller notification. For a utility the CRA is mostly a procurement lever rather than a direct obligation: it is your relay, RTU, inverter and gateway vendors who now carry the reporting duty, and their disclosures are about to become more timely and more visible. Reported here factually as a regulatory milestone, without comment on the underlying policy debate.

Info Policy — Bulk-power system supply chain, comment period open

DOE has opened the comment window that decides what grid-equipment provenance will actually mean

The Department of Energy published a Request for Information in the Federal Register on 9 September implementing the executive order of 26 August that declared a national emergency over bulk-power-system security. The RFI asks industry for input on foreign ownership and control, equipment provenance, firmware and software integrity, and remote-access practices for grid-connected equipment, naming inverters, battery energy storage, uninterruptible power supplies, generators and industrial control systems. Comments are due 9 October 2026, and DOE held its public webinar on 16 September. This is the mechanism that turns August’s order into procurement and compliance obligations, which makes a four-week window the only inexpensive opportunity to shape what those obligations look like in practice. The substantive point for an operator is that the questions DOE is asking — who manufactured this, what firmware is on it, and who can reach it remotely — are ones most CIP-013 supply-chain programmes cannot currently answer for inverter-based resources and storage, because that equipment usually arrives through an EPC contract rather than being bought directly.

Info Regulation & Policy — EU, now in force

EU Cyber Resilience Act reporting went live on 11 September — 24 hours to tell ENISA about an exploited flaw

The vulnerability and incident reporting obligations of the EU Cyber Resilience Act took effect on 11 September 2026. Manufacturers of products with digital elements must now notify an actively exploited vulnerability or a severe security incident through ENISA’s Single Reporting Platform within 24 hours of becoming aware of it, follow up within 72 hours with detail on the exploit and mitigation status, and file a final report within 14 days of a fix becoming available. They must also inform affected users. The regime reaches manufacturers placing products on the EU market regardless of where they are based, with penalties running to €15 million at the top of the scale. For a utility the practical effect is on the supply side rather than the compliance side: relay, RTU, EMS/SCADA, inverter and DER-platform vendors selling into Europe are now on a 24-hour clock, so exploitation-confirmed disclosures about grid equipment should surface faster and in a more standardised form than the sector is used to.

Not applicable — obligations fall on product manufacturers, not on operators of the products Action: Two things are worth doing while this is new. First, expect the tempo of vendor disclosure to change and make sure you can absorb it: confirm that your protection, SCADA, inverter and DER vendors’ security advisories reach a named person on your side rather than a shared inbox, and that a 24-hour vendor notification does not sit unread over a weekend. Second, use it in procurement. Ask vendors selling into the EU how they will meet the Single Reporting Platform obligation and whether customers will be notified on the same clock as ENISA or afterwards, and get the answer written into contracts and support agreements at renewal. If your own organisation manufactures or rebadges any digital product sold into the EU — some utilities do, through metering, DER or services subsidiaries — confirm with legal whether the manufacturer obligations now apply to you directly.

Info Policy — Federal incident reporting

117 federal reporting rules across 27 agencies — and the first hours of an incident are when they collide

The McCrary Institute and the US Chamber of Commerce published analysis on 8 September, drawing on Government Accountability Office work, counting 117 separate federal cyber incident reporting regulations spread across 27 agencies, 48 of which apply to private industry. Their argument is operational rather than ideological: duplicative notifications with different thresholds, formats and clocks pull the same small group of people away from containment during the hours when containment is most effective. Electricity operators recognise the shape of this — a single event at a registered entity can trigger E-ISAC reporting, an OE-417 to the Department of Energy, NERC CIP-008 obligations, state utility commission notification, and, for entities with federal contracts or regulated data, more besides. The analysis lands as CIRCIA rules are expected to be finalised this autumn, which is the point at which harmonisation either happens or does not.

Not applicable — policy analysis, not a vulnerability or an incident No CVE
No patch applies — the exposure is response capacity during the first hours of an incident, not a technical defect Action: Build the reporting matrix before you need it. Write down every notification obligation that a single OT incident would trigger at your utility — E-ISAC, OE-417, CIP-008, state commission, cyber insurer, contractual customer notice — with the clock, the threshold and the named person who files each one. Then confirm that person is not also on the containment bridge, because that overlap is precisely what the analysis flags. Pre-draft the templates so the first filing is an editing job rather than a writing job, and rehearse the matrix in your next tabletop with the clock running. Track the CIRCIA final rule when it publishes this autumn and map it onto the matrix rather than bolting it on separately; if it changes your thresholds, your incident response plan needs the update at the same time.

Info Advisory — CISA/FBI guidance on third-party ICS integrator risk

CISA and the FBI tell critical-infrastructure operators that their ICS integrator may be the softest target in the chain

CISA and the FBI published joint guidance on 24 September, “Considerations for Critical Infrastructure Operators Working With Third-Party ICS Integrators,” urging operators to apply least privilege to integrator access, require on-demand rather than standing remote access, log and monitor every integrator session, and write cybersecurity, data-handling and change-management terms into contracts. The guidance grounds the warning in FBI technical analysis of a March–April 2025 intrusion at a US industrial-automation vendor that provided system integration, engineering consulting and SCADA programming — including to power utilities and transportation companies. Foreign actors who compromised the vendor staged nine archive files, roughly 800 items, containing customer network schematics, device configurations and SCADA/ICS details apparently for exfiltration; the agencies say that kind of data could support follow-on attacks on the vendor’s customers. No customer of the vendor is named as compromised, and no utility outage or OT manipulation resulted from the 2025 intrusion as far as public reporting shows. For a power operator the relevance is direct: engineering, protection-and-control and telecoms contractors routinely hold standing VPN access, SCADA program files and network diagrams for exactly the systems this dashboard tracks, and the guidance is a checklist for auditing that access before someone else finds it first.

Not a new exploit — the guidance cites a March–April 2025 intrusion at an integrator, confirmed by FBI technical analysis; no new 2026 compromise is claimed No CVE — this is guidance, not a vulnerability advisory
No patch applies — the fix is contract, access and monitoring controls Action: List every integrator, protection-and-control contractor and telecoms provider with standing access to engineering workstations, SCADA programming tools or network diagrams for your generation, transmission or substation systems. Convert standing VPN or remote-access accounts to on-demand access that requires your explicit authorization each session, and make sure every integrator session is logged and reviewed. Add or update contract language covering where the integrator stores your data, what happens to it at contract end, and your right to audit their security controls. Ask each one directly whether they were the vendor in the 2025 incident described in the advisory, or whether they have had a similar compromise; do not assume a “no” without evidence.
