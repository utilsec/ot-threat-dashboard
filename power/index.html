Cyber-informed engineering argues the useful question is not how to keep attackers out, but which consequences you can design away

An analysis published 15 September sets out the case for cyber-informed engineering in critical infrastructure: that industrial systems operate under a constraint IT does not share, because a compromised network does not automatically have to become a catastrophic physical event. Info Policy — NERC reliability standard, transmission physical security

FERC approves CIP-014-4: one 36-month cycle, jointly owned facilities in scope, and a 1,500-foot look around the fence

FERC has approved NERC reliability standard CIP-014-4, published in the Federal Register on 15 September under section 215 of the Federal Power Act and reported by Industrial Cyber the following day. The standard governs how transmission owners identify and assess the stations and substations whose loss could result in instability, uncontrolled separation or cascading. Four changes matter operationally. Applicability now extends to jointly owned transmission facilities. The previous 30-month and 60-month cycles are consolidated into a single 36-calendar-month review and assessment cycle, aligned with TPL-001 planning. Risk assessments must use steady-state and dynamic simulations across peak and off-peak load cases. And the assessment must consider stations and substations within 1,500 feet, about 457 metres, of the facility in question. The effective date is 1 October 2028, and FERC estimates roughly 344 transmission owners in scope at about ten hours a year each. It follows the 2022 substation attacks and FERC’s December 2022 directive to evaluate CIP-014-3. This is physical security rather than cyber — but the identification step is shared work, because the same station list drives CIP-002 classification, and a facility that changes status under one standard tends to change it under the other.

Info Guidance — Detection and response for critical infrastructure

CISA makes the case for decoys — and OT is the one place where adding a fake asset is easier than changing a real one

CISA published “Using Cyber Decoys to Strengthen Detection and Response” on 17 September. The guide defines decoys as assets that appear to be legitimate systems, accounts or data but exist to distract adversaries, detect their presence or support the collection of threat intelligence, and it structures deployment across three phases: preparation, execution and analysis. CISA positions the technique alongside zero trust as an incremental, low-disruption detection measure rather than a replacement for controls. It reads better in an OT context than in an IT one for a simple reason: the standard objection to new detection in a control environment is that touching a production system carries risk, and a decoy does not touch one. A fake historian, a spare engineering workstation that is not actually used, or a honeytoken credential seeded on a jump host changes nothing in the process. And because legitimate traffic inside a control network is narrow and predictable, a single interaction with a decoy is a far higher-quality alert than most of what a utility SOC currently spends its day triaging. The cost is maintenance: a decoy that stops matching the estate around it becomes obvious to an attacker and useless to you.

Info Regulation & Policy — EU, now in force

EU Cyber Resilience Act reporting went live on 11 September — 24 hours to tell ENISA about an exploited flaw

The vulnerability and incident reporting obligations of the EU Cyber Resilience Act took effect on 11 September 2026. Manufacturers of products with digital elements must now notify an actively exploited vulnerability or a severe security incident through ENISA’s Single Reporting Platform within 24 hours of becoming aware of it, follow up within 72 hours with detail on the exploit and mitigation status, and file a final report within 14 days of a fix becoming available. They must also inform affected users. The regime reaches manufacturers placing products on the EU market regardless of where they are based, with penalties running to €15 million at the top of the scale. For a utility the practical effect is on the supply side rather than the compliance side: relay, RTU, EMS/SCADA, inverter and DER-platform vendors selling into Europe are now on a 24-hour clock, so exploitation-confirmed disclosures about grid equipment should surface faster and in a more standardised form than the sector is used to.
