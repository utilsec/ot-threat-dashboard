Top Story

CISA and FBI: a compromised ICS integrator held utilities’ SCADA data — limit what yours can reach

CISA and the FBI released a joint fact sheet on 24 September, “Considerations for Critical Infrastructure Operators Working With Third-Party ICS Integrators” (dated 23 September). Its motivating case: in March–April 2025, foreign actors inside a US industrial automation company that does system integration and SCADA programming for customers including power utilities and transportation entities searched for “customers” and “SCADA” and staged nine archives of about 800 files — customer SCADA information, ICS device details and schematics — for exfiltration. The agencies do not name the company, the actor or its country, and do not say any utility was breached. Their advice: write security and supply-chain requirements into integrator contracts, apply least privilege, route remote access through monitored, logged and preferably on-demand paths, know what data integrators hold and where, and be able to operate if an integrator is compromised.

UAT-11988, a ransomware operator, logged in using the CVE-2026-20316 static credentials, and its later actions were “consistent with those of Qilin ransomware affiliates.” Cisco shipped a hardening release on 16 September. Critical Incident — Exploited management-server and VPN-gateway flaws, CISA KEV

Check Point management-server and VPN-gateway flaws were exploited before KEV listing; the federal deadline passed on 25 September

CISA added two Check Point flaws to KEV on 22 September. CVE-2026-93616 is a pre-authentication path traversal in Security Management Server, Multi-Domain Security Management, Log Server and SmartEvent that lets an attacker upload and run scripts; it was exploited as a zero-day on 23 July, and Check Point says a handful of customers were attacked. CVE-2026-85102 is an improper certificate-validation flaw that gives unauthenticated remote code execution on Security Gateway and Spark firewalls running site-to-site or remote-access VPN, with an exploitation wave from 12 September. For a utility these appliances often terminate the VPNs that contractors and integrators use to reach plant and substation networks. The same 22 September KEV batch added F5 BIG-IP APM CVE-2026-94127 (OAuth-profile configurations only) and Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator CVE-2026-93952; all four deadlines fell on 25 September. No power-sector victim has been named.

High Incident — Confirmed customer-data breach at a US electric & gas utility

CenterPoint Energy confirms a breach of about 7.5 million customer records; the actor threatens to target “infrastructure” next

CenterPoint Energy, which serves roughly 7 million electric and gas customers across Texas, Indiana, Minnesota and Ohio, told the SEC on 15 September that an unauthorized third party had obtained customer personal information through one of its externally facing systems. A threat actor had posted a 2.5 GB archive it claimed held about 7.5 million records on a cybercrime forum on 12 September, adding: “next time we won’t simply pull data, we’ll start attacking the main infrastructure.” CenterPoint says the incident has not affected electric or gas delivery and, based on its investigation so far, does not expect a material impact; no vulnerability, intrusion vector or actor identity has been disclosed. This reads as an IT-side data breach, not an OT compromise — but the actor’s own words, and CenterPoint’s history of prior IT intrusions (an access broker in 2024, exposure via the Cl0p MOVEit campaign), are reason enough for other utilities to check that customer-facing systems are properly segmented from anything that touches control-system networks.

High Threat Intelligence — Nation-state pre-positioning via a code server

Red Heron, assessed as PRC-linked, stole secrets and planted SSH keys in a renewable operator’s code server

Acronis reported on 13 September that an actor it tracks as Red Heron exploited CVE-2026-60004, a critical remote-code-execution flaw in self-hosted Gitea, to compromise 11 targets in five countries (Canada, the US, Taiwan, Argentina and Sri Lanka). The clearest power-sector case: a Canadian renewable-energy operator, where Acronis documented 22 exploitation sessions; the actor took source code, credentials and secrets, mapped internal Docker networks, and planted its own SSH keys for return access. Acronis holds the PRC-linked attribution at moderate confidence; nothing here shows hands-on manipulation of grid control, but the stolen credentials and network map are exactly what a follow-on intrusion would need. The campaign also exposed a previously undocumented Linux rootkit, SIXZUT. Gitea’s fix has been available since 27 July, and CISA’s KEV deadline passed a month ago; no new development was reported as of 27 September.

Medium Research — IT/OT/IoT/IoMT network convergence

Forescout study of 47,700 network segments: only 13% of those holding OT devices are OT-only

Forescout’s Vedere Labs published a 22 September analysis of 47,700 network segments containing more than 2.5 million devices across 209 organizations. Only 13% of segments containing OT devices were OT-only; the rest also held other types of device, and almost half mixed OT with both IT and IoT assets. Forescout also measured each sector’s average “blast radius” — devices per segment — at 9 for utilities, against 29 for manufacturing and 72 for oil and gas. Utilities fared better than most on that measure, but the core finding still applies: a mixed segment is what turns an IT-side credential theft, an exploited management console (see this edition’s Cisco and Check Point items) or a compromised integrator account into a path onto generation, substation or DER control networks, rather than a contained incident.

Critical Advisory — Confirmed exploited remote-access gateway zero-days, CISA KEV, patched

Citrix confirms two NetScaler zero-days were exploited before a fix existed; both are now in CISA KEV with a 30 September deadline

Citrix confirmed on 27 September, a day after watchTowr’s disclosure, that CVE-2026-88771 and CVE-2026-88772 (both CVSS 9.5) had been exploited against NetScaler ADC and Gateway before patches existed. CVE-2026-88771 is an improper-input-validation flaw giving unauthenticated remote code execution regardless of configuration; CVE-2026-88772 is a memory overflow reachable when DTLS is enabled, the default for VPN virtual servers. CISA added both to KEV the same day with a 30 September federal deadline. Citrix’s bulletin CTX697096 also fixes six other flaws (CVE-2026-88773 through -88778, CVSS 7.0–9.3) not reported as exploited. Fixed builds are 14.1-73.37 and 13.1-64.23 (and FIPS equivalents); no workaround exists. No actor has been named. NetScaler Gateway is a common remote-access front door into utility corporate networks and, from there, OT DMZs.

High Advisory — Exploited edge-router flaws, CISA KEV

CISA adds a third exploited MikroTik RouterOS flaw to KEV; paired with an earlier one it gives password-free admin access

CISA added CVE-2026-67279, an SSH workflow bypass in MikroTik RouterOS rated CVSS 6.9, to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 25 September with a 28 September federal deadline. It joins CVE-2026-86060 and CVE-2026-67277, added on 10 September. CERT Polska reported in-the-wild exploitation from about 2 September of a two-flaw SSH chain it calls “MikroTrick”; its 22 September technical analysis names the chain as CVE-2026-67279 combined with CVE-2026-86060, which gives an unauthenticated attacker full administrative control. Fixed releases are RouterOS 7.24.2, 7.23.4 and 6.49.21; the related CVE-2026-67278 needs 7.24.3 or 7.23.6. Shadowserver counted more than 122,500 MikroTik devices with SSH reachable from the internet in early September — an exposure count, not a count of compromised or unpatched devices. Editorial assessment: RouterOS is common on cellular and backhaul routers at small remote sites, which for a power operator can mean substations, solar and wind sites, and telemetry links.

Confirmed — exploitation confirmed by CISA KEV for all three CVEs and by CERT Polska for the MikroTrick chain; no actor named In CISA KEV — CVE-2026-86060 and CVE-2026-67277 (added 10 Sep 2026); CVE-2026-67279 (added 25 Sep 2026, federal deadline today, 28 Sep 2026); CVE-2026-67276 not KEV-listed
Patched — RouterOS 7.24.2, 7.23.4, 6.49.21 (7.24.3 or 7.23.6 for CVE-2026-67278) Action: Find every RouterOS device on your estate and your contractors’, including cellular gateways at substations and generation sites; upgrade to a fixed release and block SSH and WebFig from the internet. Check logs for CERT Polska’s indicators (“login failure for user -2 … via ssh”, users “added by ssh:-2@”, an unexpected privileged account named “ops”) and treat any device showing them as compromised.

Critical Advisory — Protection relay authentication bypass, no known exploitation

A predictable session ID lets an attacker bypass authentication on Siemens’ Reyrolle 7SR5 protection relay

Siemens disclosed CVE-2026-62645 on 8 September as part of that month’s ICS Patch Tuesday batch: a CVSS 9.8 missing-authentication flaw (CWE-306) in the Reyrolle 7SR5 protection relay’s web interface. The relay exposes information that lets an attacker calculate current and past session IDs, defeating the login entirely. All versions before V2.70 are affected. No in-the-wild exploitation or public exploit code has been reported. Protection relays like this trip breakers to protect equipment during faults; an attacker with unauthenticated access could, in principle, alter settings or block a legitimate trip, though doing so would require network reach to the relay first.

No known exploitation — no public exploit code Not in CISA KEV
Patched — Reyrolle 7SR5 V2.70 and later Action: Inventory Reyrolle 7SR5 relays and schedule the V2.70 upgrade at the next protection-system outage window; in the interim, confirm the relay’s web interface is not reachable from any network beyond the protection LAN.

Info Policy — EU Cyber Resilience Act incident-reporting obligations

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s incident-reporting clock started running on 11 September

As of 11 September, manufacturers of connected products sold into the EU — a list that includes most of the vendors behind generation, substation and DER equipment — must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe security incidents to ENISA’s new Single Reporting Platform within set deadlines, even for products already installed and still within their support window. The rule applies to embedded software, control systems and networking gear, not just finished consumer devices. For a US utility this mostly shows up indirectly, in tighter and more standardized vendor disclosure timelines, but multinational vendors and integrators with EU operations should confirm their own reporting processes are live.

Not an exploit advisory — a regulatory reporting obligation Not KEV-related
No patch applicable Action: If you operate or procure from vendors with EU manufacturing or sales operations, confirm they have a live CRA reporting process, and watch for more granular, faster vendor disclosure timelines as the obligation beds in.
