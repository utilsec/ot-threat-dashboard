Top Story

Attackers crossed from a wind farm into an unrelated Polish CHP plant over a private cellular APN — and stopped its steam turbine

CERT Polska disclosed on 8 August that a December 2025 intrusion at a small Polish combined heat and power plant, which supplies heat to roughly 50,000 residents, was reached through a private cellular APN. It is the first observed case of that path into an OT network. Because the distribution operator’s private APN allowed arbitrary devices on it to talk to one another, the attacker pivoted from a compromised wind-farm network to a controller at an unrelated CHP site and shut down the steam turbine and the water treatment system. Staff did not recognise it as an attack for months, assuming contractor error during concurrent maintenance. For utilities the lesson is uncomfortable: private APNs, widely recommended as a safer alternative to internet exposure, isolate a site only as well as their peer-to-peer rules do.

Confirmed OT-disruptive incident — turbine and water treatment stopped No CVE exploited — network-path and configuration failure
No patch — architectural: APN peer isolation and OT segmentation Action: Ask your cellular provider and your DSO for the peer-to-peer policy on every private APN your substations, wind or solar sites and plants use, and require device-to-device isolation by default. Then confirm your OT firewall treats the APN as an untrusted network, not an internal one.

Attack Path The “safer than the internet” network turned out to be a shared network

Private cellular APNs are recommended precisely so field sites avoid public internet exposure. CERT Polska’s report shows that when the APN permits device-to-device traffic, every site on it becomes a potential entry point for every other site — including sites owned by different companies. The sector conversation this week has been less about the specific attacker and more about how many operators have ever asked their carrier what the peer policy on their APN actually is.

High Incident — Municipal utilities

The Iranian-affiliated PLC campaign is not water-only — energy and municipal gas systems are named in scope

Joint advisory AA26-097A, issued in April by the FBI, CISA, NSA, EPA, DOE, Cyber National Mission Force and Treasury and updated on 22 July, describes Iranian-affiliated actors reaching internet-exposed PLCs across multiple US critical infrastructure sectors — water and wastewater, energy and government or municipal environments. The activity continues: utilities in at least 12 states have reported incidents since late July, and in Childersburg, Alabama the targeted operator runs water, sewer and gas together. Combined municipal utilities are the exposure most power-sector readers should check first, because a single small operations team often shares remote access, credentials and cellular routers across all three services. The July update also expanded observed vendor scope beyond Rockwell to Schneider Electric and Siemens PLCs.

Confirmed active campaign — operator lockout and operational disruption reported No CVE exploited — internet-exposed controllers with default, shared or absent credentials

Medium Research — Ransomware

Dragos counted 1,140 industrial ransomware incidents in Q2 — and no operator touched a control system

Dragos recorded 1,140 ransomware incidents at industrial organisations in the second quarter of 2026, up 12 percent from 1,020 in Q1, with North America accounting for 514. Manufacturing absorbed 747 of them; organisations that support ICS environments — integrators, engineering firms, service providers — took another 117. The finding that matters for generation and transmission is what did not happen: Dragos observed no ransomware operator directly manipulating an industrial control system during the quarter. Disruption came from encrypted enterprise IT and from precautionary shutdowns of virtualisation and shared infrastructure. Your outage risk from ransomware is therefore mostly a question of how cleanly you can run the plant with the business network dark, and how many OT dependencies quietly live on shared hypervisors.

High Threat Intelligence — Ransomware

Gunra ransomware is walking in through unpatched VPNs and firewalls, and utilities are named in scope

The FBI, CISA, DoD Cyber Crime Center, NSA, US Secret Service and the Republic of Korea’s National Police Agency issued #StopRansomware advisory AA26-222A on Gunra, a double-extortion operation derived from leaked Conti source that moved to a structured affiliate model in early 2026. Its victim base explicitly includes utilities, manufacturing and transport. The initial access is not novel: affiliates are exploiting known authentication-bypass flaws in Fortinet FortiOS and FortiProxy — CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 — along with exposed RDP and other edge infrastructure. For a utility, the practical read is that the box most likely to start your next incident is the remote-access appliance in front of the corporate network, not anything inside the electronic security perimeter.

High Threat Intelligence — Virtualisation

A suspected China-nexus actor is mass-exploiting a patched vCenter flaw — the layer many OT DMZs sit on

Researchers have tied exploitation of CVE-2026-59310, a directory-traversal flaw in Broadcom VMware vCenter rated CVSS 9.8 and patched on 29 July, to a suspected China-nexus actor. German incident-response firm QUIRSO tracked roughly 361 unique victim IP addresses across 47 countries, with 343 appearing within the first three days of August; it assesses with moderate confidence that the operator is Chinese-speaking. The attack chain establishes persistence through cron, systemd and the reverse_ssh tool, drops a backdoor, and in some cases deploys Babuk-derived ransomware. Utilities should care because historians, HMIs, jump hosts and OT DMZ services are routinely virtualised: root on vCenter is root on the hypervisor carrying those workloads, and that is precisely the precautionary-shutdown scenario Dragos described.

Actively exploited at scale — ~361 victim IPs across 47 countries Not listed in CISA KEV at generation time
Patch available — Broadcom fix released 29 Jul 2026 Action: Patch vCenter now if you have not, and treat any unpatched instance as compromised: hunt for unexpected cron and systemd units, reverse_ssh binaries and new SSH keys. Separately, confirm whether any OT-critical virtual machine shares a cluster with enterprise workloads — if so, plan the split.

Critical Advisory — HMI gateway

Haiwell IoT Cloud HMI Gateway carries an unauthenticated root command-injection flaw rated CVSS 10.0

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-02 covers CVE-2026-19188, an OS command-injection vulnerability in the Haiwell IoT Cloud HMI Gateway rated CVSS 10.0. The Net Check feature reachable through the /setting endpoint fails to sanitise input passed to the cmdPing Socket.io event, so an attacker can execute arbitrary operating-system commands as root. CISA lists Energy, Critical Manufacturing and Water and Wastewater among the affected sectors. Haiwell has published fixed firmware. These low-cost HMI and cloud-gateway products turn up in small generation sites, balance-of-plant systems and contractor-installed remote monitoring far more often than asset registers suggest, and they are frequently the device someone put on a cellular router so it could be checked from a phone.

Info Regulation — NERC CIP

CIP-015-1 internal network security monitoring: the first compliance date is 1 October 2028

CIP-015-1, the NERC reliability standard requiring internal network security monitoring inside the electronic security perimeter, is in force with staged compliance dates. High- and medium-impact BES Cyber Systems with external routable connectivity must implement INSM by 1 October 2028; all other BES Cyber Systems with ERC have until 1 October 2030. The requirements cover collecting network data feeds, detecting anomalous activity, evaluating what is detected, protecting the monitoring data itself and retaining investigation records. FERC has also directed NERC to extend the standard’s scope to EACMS and PACS outside the ESP. The Polish CHP case is a fair argument for not waiting: the plant lacked the internal visibility to distinguish an intrusion from a maintenance error for three months.

Info Regulation — EU

EU Cyber Resilience Act reporting obligations begin in September 2026 for product manufacturers

From September 2026, manufacturers covered by the EU Cyber Resilience Act must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and serious security incidents in their products. For European energy operators this is a supply-side change rather than a new obligation of their own, but it should improve the quality and timeliness of disclosure from equipment vendors. It stacks on top of NIS2, under which electricity generation, transmission and distribution, district heating, gas and hydrogen are essential entities, and on the sector-specific Network Code on Cybersecurity (EU 2024/1366), which adds critical-impact classification, recurring union-wide risk assessments and cross-border crisis-management duties. The NIS2 Cooperation Group adopted common incident-reporting templates on 26 May 2026.
