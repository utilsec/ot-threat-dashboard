Critical Incident — Lateral movement between unrelated operators

CERT Polska: an attacker crossed a shared private cellular network from a wind farm into a heating plant and stopped its turbine

CERT Polska’s follow-up report describes an intrusion that began at a wind farm and ended at an unrelated combined heat and power plant, with the turbine taken out of service. The bridge was a private APN both operators bought from the same carrier. A private APN is a billing and addressing construct, not a security boundary: if devices on it can address one another, one operator’s compromised cellular router becomes another operator’s ingress path. For anyone running distributed generation over carrier links — wind, solar, storage, district energy, remote substations — this is the clearest published example of an attack path most operators have never asked their carrier about.

Confirmed — CERT Polska documents intrusion and a turbine taken out of service; incident occurred 29 Dec 2025, disclosed 8 Aug 2026 No CVE — the finding is a network design and carrier-isolation failure, not a product vulnerability
No patch applies — remediation is carrier routing policy, per-site isolation and device-level firewalling Action: Write to your carrier account team and ask, in writing, three questions: is our APN shared with other subscribers, can devices on it route to one another, and what isolation option is available. Assume the answer is unfavourable until you have it in writing. In the meantime, treat every cellular-attached controller and RTU as internet-exposed — put a firewall or VPN concentrator you control between the modem and the process network, disable inbound management on the modem, and enforce per-site credentials rather than a fleet password. Medium Threat Intelligence — Scenario modelling

GRIDLOCK: modelling puts a 92% chance of a major attack on UK battery storage by 2031 — and 29% of the fleet is enough

Centrii published its GRIDLOCK report on 3 September, modelling what happens when an adversary uses grid-connected battery storage as the weapon rather than the target. In the scenario, compromised BESS units are commanded to swing output in concert, manipulating physical power flows to destabilise system frequency rather than saturating a network link — the report frames it as a grid-scale denial of service delivered in energy. Across 10,000 Monte Carlo runs against three security postures, Centrii puts the probability of a major attack on UK battery storage by 2031 at 92%, and calculates that compromising roughly 29% of national capacity — on the order of 400 units — could be enough to trigger a nationwide outage affecting 67 million people, with costs modelled up to £10 billion. Mandatory certification and regular attack-readiness drills cut that probability to 61% and push the earliest likely window from 2027 to 2028. A parallel run on Texas returns the same 92% figure by 2031, with economic damage modelled up to $65 billion. This is modelling rather than a reported incident, and its value is in the premise it tests: a large amount of fast-acting capacity dispatched from a small number of control planes.

No incident reported — GRIDLOCK is a modelled scenario built from Monte Carlo simulation, not an observed attack Not a CVE — the finding concerns aggregated dispatch authority, not a product flaw
No patch applies — the mitigations modelled are certification, readiness drills and constraints on fleet-wide dispatch Action: Treat this as a tabletop prompt, not a patch cycle. Establish how much of the fast-frequency-response capacity in your balancing area sits behind a single optimiser or manufacturer cloud platform — if you are a system operator, ask your connected storage counterparties directly; if you own assets, answer it for your own fleet. Then test the specific failure the report models: if several hundred megawatts of storage swung the wrong way at once, how quickly would your control room tell that apart from a market-driven response, and what limits at the site controller would blunt it regardless of what the cloud commanded? Push for rate-of-change limits enforced locally at the asset rather than centrally, and add a coordinated-BESS scenario to your exercise programme this planning cycle rather than next.

Info Research — Isolation readiness, practitioner guidance

Six steps to being able to disconnect — and a planning horizon of three months running islanded

Nozomi Networks published a practitioner summary on 2 September of the Five Eyes “CI Fortify” guidance released in late July, reducing it to six steps: identify vital systems and their dependencies; establish trust levels and connection maps; pre-build the separation points; create and test graduated isolation plans; retain visibility while isolated; and run post-isolation checks before reconnecting. The step that changes the engineering is the second-to-last one — keeping monitoring alive after you cut the link, since an isolation plan that also blinds the control room is not one anyone will execute. The addition worth arguing about internally is the planning horizon: Nozomi suggests operators assume they may need to sustain isolated operations for up to three months. For generation and transmission that pushes the question past network design and into spares, licence servers, time synchronisation and how long an EMS can run without its enterprise dependencies.

Confirmed — Nozomi Networks analysis published 2 Sep 2026, summarising the Five Eyes CI Fortify guidance released 28 Jul 2026 No CVE — this is preparedness guidance, not a product vulnerability
No patch applies — the work is dependency mapping, pre-built separation points and tested isolation plans Action: Do the first two steps properly and the rest follow. Take your EMS, generation control and substation automation environments and write down, per system, what it depends on that lives outside the control network — Active Directory, DNS, NTP, licence servers, historian replication, vendor remote support. That list is the isolation plan; anything on it either gets a local equivalent or becomes a known casualty. Then pre-build the separation points: a documented, tested way to drop external connectivity that does not require an engineer to improvise at a firewall under pressure. Test at least one graduated tier this quarter — cutting vendor remote access while everything else keeps running is the achievable version and the one you are most likely to need.

Medium Threat Intelligence — Analysis

A hostile battery dispatch looks identical to a profitable one — and very few control planes can order it

Help Net Security published an analysis on 2 September, built on modelling from Rafael Narezzi of the London firm Centrii, arguing that grid-connected battery energy storage has concentrated an unusual amount of fast-acting power behind a small number of cloud control planes. Batteries earn revenue by responding to frequency in under a second, so on a control room screen a hostile coordinated swing and a legitimate revenue-driven one look the same — the frequency trace alone tells the operator nothing about intent. Control is also pooled: third-party optimisers and manufacturer cloud platforms dispatch fleets spread across many sites and several balancing areas, which means the meaningful unit of risk is the platform, not the individual site. This is analysis and modelling rather than a reported incident, and it is worth reading as a design question about who can issue a fleet-wide setpoint and what would notice if someone else did.

No incident reported — this is published modelling and analysis, not an observed attack Not a CVE
No patch applies — the finding is architectural: aggregated dispatch authority with limited independent verification Action: Write down, for each storage asset you own or offtake from, who can issue a dispatch or setpoint change, through which platform, and what authentication stands in front of it. Include the optimiser and any manufacturer cloud service, not just your own EMS. Then ask the harder question: what independent signal would tell your control room that a dispatch was not commanded by an authorised party? If the honest answer is none, that is the gap — candidate answers include an out-of-band confirmation path for fleet-wide commands, rate-of-change limits enforced locally at the site controller rather than in the cloud, and alerting on dispatch patterns that deviate from the market signal. High Research — AI-assisted exploit porting against PLCs

Researchers used an AI model to port a working PLC exploit to a second controller — then bricked it

Forescout’s Vedere Labs set a large language model the task of taking a working remote-code-execution exploit for one WAGO controller — the 750-852, via CVE-2021-31886, a pre-authentication buffer overflow in its Nucleus FTP server — and re-targeting it at a related model, the 750-831. Critical Threat intel — Joint federal advisory on ICS protocol tooling

Five agencies warn AI-written scripts are reading Siemens S7 controllers across the energy sector

Joint advisory AA26-231A from NSA, CISA, FBI, DOE and EPA describes actors using AI assistance to turn a well-documented open-source S7 library into custom tooling that queries internet-exposed Siemens S7 controllers and blends in as legitimate monitoring traffic. Medium Guidance — Internet exposure reduction for IT, OT and ICS

CISA publishes internet exposure reduction guidance — and the target list for this sector is unmanned substations, distributed generation and storage sites, and the cellular-linked RTUs and gateways that sit behind them

CISA released guidance on 26 August directing organisations to identify which of their systems are reachable from the internet, remove remote access that is not required, and harden whatever must stay externally accessible. It is deliberately unglamorous: an inventory step, a removal step and a hardening step, published in the same week the UK NCSC warned that internet-exposed OT and edge devices are being targeted with real-world disruption already caused. Grid operators carry the widest population of remotely sited, thinly monitored equipment of any sector. The guidance is aimed squarely at the pattern that produced this month’s incidents: a controller or gateway reachable from the public internet because a carrier link, a vendor support tunnel or a decommissioned project left it that way. The guidance carries no deadline and applies to no one by force, which is precisely why it tends to be filed rather than executed.

High Policy — Supply chain, bulk-power system equipment

A new executive order puts foreign-made grid equipment — including RTUs and control systems — under federal review

Executive Order 14420, signed 26 August, prohibits the acquisition, importation, transfer or installation of certain foreign-produced bulk-power system electric equipment and directs the Department of Energy to restrict, condition or unwind equipment it judges to carry sabotage, cyber, supply-disruption or catastrophic-infrastructure risk. The order is grounded in a declared national emergency over the possibility of digital backdoors built into foreign-manufactured equipment that would permit remote access and sabotage. What makes it operationally significant rather than purely political is the scope: alongside transformers, inverters and energy storage, it names industrial control systems explicitly, including remote terminal units. Countries named in reporting on the order include China, Russia, Iran and North Korea. Implementation detail, including how existing installed equipment will be treated, sits with DOE and has not yet been published.

Confirmed — executive order signed and published 26 Aug 2026; implementing rules and covered-equipment criteria to follow from the Department of Energy No CVE — this is a supply-chain and procurement control, not a product vulnerability
No patch applies — the action required is asset provenance and procurement review Action: Start with provenance, because you will be asked for it. Build a list of bulk-power system assets by manufacturer and country of manufacture — transformers, inverters, storage systems, RTUs and the control systems around them — and include the firmware and remote-support arrangements, since that is where a backdoor concern actually lives. For anything with vendor remote access, confirm the path is brokered, logged and revocable by you rather than always-on. Flag pending purchase orders and in-flight projects to procurement now, before DOE publishes criteria, so a covered item is not discovered on a loading dock. Keep the analysis factual and vendor-specific; the order sets direction, and the enforceable detail is still to come.

Info Policy — NERC CIP compliance calendar

The 2026 CIP picture: CIP-003-9 and CIP-012-2 now in force, CIP-015 internal monitoring ahead

Three dates define this year for registered entities. CIP-003-9 became enforceable on 1 April 2026, extending governance expectations to low-impact BES cyber systems with particular attention to vendor remote access and supply chain. CIP-012-2 took effect on 1 July 2026, tightening protection of real-time operational data exchanged between control centres. CIP-015-1 has been effective since September 2025 with phased compliance running to 2030, and introduces internal network security monitoring — east-west visibility inside the electronic security perimeter rather than only at its edge. NERC’s January 2026 CIP roadmap signals where the standards go next, including cloud, telecommunications dependency and inverter-based resources.
