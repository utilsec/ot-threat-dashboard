Critical Incident — Exploited remote-access appliance zero-days, CISA KEV; fourth critical flaw patched

A fourth critical Citrix NetScaler flaw means some appliances patched for the exploited zero-days must be upgraded again

Citrix published bulletin CTX697191 on 8 October for CVE-2026-107406 (CVSS v4.0 9.5), a memory overflow in NetScaler ADC and Gateway configured as a SAML service provider or identity provider that Citrix says may lead to code execution or denial of service. The builds that fixed the September zero-days stay exposed when the appliance acts as a SAML identity provider, so those customers must upgrade again; there is no workaround. Citrix says it is not aware of exploitation of the new flaw, and it is not in KEV. It lands in the middle of an active campaign: CVE-2026-88771 and CVE-2026-88772 (both CVSS v4 9.5) were exploited before patches existed and entered KEV on 27 September, and CVE-2026-88779, a denial-of-service flaw according to Citrix and watchTowr, followed on 4 October. CISA has warned that attackers use it to force reboots so that code planted through CVE-2026-88771 runs, and has published Sigma detection rules. Mandiant counts dozens of victim organizations and calls the actors suspected state-nexus without naming a group; Arctic Wolf counts at least 78 targeted organizations, and Shadowserver sees more than 20,000 exposed or potentially vulnerable appliances. Intruders planted web shells, and Mandiant warns that upgrading alone does not remove access already gained. No power-sector victim has been named, but NetScaler often fronts contractor and field-staff access at utilities, so an unhunted appliance is a possible first hop toward EMS, SCADA and substation networks (editorial assessment).

Cisco disclosed CVE-2026-76504 (CVSS 9.8) on 30 September: Catalyst SD-WAN Manager mishandles URI encoding, so a crafted HTTP request can bypass an API authentication rule and give a remote, unauthenticated attacker administrative access. Cisco says its PSIRT became aware of active exploitation in September, and CISA added the flaw to KEV the same day with a 3 October federal deadline, now passed. Three clusters, including an APT and a ransomware operator, are exploiting Cisco firewall-manager flaws CVE-2026-20079 and CVE-2026-20316

High Incident — Active credential-theft campaign against firewall and VPN gateways; no CVE

FBI and Secret Service: FortiBleed credential theft against FortiGate VPNs is still active, and energy is among the targeted sectors

The FBI and US Secret Service warned on 6 October that the FortiBleed campaign against internet-facing Fortinet FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways is still active. There is no CVE: the agencies say attackers exploit reused or leaked credentials and legacy SHA-256 password storage, and researchers describe credential stuffing and spraying, offline hash cracking and a sniffer that captures authentication traffic. Intruders create their own administrator accounts and sometimes delete or reset the real ones, locking owners out, and the access is resold; the FBI advisory links it to Payload ransomware, and SOCRadar says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments from it. SOCRadar’s figure of 86,644 working device credentials across 194 countries dates from 19 June; it is not a new count. The agencies say targets span all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors, including energy. No compromise of an OT system has been described, but FortiGates commonly sit at the IT/OT boundary and in front of remote access to plants and substations (editorial assessment).

Medium Incident — Exploited remote-support zero-day, suspected state espionage

A suspected PRC-nexus espionage actor exploited a BeyondTrust Remote Support zero-day from late August, Google says

Google Threat Intelligence Group said on 15 October that a zero-day in BeyondTrust Remote Support, CVE-2026-102764 (CVSS v4.0 9.3), was exploited from late August, and attributes the activity to ARMORY CASTLE, a suspected PRC-nexus espionage actor formerly tracked as UNC5221, based on overlapping tools and techniques. The attackers gained root on Remote Support appliances, harvested credentials, deleted logs and used the product’s file-transfer feature to pull documents from users’ desktops, deploying the SAWFLY web shell and the BARECLAW tunneler. Versions up to 26.2.3 are affected. BeyondTrust identified the defect in late September, shipped patch BT26-05-RS on 8 October and had patched its SaaS customers by 9 October; self-hosted appliances without automatic updates must be patched by hand, and releases older than 24.3.1 must be upgraded first. The flaw was not in KEV as of CISA’s 15 October catalog, and GTIG names no victims or sectors. Remote-support tools like this are how many OEMs, integrators and help desks reach plant workstations, so the relevance to power operators is the vendor-access path (editorial assessment).

High Threat Intelligence — Joint CISA/FBI guidance drawn from a compromised ICS integrator

CISA and FBI: a compromised ICS integrator held utilities’ SCADA data — limit what yours can reach

CISA and the FBI released a joint fact sheet on 24 September, “Considerations for Critical Infrastructure Operators Working With Third-Party ICS Integrators.” Its motivating case: in March–April 2025, foreign actors inside a US industrial automation company that does system integration and SCADA programming for customers including power utilities and transportation entities searched for “customers” and “SCADA” and staged nine archives of about 800 files — customer SCADA information, ICS device details and schematics — for exfiltration. The agencies do not name the company, the actor or its country, and do not say any utility was breached. Their advice: write security and supply-chain requirements into integrator contracts, apply least privilege, route remote access through monitored, on-demand channels, know what data integrators hold and where, and be able to operate if an integrator is compromised.

Critical Advisory — Synchrophasor data concentrator and historian, unauthenticated remote code execution, no known exploitation

Critical unauthenticated code-execution flaw in GPA’s openPDC and openHistorian grid-data software; fixes are out

CISA advisory ICSA-26-281-02, published 8 October, covers the Grid Protection Alliance’s open-source openPDC phasor data concentrator and openHistorian time-series historian. The most serious flaw, CVE-2026-100730 (CVSS v3.1 9.8, v4.0 9.3), is a deserialization bug in the service console: where Windows Authentication is not in use, an unauthenticated attacker on the network can reach it and run code as the service account. Other flaws in the advisory leave data publishers without authentication in default configurations, exposing topology information and STTP data exchange: CVE-2026-105281 (v3.1 7.5) and CVE-2026-85479 (v3.1 5.3). openPDC 2.9.482 and openHistorian 2.8.585 fix them; the Docker image is not fixed. New installs bind publishers to the loopback interface, but upgraded installs keep their old binding and must be checked by hand. CISA reports no known exploitation. These servers sit between substation phasor measurements and control-center analytics, so a compromised one is a foothold between networks (editorial assessment).

Medium Regulation — FERC staff report on lessons learned from FY2026 CIP audits

FERC staff CIP audit lessons: late-registered new generation, aggregated low-impact control and AI tools inside CIP networks

On 13 October FERC’s Office of Electric Reliability published a staff report on lessons from its fiscal-2026 CIP audits. Most cybersecurity processes reviewed met the mandatory standards, but auditors found three recurring gaps. Some owners registered new generation late, or had not categorized BES Cyber Systems under CIP-002-5.1a, including inverter-based resources already synchronized and above the threshold; staff traces this to misreading the Commercial Operation Date, and one entity delayed registration for more than a year. In two scenarios, low-impact systems controlled by a non-registered parent over affiliated operators, or by one contractor serving several unaffiliated operators, met medium-impact criteria once their combined control was counted. And entities are bringing what the report calls super intelligence (SI) tools, such as chatbots, assistants and automated analytics, into CIP-networked environments; most had applied controls, but practices varied, and staff warns such tools can open unexpected lateral-movement or access paths. The report is non-binding; it suggests NERC consider more guidance or changes to standards or registration.

Not an exploit advisory — a non-binding FERC staff report drawn from anonymized audits Not KEV-related
No patch applicable — compliance and governance review Action: Check that new inverter-based and other generation was registered and categorized on time against the Commercial Operation Date; re-test impact categorization wherever an affiliate or contractor monitors or controls several sites; and inventory SI tools with access inside CIP boundaries, applying CIP-004 access authorization and review, CIP-007 inventory, CIP-011 limits on BES Cyber System Information and CIP-013 vendor assessment, as the report recommends, alongside the NIST AI RMF.

Info Policy — Federal cyber incident-reporting rule under White House review

CISA’s CIRCIA incident-reporting final rule is under White House review; reporting duties start only once it is published

The Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs logged receipt of CISA’s final Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act rule (RIN 1670-AA04) on 1 October, starting the review that comes before publication. The rule text is not public, so its final scope and definitions are not yet known. The statute calls for reporting covered cyber incidents within 72 hours and ransom payments within 24 hours, but those duties begin only on dates the final rule sets. The statutory deadline passed in October 2025 and CISA’s later targets slipped; HIPAA Journal reports publication is expected before year-end. Power-sector participants in a GAO roundtable released 28 September already cited overlapping reporting rules, including NERC CIP, and every participant named the forthcoming CIRCIA rule as a concern.
