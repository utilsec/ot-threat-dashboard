High Incident — Active credential-theft campaign against boundary firewalls, agency-confirmed, all critical-infrastructure sectors

The FBI and Secret Service say FortiBleed credential theft against FortiGate firewalls is still running — across every critical-infrastructure sector, with no CVE to patch

The FBI and the US Secret Service issued a joint advisory on Tuesday 6 October warning that the FortiBleed campaign against internet-facing Fortinet FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways remains an active threat. It is not a software vulnerability and has no CVE: per the agencies, it exploits reused or leaked credentials and legacy SHA-256 password storage. Researchers describe credential stuffing and spraying from leak dumps and infostealer logs, offline cracking of extracted password hashes on a GPU cluster, and a Go-based sniffer that passively captures authentication traffic across 24 protocols. Some owners were locked out after the attackers created their own administrator accounts and deleted or reset the legitimate ones. The agencies say compromised firewalls have served as ransomware entry points, and the FBI advisory links the access to Payload ransomware. Cybersecurity Dive reports that targets span all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors — a list that by definition includes transportation systems — naming energy, communications, and food and agriculture among them. The most-cited scale figure, more than 86,644 working device credentials across 194 countries, is SOCRadar’s count as of 19 June, not a new tally; SOCRadar says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments stemming from the access and describes the operator as a Russian-speaking access broker, which is a researcher assessment, not agency attribution. No OT system compromise and no rail victim has been described. The rail relevance is our editorial assessment: FortiGate is one of the most common firewalls at the IT/OT boundary of railroads and transit agencies, and a stolen administrator credential there is a way to rewrite segmentation without exploiting anything.

High Incident — Food & retail sector OT attack in France; carried here as a cross-sector lesson, not a rail incident

Attackers took over web-managed refrigeration controllers at about 700 French supermarkets — a food-sector attack, and a direct lesson for contractor-managed station and depot plant

From the night of 11–12 October, attackers remotely took control of internet-reachable refrigeration management controllers in French supermarkets and forced them into continuous or extended defrost, with setpoints of up to about 50 °C, spoiling stock; the Paris prosecutor reports outbreaks of fire at some sites. A second wave on the night of 12–13 October hit stores with Dixell controllers. Finance Minister Roland Lescure told Parliament on 13 October that about 700 of France’s roughly 10,000 supermarkets were affected, and Carrefour, Coopérative U, E.Leclerc, Intermarché/Netto and Monoprix have confirmed impact. CERT-FR’s alert CERTFR-2026-ALE-012 named Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC controllers on 12 October, added Copeland and Dixell xweb on 13 October, and in its 16 October update says attack campaigns are probably still ongoing. As of 23 October CERT-FR had issued no further version, and no new attack nights had been reported since 13 October. Danfoss and Copeland say most affected units are older, end-of-life models. No group has claimed the attack, no ransom has been demanded and the entry route has not been established; the national cyber agency ANSSI has mobilised, and the Paris prosecutor’s cybercrime section has opened an inquiry. Reports of targeting in other European countries come from one security firm, Sekoia, and have not been confirmed by any foreign authority. Separately, Claroty’s Team82 showed on 13 October that an AI coding agent, steered by a researcher, reproduced a previously disclosed Danfoss exploit chain to root in about 28 minutes; it does not say those flaws were used in France. This is not a rail incident and it does not count toward this page’s threat level. It is here as our editorial lesson: stations, depots and tunnels run the same kind of contractor-supported, web-managed plant — HVAC, ventilation, pumping, lighting — and this attack needed little more than reachability and outdated controllers.

High Threat Intelligence & Research — Mass exploitation of an enterprise application, transportation among sectors hit

Mandiant says ShinyHunters is back exploiting Oracle PeopleSoft by encoding one letter past WAF rules — transportation is among the sectors with compromised systems

Google Threat Intelligence and Mandiant reported on 25 September a renewed mass-exploitation campaign by UNC6240, the group known as ShinyHunters, against CVE-2026-35273, an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw in the PeopleSoft Environment Management Hub (the /PSEMHUB/ path), rated CVSS v3.1 9.8. The flaw was first exploited as a zero-day against academic institutions in late May and early June, and Oracle has had a fix out since June. The new wave requests /%50SEMHUB/ instead: WAF rules that match the literal path miss it, and WebLogic decodes and routes the request anyway, reaching, in Mandiant’s words, “systems whose operators may have believed their WAF rules had mitigated the exposure”. Mandiant counts “dozens of systems” compromised globally across higher education, technology and IT services, healthcare, agriculture, transportation and government, names no victims, and says it has notified more than 100 organisations whose addresses matched vulnerable endpoints. The tooling includes JSP web shells, Neo-reGeorg tunnels, a SIDEEYE backdoor delivered in a trojanised Light Alloy media-player installer, and MeshAgent. CISA has listed CVE-2026-35273 in the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue since 12 June 2026 and marks it as known to be used in ransomware campaigns. Since then the FBI has said, on 6 October, that a breach of its own jobs portal resulted from a contractor that failed to apply a patch explicitly issued for the platform — which Reuters, citing unnamed sources, identified as Oracle PeopleSoft — and on 9 October it announced another arrest of a suspected ShinyHunters member, saying the group has breached more than 140 organisations. Oracle’s next quarterly Critical Patch Update is due on 20 October. The rail relevance is our editorial assessment: many transit authorities and railroads run PeopleSoft for HR, payroll and finance, and a web shell on that tier puts employee records and whatever directory and database credentials the application holds within an attacker’s reach. It is an IT-side risk, not an OT one.

Info Threat Intelligence & Research — Vendor incident-response observation and cleanup guidance

Dragos sees old worms — Sality, Conficker, WannaCry — turning up more often in OT incident calls, and warns the wrong cleanup can hurt more than the infection

Dragos’s Lesley Carhart and Jan Hoff wrote on 15 October that requests for help with legacy commodity malware such as Sality, Gammima, WannaCry and Conficker have risen sharply in recent years, and that many OT networks Dragos is called into carry between 20 and 70 distinct commodity-malware variants across several subnets, often on systems older than Windows XP Service Pack 2. They attribute the rise to more networking and remote access, better visibility, and regulation that expects critical-infrastructure networks to be clean. Their answer is a “Now / Next / Never” framework, with a white paper, for deciding what to clean immediately, what to schedule and what to leave contained, and a warning that the wrong cleanup can be riskier than the infection itself. No actor or campaign is involved. CISA and the FBI say an automation integrator serving power and transportation customers lost about 800 files of their SCADA data — your signalling contractor holds the same kind of files

The FBI and CISA publicised a joint fact sheet on 24 September (the document itself is dated 23 September), “Considerations for Critical Infrastructure Operators Working With Third-Party ICS Integrators”. It is built around a 2025 case: in March and April of that year, foreign cyber actors accessed a US industrial automation solutions company that provided system integration, engineering consulting and SCADA programming services to power utilities and transportation entities, and packaged customer SCADA information, ICS device details and schematics — roughly 800 files — into nine archive files. The agencies do not name the integrator, its customers or the actor, and describe no follow-on disruption. The core recommendation is the principle of least privilege for every integrator connection, with secure, operator-controlled remote access, an inventory of the data and hardware each integrator can reach, minimal internet exposure, cybersecurity requirements in contracts, and offline backups with the ability to operate manually. For rail the fit is direct: signalling, communications, SCADA and passenger-information projects are almost always delivered by integrators, whose own servers hold your design documents, configurations and credentials long after commissioning.
