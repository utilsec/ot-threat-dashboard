A brake-controller recall quietly carried a security fix — and the EC-80 sits on transit buses as well as trucks

This was a quiet fortnight for rail and transit. No railroad or transit agency has confirmed a cyber incident, no new TSA rail security directive was issued, and nothing was published against signalling, interlockings or positive train control between 5 and 19 August. The most sector-relevant item in the window is a vehicle one. Researchers at the National Motor Freight Traffic Association examined a safety recall covering the Bendix EC-80 antilock braking and electronic stability control module and found that the accompanying firmware work also remediated remote code execution and denial-of-service conditions in the controller — security fixes delivered through a safety recall rather than through a security advisory. The recall itself was driven by fault conditions traced to electromagnetic noise, not to an attacker: there is no exploitation, no known malicious activity and no evidence anyone has used these conditions against a vehicle in service. Related NMFTA work on the J2497 power-line telematics link, which references CVE-2022-26131, is the wider research thread this sits in. The reason a transit audience should care is distribution, not drama. The EC-80 family is fitted to heavy trucks and transit buses, so for any agency running both rail and bus this is operational technology already in revenue service, maintained by a fleet team, patched through recall and technical-service-bulletin channels that almost never route past a security function, and tracked in a maintenance system rather than an asset inventory. Most agencies can say precisely which firmware version is in a wayside cabinet and cannot say the same for a braking ECU. This item is worth using as a prompt to close that gap while nothing is on fire.

High Incident — Ransomware

Medusa has passed 500 critical-infrastructure victims — and it weaponises new flaws inside a day

CISA and the FBI refreshed their #StopRansomware advisory on Medusa (an update to AA25-071A). The cumulative critical-infrastructure victim count passed 500 as of April 2026, up from roughly 300 in the March 2025 version — about 200 new victims in a year. Named sectors are healthcare, critical manufacturing, the defense industrial base, government facilities, information technology and financial services; no railroad or transit agency is named. The operationally load-bearing finding is tempo: the agencies report Medusa weaponising newly disclosed vulnerabilities within roughly 24 hours, and in some cases using working exploits up to a week before public disclosure, because the group buys exploits rather than developing them. Impact is described as encryption of the enterprise estate plus data theft, with no control-system involvement alleged. That is the shape rail should plan against. A railroad or transit agency losing its enterprise layer loses dispatch support, fare collection and ticketing, passenger information, crew rostering and maintenance management, along with the virtualisation and identity platforms underneath them — none of it individually safety-critical, all of it required to run a normal timetable, and none of it protected by the fact that signalling sits on a separate network.

Medium Incident — Rolling-stock supply chain

Stadler refused a $12.3M Everest demand — trains in service and global production were unaffected

Carried forward, because no rail or transit operator incident has been reported anywhere in this window: Swiss rolling-stock manufacturer Stadler confirmed in late July that attackers reached a data-exchange platform it shares with one of its suppliers, and refused a ransom demand of SFr 10 million (about $12.3 million) from the Everest extortion group, stating it will not pay under any circumstances. Stadler filed a criminal complaint with the Thurgau cantonal police. The company says the material taken was technical information that is not security relevant and that no relevant personal data was stolen, that its rail vehicles operating worldwide are not affected, and that global production continues as normal. The claim of a large data set is the actor’s; the operational statements are Stadler’s. For operators the interesting part is the platform class rather than the victim. Engineering and document-exchange portals shared between a rolling-stock or signalling OEM and its suppliers routinely hold vehicle technical documentation, configuration and diagnostic material about fleets that other operators run, and they typically sit outside both companies’ core networks — and outside either one’s monitoring.

Critical Advisory — CISA KEV

An actively exploited Windows IKE flaw is in KEV with a three-day deadline — and IPsec carries rail remote access

CISA added CVE-2026-33824 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 18 August with a federal remediation deadline of 21 August: a double free in the Windows IKE extensions service (IKEEXT) leading to remote code execution. A three-day deadline is CISA’s signal that exploitation is happening at scale rather than in isolated cases. Three other flaws were added the same day — a VMware vCenter path traversal (CVE-2026-59310), a SharePoint authentication weakness (CVE-2026-55040) and an Apple macOS Screen Sharing authentication flaw (CVE-2026-65400) — all carrying the same deadline. The IKE entry is the one with an OT tail. IKE is the key-exchange half of IPsec, and IPsec is what terminates site-to-site and remote-access tunnels: wayside and level-crossing cabinet backhaul, depot and workshop links, control-centre interconnects, and the vendor support tunnels used to reach signalling, SCADA and rolling-stock diagnostic systems. The Windows hosts that run IKEEXT in those paths tend to be jump servers, remote-access gateways and engineering workstations — the machines that are hardest to reboot on demand and easiest to leave a cycle behind.

Actively exploited — three-day federal remediation deadline CVE-2026-33824 — added to CISA KEV 18 Aug 2026, deadline 21 Aug 2026
Available — Microsoft updates; treat as a KEV-clock change, not a monthly one Action: Find the Windows hosts that terminate IPsec on your OT boundary and in your remote-access path, and patch those first rather than working through the estate alphabetically. Where a host cannot be patched inside the window, restrict which addresses may initiate IKE to it and watch for unexpected reloads or service restarts. Take the opportunity to write down what breaks when that tunnel is down — which vendor support sessions, which telemetry feeds, which control-centre links — because an outage during patching should not be the moment you discover it.
Suggested SLA: Patch IPsec-terminating hosts — by the 21 Aug KEV deadline; remaining Windows estate — 14 days Owner:

High Advisory — CISA Malcolm

Six flaws in CISA’s own OT monitoring suite, including an 8.8 path to code execution

ICSA-26-230-01 documents six vulnerabilities in Malcolm, the open-source OT network-traffic-analysis suite CISA itself publishes: CVE-2026-55676 (CVSS 8.8, an unrestricted file upload leading to arbitrary code execution), CVE-2026-63177 (7.1, path traversal during archive extraction), CVE-2026-63133 and CVE-2026-19671 (6.5 each), CVE-2026-63134 (5.4, role-based access-control bypass using unnormalised URI paths) and CVE-2026-19670 (5.4, an authorization bypass via percent-encoding), alongside resource-exhaustion conditions usable for denial of service. Fixes land in Malcolm 26.07.0 and 26.08.0 depending on the CVE. None is known to be exploited. The reason this belongs on a rail page rather than in a footnote is where the software sits. Malcolm is deployed inside the OT network, usually fed by a SPAN port or TAP, which means it sees signalling, SCADA, depot and telemetry traffic in aggregate — and because it is free, it is disproportionately common at exactly the transit agencies and short lines that could not fund a commercial OT monitoring platform. A monitoring host is a high-value pivot precisely because it is connected to everything and is routinely exempted from the segmentation rules applied to everything else.

Hardcoded key and path traversal in Airwall — the overlay some operators use to segment OT remote access

ICSA-26-225-03 covers Johnson Controls Airwall up to and including version 4.0.4: CVE-2026-64887 (CVSS 6.8), a hardcoded cryptographic key that allows decryption of sensitive data and configuration files, and CVE-2026-34492 (6.4), a path traversal permitting arbitrary file read. Medium Advisory — Siemens

Siemens patched CVE-2026-59693 (CVSS 4.3) in Desigo DXR2 and PXC3/4/5/7 room and building automation controllers: a specially crafted BACnet packet causes the controller to stop responding until it is manually reset, a denial-of-service rather than a code-execution flaw. Firmware updates are available from Siemens.

Medium Research — Ransomware

Industrial ransomware is up 12% — and for transportation the disruption came through ERP, not the control system

Dragos recorded 1,140 ransomware incidents against industrial organisations in Q2 2026, up 12% on Q1’s 1,020. Manufacturing absorbed 747 of them, around 65%, followed by construction (176), supporting engineering firms and integrators (117), equipment manufacturing (114) and food and beverage (70). The United States led with 431 incidents, 38% of the total, and the most active claimants were Qilin (140), Akira (129) and The Gentlemen (125). The finding that matters most is a negative one: across every sector, Dragos observed zero cases in the quarter where a ransomware operator reached Stage 2 of the ICS Cyber Kill Chain or directly manipulated a control system. Where production stopped, it stopped because virtualisation, ERP, identity or remote-access platforms went down and operations could not continue without them. Transportation is among the sectors where that pattern held. No rail or transit victim is named in the quarter’s data. For a transit agency the read-across is direct: the systems that would take you off the timetable are the ones your finance and IT colleagues own — scheduling and dispatch support, fare collection, passenger information, maintenance management, and the identity platform that lets staff log into any of it.

Low Policy — Federal R&D

No new TSA rail directive this window — Project CHARIOT is where the federal rail OT work is actually happening

Nothing new arrived from TSA on rail cybersecurity between 5 and 19 August, and no positive train control or interlocking advisory was published. The most useful federal reference point remains Project CHARIOT — Critical Infrastructure Hardening Achieved through Risk-reduction in Informational and Operational Technology — run by the DHS Science and Technology Directorate with the Federal Railroad Administration, CISA, Pacific Northwest National Laboratory and Idaho National Laboratory, alongside industry partners including the Association of American Railroads and ENSCO. It provides an at-scale platform for testing cyber scenarios against rail, oil and natural gas systems. Field testing at the Transportation Technology Center in Pueblo, Colorado examined head-of-train and end-of-train communications — the radio link an engineer relies on to monitor and control a train over its full length. The stated aims are identifying weaknesses in digitally controlled operational technology, feeding results into industry standards and building security into those standards rather than bolting it on later. This is research and standards work, not a regulatory obligation, and nothing here creates a compliance deadline.

Low Policy — National strategy

The new national science and technology strategy names OT/ICS resilience an explicit priority

The National Security Science and Technology Strategy, released on 19 August, sets out four pillars and names operational technology and industrial control system resilience, along with technology supply chains, as explicit priorities within them. It is a strategy document rather than a rule: there is no compliance obligation, no reporting requirement and no deadline attached, and nothing in it changes what a railroad or transit agency must do this week. What strategy documents do change, over a longer horizon, is where federal research money and programme attention go — the same channel that funds work like Project CHARIOT and shapes the standards that eventually reach rolling stock and wayside procurement specifications. Worth reading if you write technical requirements into supplier contracts; not worth an action item if you do not.
