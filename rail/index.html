A brake controller safety recall quietly fixed a wirelessly reachable code execution flaw

Researchers at the National Motor Freight Traffic Association found that a 2024 safety recall for the Bendix EC80 heavy-vehicle brake controller also silently fixed serious security defects, alongside the memory corruption issue Bendix publicly disclosed. Critical Incident — Nation-state / PLCs

Five federal agencies warn AI-written scripts are reading Siemens S7 controllers on exposed networks

The NSA, CISA, FBI, Department of Energy and EPA issued a joint advisory on 19 August warning that threat actors are combining the open-source snap7 and python-snap7 automation libraries with AI-assisted scripting to build custom tools that mimic legitimate OT monitoring software. Those tools give read and write access to Siemens S7 controller memory, configuration data and ladder logic over the S7comm protocol. Critical manufacturing, energy, water, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities are named as the most-targeted sectors. Actors locate exposed or poorly segmented devices with internet scanning services and get in through default or weakly configured credentials rather than exploiting a vulnerability. Affected lines run from the S7-200 and S7-300 through the S7-1200 and S7-1500, including F-series safety controllers. Rail is not named, but S7-class controllers are widespread in station and depot building services, platform screen door interfaces, escalator and lift control, tunnel ventilation, yard and washplant systems, and wayside auxiliary equipment — much of it commissioned by contractors and connected for remote support.

High Incident — Intermodal logistics

North Carolina Ports intrusion disrupts cargo processing at a rail-served inland port

The North Carolina State Ports Authority detected an unauthorised intrusion on 4 August that triggered a systems-wide IT outage affecting processing workflows across all three of its facilities: the Port of Wilmington, the Port of Morehead City and the rail-served Charlotte Inland Port. Truck movements, cargo processing and administrative systems were disrupted while restoration was underway, though terminal operations and vessel activity continued and some cargo processing shifted to manual handling. No control system was involved. For railroads and intermodal operators the significance is that inland port throughput is a shared dependency: a partner’s outage constrains rail movements and dwell without any part of the railroad’s own network being touched, and most operators have no contractual visibility into how long that partner expects recovery to take.

High Threat intel — Ransomware / edge exploitation

Six agencies warn the Gunra ransomware crew is walking into critical infrastructure through two 2025 Fortinet bugs

CISA, the FBI, the Defense Cyber Crime Center, the NSA, the Secret Service and the Korean National Police Agency published joint advisory AA26-222A on 10 August on Gunra, a ransomware operation that has hit organisations across critical infrastructure sectors since April 2025. The advisory’s operational point is unglamorous: Gunra’s initial access is overwhelmingly through internet-facing edge devices, and specifically two Fortinet flaws — CVE-2024-21762 and CVE-2024-55591 — both patched in 2025 and both still reachable on unpatched appliances. Once inside, the group steals data before encrypting, and runs a double-extortion leak site. The agencies published detection guidance, indicators and a set of mitigations centred on patching edge appliances, enforcing phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication and segmenting recovery infrastructure. Passenger and freight operators are in the critical-infrastructure set this advisory covers, and the entry point is a perimeter appliance rather than anything rail-specific. The operational risk for rail is the same one Pittsburgh Regional Transit lived through: lose the business and asset-tracking systems and you lose the ability to see where stock is, even when signalling itself is untouched.

Load AA26-222A indicators into monitoring, and confirm that asset-tracking and dispatch support data can be restored without domain credentials.

Medium Research — Facility CPS exposure

Claroty finds one in five facility cyber-physical assets a single hop from internet-exposed systems

Team82 analysed more than 750,000 cyber-physical assets across large facilities and found nearly one in five sitting one hop from a system making outbound internet connections. Close to 90 percent of the building management systems examined communicated over insecure protocols and 40 percent ran outdated firmware, with around a third of HVAC and cooling systems one hop from an internet-exposed system. The study covers data centres, but the asset classes map directly onto stations, depots and control centres: power distribution, building management, physical access control, fire protection and ventilation. In rail those systems are usually owned by property or facilities rather than by signalling engineering, procured under separate contracts, connected by suppliers for remote monitoring, and absent from the OT asset register — which is how the distance from the internet to a tunnel ventilation controller gets short without anyone deciding it should.

High Threat intel — Supply chain / engineering workstations

A conferencing server was made to hand out a backdoored client to the engineers who joined the call

Kaspersky ICS-CERT reported on 12 August that the Head Mare group chained two flaws in unpatched TrueConf Server installations — tracked by Kaspersky as KLCERT-26-057 and KLCERT-26-058 — to run code and escalate privileges on the server, then swapped the legitimate client installer for a trojanised build carrying the PhantomCore and PhantomGraph backdoors. Anyone who subsequently updated their conferencing client from that server was infected. The victims named span instrumentation, electronics, transportation, energy, IT and software development. TrueConf shipped fixes on 18 June 2026 for versions 5.3.x before 5.3.9, 5.4.x before 5.4.9 and 5.5.x before 5.5.5; the exploited servers were simply never updated. Transportation is one of the sectors Kaspersky names. Railways run a large conferencing estate across operations control centres, maintenance depots, signalling contractors and rolling-stock suppliers, and the people on those calls are frequently the same ones holding signalling engineering tools and remote-access credentials into wayside and depot systems. A backdoored client distributed as a normal update crosses straight into that population.

Actively exploited — Kaspersky documents live intrusions and trojanised installers in the wild Vendor-tracked identifiers (KLCERT-26-057/058) — no CISA KEV entry
Patched 18 Jun 2026 — exposure is unpatched servers and already-distributed clients Action: Identify every TrueConf Server used by operations, depots and signalling contractors and confirm it is patched to 5.3.9 / 5.4.9 / 5.5.5 or later. Treat any engineering or dispatch workstation that updated its client from an unpatched server since mid-June as suspect, and rebuild rather than scan — the backdoor arrives inside a signed-looking installer.

Critical Advisory — Remote access edge

Citrix is telling admins to patch NetScaler now — an unauthenticated bypass on the appliance that fronts most OT remote access

Citrix published bulletin CTX696939 on 20 August covering two NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway flaws. The serious one, CVE-2026-19490, is an authentication bypass carrying a CVSS score of 9.3: on Gateway and AAA virtual-server configurations an unauthenticated attacker can skip the login step entirely. The second, CVE-2026-19489, is a memory overflow that can produce unpredictable behaviour or a denial of service, and only applies where SIP ALG is enabled on a Large Scale NAT group. Fixed builds are 14.1-73.32 and 13.1-63.21 or later. Citrix, Rapid7 and Finland’s national cyber authority all said they were not aware of in-the-wild exploitation at publication, while urging immediate patching — NetScaler has a long record of exploited edge flaws. On a rail estate NetScaler typically fronts supplier access into signalling maintenance networks, depot systems and station equipment, and connects remote control centres. An unauthenticated bypass there matters more than the CVSS number suggests, because that appliance is often the only boundary between a contractor’s laptop and a maintenance VLAN that reaches trackside.

Medium Advisory — Life safety / facility systems

Fire-alarm management software stores its credentials in cleartext in memory, CISA warns

CISA published ICSA-26-232-01 on 20 August covering Johnson Controls Simplex Incident Manager, the software operators use to monitor and manage Simplex fire alarm panels across a property portfolio. Versions up to and including V2.01 keep user passwords and authentication tokens unencrypted in system memory while the application runs, tracked as CVE-2026-27875 and classified as CWE-316. A local attacker who already holds low-privileged access to the workstation can read those credentials out of memory and reuse them against the application and the systems it connects to. CISA lists critical manufacturing, commercial facilities, government facilities, transportation systems and energy among the affected sectors, with deployment worldwide. Johnson Controls has published remediation guidance in JCI-PSA-2026-28. Stations, depots and control centres run Simplex fire detection, and on a rail estate the fire-alarm management workstation is almost always a facilities asset — shared account, in a back office, administered by a building services contractor rather than the signalling or IT team. That is precisely the local low-privileged access this flaw needs to yield working panel credentials.

No known public exploitation — requires existing local low-privileged access Not in CISA KEV
Vendor guidance available — Johnson Controls advisory JCI-PSA-2026-28 Action: Ask your station and depot facilities managers whether Simplex Incident Manager is in use and at what version, then apply the Johnson Controls guidance in JCI-PSA-2026-28. Until it is patched, remove shared logins from the host, restrict interactive access to named staff and confirm the credentials it stores are not reused for access control or BMS. Life-safety systems belong in the asset inventory even when facilities owns them.

Critical Advisory — Exploited edge / KEV

Cisco ASA and FTD VPN zero-day exploited to reload firewalls, added to CISA KEV

Cisco has patched CVE-2026-20349, a flaw in the remote-access SSL VPN service of Secure Firewall ASA and Secure Firewall Threat Defense software that attackers are exploiting to force appliances offline. A specially crafted HTTP request to the vulnerable service causes an unexpected reload and a denial-of-service condition, requiring no authentication and no user interaction. Cisco’s PSIRT confirmed active exploitation in August, and CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue with a federal remediation deadline of 14 August. Hot fixes cover ASA 9.16, 9.18, 9.20, 9.22, 9.23 and 9.24 and FTD 7.0, 7.2, 7.4, 7.6, 7.7 and 10.0. In rail these appliances typically terminate supplier access into signalling maintenance networks, depot systems and station equipment, and connect remote control centres — so exploitation removes the remote diagnostic path exactly when a fault needs a specialist who is not on site.

Medium Regulation — TSA security directives

TSA rail security directive runs to 15 January 2027 while the surface cyber rule remains proposed

Security Directive 1580-21-01E, Enhancing Rail Cybersecurity, took effect on 16 January 2026 and runs through 15 January 2027, applying to designated freight railroad carriers and requiring performance-based cybersecurity measures including incident reporting to CISA. Behind it sits the broader rulemaking, Enhancing Surface Cyber Risk Management, which would require higher-risk pipeline, freight railroad, passenger railroad and rail transit owners and operators to establish and maintain a comprehensive cyber risk management programme and report incidents to CISA. That rule remains proposed rather than final, so the directives continue to carry the obligation. The practical planning point is the annual reissue cycle: directives are renewed rather than permanent, and each renewal has historically tightened expectations, so an operator building only to the current text tends to be behind at the next issue.

Medium Regulation — EU Cyber Resilience Act

From 11 September 2026 exploited vulnerabilities in digital products must be reported within 24 hours

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s reporting obligations take effect on 11 September 2026. From that date, manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents affecting those products: an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware, a full notification within 72 hours, and a final report within 14 days of a corrective measure being available, or within a month for a severe incident. Reporting flows through a single reporting platform to the relevant national CSIRT with simultaneous availability to ENISA. The obligations cover products already on the EU market, not only new releases. For rail this cuts two ways. Operators buying signalling, rolling stock systems and station equipment from EU-facing suppliers should expect faster and more formal vulnerability disclosure from September. Operators who themselves place digital products on the EU market are manufacturers under the Act.
