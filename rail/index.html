Top Story

Attackers took hands-on control of water-utility PLCs across a dozen states — locking out operators and knocking a treatment plant offline

Between 26–27 July 2026 a coordinated campaign disrupted automated controls at more than 30 Minnesota community water systems, and confirmed intrusions have since been reported across roughly a dozen states including Georgia, Michigan, South Dakota, Alabama and New Jersey. This is hands-on manipulation of control equipment, not a data breach: CISA says the actors changed PLC passwords and altered device IP addresses to lock operators out of their own equipment. In Braham, Minnesota a pump failure forced a treatment facility offline and prompted a resident conservation notice; Clayton County, Georgia issued a precautionary boil-water advisory after a pump-station disruption, later lifted. Dragos’s Rob Lee told NPR he could not recall “this many targets at once with operational impact.” Every affected utility reported that drinking water stayed safe, with staff falling back to manual operation. Federal officials have pointed to Iran-linked activity — a TLP:AMBER WaterISAC assessment reportedly found the evidence aligned with campaigns previously attributed to Iran — but no formal public attribution has been made, so treat the actor as suspected rather than confirmed.

Confirmed OT-disruptive incident — operator lockout, pump failure, plant offline across multiple utilities No single CVE — abuse of internet-exposed PLCs, default/absent credentials and weak remote access
No patch — architectural: remove OT from the internet, credential and remote-access hygiene Action: Today, not this quarter: find every controller, HMI and cellular router of yours reachable from the internet and take it off. Then change every default and shared PLC password, put remote access behind MFA and a broker, back up and hash your PLC project files and logic so you can prove and restore known-good state, and rehearse running the plant manually with the SCADA layer assumed hostile.

Medium Incident — Ransomware

Stadler refused a $12.3M Everest ransom after a supplier platform breach — and kept building trains

The Everest ransomware group breached a data-exchange platform shared between Swiss rolling-stock manufacturer Stadler Rail and one of its suppliers in mid-July 2026, stealing technical information Stadler describes as belonging to the supplier and not safety-related. Stadler says its own IT systems were not compromised, production continued, and rail vehicles in service worldwide were unaffected. It refused the roughly SFr10m ($12.3m) demand and filed a criminal complaint with Thurgau cantonal police. This is the shape most rail cyber incidents take: extortion against engineering and supplier data, disruptive and expensive, with the systems that actually move trains untouched. That is not luck — it reflects segmentation that held.

Confirmed extortion attempt — supplier data platform; production and rolling stock unaffected No CVE — compromised credentials on a shared third-party platform
Not applicable — identity and third-party access control Action: Inventory the shared engineering and data-exchange platforms you use with suppliers, enforce MFA and least privilege on all of them, and confirm a supplier-side compromise cannot reach your engineering or production networks. Decide your ransom position and its communications in advance, not during.

High Incident — Signalling

The Taiwan brake-radio hack remains rail’s clearest proof that unauthenticated RF is a control channel

On 5 April 2026 a 23-year-old student triggered emergency braking on four Taiwan High Speed Rail trains, halting them for around 48 minutes, by using a low-cost software-defined radio to decode the parameters of THSR’s TETRA train-radio system and transmit a forged General Alarm beacon from reprogrammed handheld radios. Investigators reported the TETRA configuration had gone essentially unchanged for around 19 years. Authorities arrested the main suspect on 28 April, seizing an SDR device, a laptop and eleven handheld radios. It is included here as standing context rather than fresh news, because it is still the only confirmed real-world case of an unauthenticated RF safety channel being abused to stop trains — no malware, no CVE, no network intrusion required.

Confirmed OT-disruptive incident — emergency braking forced on four trains No CVE — abuse of unauthenticated TETRA train-radio signalling
No patch — RF authentication and key-management weakness; mitigation is monitoring and key rotation Action: Find out when your radio and RF signalling parameters and keys were last rotated. If the answer is measured in years or nobody knows, that is the finding. Add RF spectrum and radio-network monitoring for duplicate or anomalous alarm frames, and build operational detection for unexplained emergency-brake events.
Suggested SLA: RF key rotation and anomaly monitoring review — this planning cycle Owner:

Medium Incident — Availability

Deutsche Bahn’s booking and information systems went down to a DDoS — trains kept moving

A distributed denial-of-service attack took down bahn.de and the DB Navigator app for Germany’s national rail operator in February 2026. No group claimed responsibility, though reporting noted the broader pattern of pro-Russian hacktivist DDoS activity against European critical infrastructure. Train movement was unaffected. It is included as standing context because it and the Ukrzaliznytsia attack define the realistic European rail threat model: high-visibility, passenger-facing availability attacks that generate substantial public disruption and press coverage while never touching signalling or dispatch. Planning for that is a communications and passenger-information problem at least as much as a security one.

Confirmed availability attack — passenger-facing IT only, no signalling impact No CVE
Not applicable — DDoS resilience Action: Confirm your passenger-information and ticketing platforms have DDoS protection that has been tested, and that station staff have an offline procedure for informing and ticketing passengers when the app and website are gone. That procedure is what determines whether this is an inconvenience or a crisis.

High Advisory — CISA KEV

Cisco ASA and Firepower join KEV under active exploitation — the OT perimeter is the target again

CISA added CVE-2026-20349, a heap-inspection vulnerability in Cisco Secure Firewall ASA and Firepower Threat Defense software, to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 11 August 2026, citing evidence of active exploitation. The same batch added CVE-2026-68820, a use-after-free in the Windows afd.sys driver enabling local privilege escalation — relevant because Windows engineering workstations and HMI servers are exactly where that gets chained after initial access — and CVE-2026-72898, a SQL injection in Metabase exposing stored credentials. Earlier in the month CISA added CVE-2026-8037, an unauthenticated command injection in Progress Kemp LoadMaster carrying CVSS 9.6, after sustained exploitation attempts, with a federal patch deadline of 10 August under BOD 26-04. None of these are ICS products. All of them sit in front of, or inside, the path into OT.

Active — all four added to KEV on evidence of exploitation CVE-2026-20349, CVE-2026-68820, CVE-2026-72898, CVE-2026-8037 — all in CISA KEV
Available — vendor fixes released Action: Patch the perimeter and remote-access tier on the KEV clock, not your OT change-control clock — these devices are IT-managed but they are the front door to your plant network. Then verify what a compromise of that firewall would actually reach: if the answer is “the control network,” the segmentation work matters more than the patch.

High Advisory — Remote access

An N-able N-central auth bypass was exploited, patched incompletely, then exploited again

CISA added two N-able N-central authentication-bypass flaws to KEV within two days: CVE-2026-18556 and CVE-2026-18577, both CVSS 8.2, the second being an incomplete-fix follow-on to the first. N-able confirmed a limited number of customer breaches. Attackers who exploit it take over administrator accounts and can pivot to managed endpoints through the platform’s Take Control remote-session feature. Huntress reported that 55.6% of reachable cloud servers were still unpatched at the time of its analysis. The fix is 2026.3.1.7 / 2026.3 HF1. RMM platforms matter disproportionately in OT because they are how integrators, machine builders and managed-service providers reach plant equipment — often through a path that was never security-reviewed and does not appear on the network diagram.

Active — N-able confirmed customer breaches; both CVEs in KEV CVE-2026-18556 and CVE-2026-18577 — both in CISA KEV
Available — 2026.3.1.7 / 2026.3 HF1 Action: Patch immediately if you run N-central. More importantly, ask which RMM and remote-support tools your integrators and OEMs use to reach your equipment, whether those sessions are brokered and logged on your side, and whether you can revoke that access unilaterally today. Most sites cannot answer the third question.

Critical Advisory — CVSS 10.0

A CVSS 10.0 command injection in Haiwell’s cloud HMI gateway hands an unauthenticated attacker root

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-02 covers CVE-2026-19188, an OS command injection (CWE-78) in the Haiwell IoT Cloud HMI Gateway that scores the full CVSS 10.0. The flaw sits in the device’s Net Check function: an unauthenticated attacker can reach the cmdPing Socket.io event and execute arbitrary commands as root. CISA flags the product’s use in energy, critical manufacturing and water/wastewater environments. Affected firmware is 3.40.1.12; the fix is 3.50.1.19. No in-the-wild exploitation had been reported at publication, which is the good news — a maximum-severity, unauthenticated, internet-reachable root execution on a gateway product is exactly the profile that gets weaponised quickly once someone bothers.

No confirmed exploitation reported at publication CVE-2026-19188 — not in CISA KEV as of this edition
Available — firmware 3.50.1.19 Action: Inventory for Haiwell gateways — they arrive through machine builders and integrators more often than through your own procurement, so an asset-register search may miss them. Patch to 3.50.1.19, and in the meantime confirm none is reachable from the internet or from your business network.

High Research — Exposure

Researchers found 4,407 internet-facing Rockwell controllers — and 19 of 22 in already-attacked cities ran firmware vulnerable to a 2017 bug

A Cloud Security Alliance scan on 3 August 2026 identified 4,407 internet-facing Rockwell / Allen-Bradley controllers worldwide, 2,844 of them in the United States. The more uncomfortable finding is local: of 22 exposed controllers located in cities already hit by the July campaign, 19 were still running firmware vulnerable to CVE-2017-16740, a buffer-overflow flaw patched back in 2017. Separately, CISA Acting Director Nick Andersen said federal responders assisting with the recovery are still finding water-system PLCs reachable from the internet with factory-default credentials or no password at all. Broader counts cited during the response put roughly 10,000 Rockwell, Siemens and Schneider devices on the public internet sector-wide. The takeaway is not a novel zero-day; it is that a decade of unpatched, unauthenticated exposure is doing the work for the attacker.

No confirmed exploitation of this specific CVE in the campaign — but the exposure it measures is being actively abused CVE-2017-16740 — not in CISA KEV
Available — patched in 2017; MicroLogix 1400 firmware v21.003+ recommended Action: Do not assume this is someone else’s problem. Search your own public IP ranges and your cellular/LTE router estate with Shodan or Censys, from outside your network, and treat anything that answers on an industrial protocol as an incident. Then check controller firmware age — multi-year patch lag is the actual finding here.
Suggested SLA: External exposure scan — 7 days; controller firmware review — 90 days Owner:

High Threat Intel — Ransomware

Dragos counted 1,140 industrial ransomware incidents in Q2 — and not one reached stage 2 of the ICS kill chain

Dragos’s Q2 2026 industrial ransomware analysis recorded 1,140 incidents against industrial organisations, up 12% on Q1. Manufacturing absorbed 747 of them (65%), led by construction at 176 and equipment manufacturing at 114; food and beverage accounted for 70. The finding worth carrying into your own risk conversations is the negative one: Dragos found no case in Q2 where a ransomware operator reached stage 2 of the ICS Cyber Kill Chain. Production stopped because enterprise IT and ERP systems stopped — order entry, scheduling, shipping, labelling — not because anyone touched a controller. Named Q2 cases include West Pharmaceutical Services and Foxconn, the latter with a roughly two-week outage at Wisconsin and Texas sites. That pattern should shape where you spend: the loss is real, but the control is IT/OT dependency mapping and recovery, not deeper controller hardening.

Not applicable — incident-tracking analysis No CVE
Not applicable Action: Map which enterprise systems your plant genuinely cannot run without for more than a shift — MES, historian, ERP order flow, label printing, shipping manifests — and test running without each one. That list, not a controller inventory, is what determines how long a ransomware event stops your production.

Info Research — Exercise

Cyber Europe 2026 stress-tested rail and maritime operators together

ENISA ran what it described as its largest cybersecurity exercise to date on 10–11 June 2026, simulating coordinated attacks against European rail and maritime transport networks; Italy alone fielded 21 critical-infrastructure operators. Pairing the two sectors in one scenario is the interesting design choice — it reflects how a disruption to intermodal freight propagates across both, and how the two regulatory regimes (NIS2, the rail security directives, IACS class rules) intersect in practice. Exercise findings are not fully public, but participation is a reasonable proxy for maturity: operators who took part have a tested playbook and named the gaps, and those who did not have neither.

Not applicable Action: If you did not take part, run the intermodal scenario yourself: a disruption at a port or rail interchange that stops handover in both directions. The value is discovering which decisions have no named owner, which is what these exercises reliably surface.

Info Regulation — Supply chain

European rail suppliers want the power to exclude high-risk vendors from embedded rail technology

UNIFE, the European rail supply industry association, asked the European Commission and ENISA to assess and where necessary restrict high-risk non-EU suppliers of embedded rail technologies, and to have the Public Procurement Directive reform designate rail a strategic sector. It is an industry position rather than enacted law, and it sits alongside the EU’s broader Cybersecurity Act update. Reported here for the factual substance: the mechanism under discussion is procurement-level exclusion of suppliers on security grounds, mirroring what has already happened in telecoms and, as of this month, in US solar inverters. Rail operators planning long-lived fleet and signalling procurements should track it because it changes the supplier field, not because of its politics.

Not applicable — policy proposal No CVE
Not applicable Action: For fleet and signalling procurements running past 2027, add a contractual position on supplier-substitution risk if a component vendor is later restricted. That clause is cheap to write now and very expensive to negotiate after the fact.

Info Regulation — Policy

The OT Cybersecurity Coalition asked Congress to fix the plumbing behind critical-infrastructure threat sharing

Prompted largely by the water-sector attacks, the OT Cybersecurity Coalition — executive director Tatyana Bolton, chair Alison King of Forescout — called on Congress to confirm a director for the Department of Energy’s Office of Cybersecurity, Energy Security and Emergency Response, reauthorise the State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program, and extend the Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act of 2015, which expires in September 2026. The 2015 Act is the liability protection that makes many private-sector operators willing to share indicators with the government at all; if it lapses, the flow of the information this dashboard depends on gets thinner. Presented factually: these are the mechanisms currently under review, and their status affects operators regardless of position on the underlying policy.
