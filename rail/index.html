Medium Incident — Physical sabotage of signalling infrastructure, not cyber

Coordinated track sabotage stopped Dutch trains for a morning — physical, not cyber, and the fail-safe did exactly what it is designed to do

On the morning of Tuesday 15 September, the day of the Dutch state opening of parliament, ProRail found steel pipes up to two metres long fixed to the rails at somewhere between twenty and thirty locations, including the Amersfoort–Zwolle, Zwolle–Leeuwarden, Utrecht–Arnhem, Arnhem–Zutphen, Amersfoort–Deventer and Gouda–Oudewater lines. A pipe bridging the two rails shorts the track circuit, so the occupancy detection reports a train that is not there and the protecting signals hold at red. The first was found at about 05:30; one train struck a pipe near Steenwijk without injury; services north of Utrecht were worst affected, with knock-on disruption to German connections, and most of the network was restored by the afternoon. ProRail said the material had been placed intentionally; the AIVD intelligence service is working with police; no suspect has been named and a farmers’ protest group publicly denied involvement. This was physical sabotage with no reported cyber component, and it does not change the cyber threat level on this page. It is here because it is the only rail incident of the week and because the mechanism is instructive: an attacker who understood signalling chose to trigger the safety case rather than defeat it, and the result was delay rather than danger. That is the outcome a track circuit or axle counter is engineered to produce, and it is the same outcome a defender should expect from a well-designed vital system under most cyber conditions — a fail-safe stop, with the operational cost carried in disruption.

Info Threat Intelligence & Research — Federal detection guidance

CISA publishes a cyber-decoys guide — detection you can add without touching a vital system

CISA released “Using Cyber Decoys to Strengthen Detection and Response” on 17 September. It defines decoys as assets that appear to be legitimate systems, accounts or data but exist to distract adversaries, reveal their presence and support threat-intelligence collection, and it sets out three phases — preparation, execution and analysis — positioning the technique alongside Zero Trust rather than as a replacement for it. The reason this matters more in rail than in most sectors is what it does not require. Decoys, honeytokens and tripwire accounts are additive: a credential that should never be used, a fake engineering share, a dummy historian tag, a service account that exists only to be alerted on. None of that changes a signalling, interlocking or traction-power system, which is normally the reason detection work stalls on a railway — the asset owner will not accept a change to a vital system for a monitoring benefit. This is guidance, not a threat item: no vulnerability, no incident and no product is involved.

Info Threat Intelligence & Research — Analysis, engineering practice

Cyber-informed engineering argues the useful question is not how to keep attackers out, but which consequences you can design away

An analysis published 15 September sets out the case for cyber-informed engineering in critical infrastructure: that industrial systems operate under a constraint IT does not share, because a compromised network does not automatically have to become a catastrophic physical event. The argument is to move the security question upstream into design — identifying the physical consequences that actually matter, then engineering them out with controls that do not depend on the digital system remaining trustworthy. Rail has practised this for a century without calling it cybersecurity: interlocking, fail-safe signalling and vital logic exist precisely so that a failure upstream cannot produce an unsafe movement. The current argument is that those principles should be applied deliberately to digital systems rather than assumed to still hold. This is practitioner analysis rather than incident reporting; it names no victim and describes no attack, but it is the most useful lens available for operators whose detection budget will never match their consequence exposure.

High Advisory — Actively exploited, KEV-listed, small-office switch

CISA adds a Zyxel GS1900 switch flaw to KEV after GreyNoise counts 996 compromised units — the kind of switch found in a station office, not a signal room

CISA added CVE-2026-7273 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 21 September with a federal remediation deadline of 24 September. It is a stack-based buffer overflow in the CGI program of Zyxel GS1900-series managed switches (GS1900-8, 8HP, 10HP, 16, 24, 24E, 24EP, 24HPv2, 48 and 48HPv2) that lets an unauthenticated attacker with access to the management interface execute operating-system commands through a crafted HTTP request. GreyNoise links the same actor to data theft from an unnamed western government organisation through two WordPress flaws, and infers an overlap with the Red Heron activity already on this page through shared infrastructure and malware — an inference, not an observation. No rail or transit victim is identified. Our assessment of the rail relevance: GS1900 is a small-office switch, and the place it turns up on a railway or transit property is the station back office, the yard office, the small agency’s headquarters LAN or a contractor’s site cabin, not the signalling or traction-power network. A compromised switch there yields a network foothold and every credential configured on it.
