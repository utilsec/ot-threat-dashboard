Top Story

A private cellular APN carried attackers into a Polish power plant — the same network model rail runs its trackside gear on

CERT.PL has published a detailed account of a previously undisclosed intrusion into Poland’s energy sector, and the path matters more to rail than the target does. The attackers first compromised an internet-facing FortiGate appliance serving as firewall and VPN concentrator at a wind farm. From there they scanned the carrier-managed private APN — the cellular network the operator used to reach remote sites — for VNC, HTTP, Modbus and S7 services, and found a WAGO PFC200 controller at a combined heat and power plant with a WAN-facing management interface secured only by default credentials. They enabled SSH on the device, tunnelled into the plant’s OT network, and stopped a steam turbine and the process water treatment system; several ICS devices were permanently bricked during the clean-up. The intrusion itself dates to 29 December 2025, and heat and electricity deliveries to the plant’s roughly 50,000 customers were not interrupted, so this is a disclosed past event rather than live activity. CERT.PL describes it as the first publicly documented case of an attacker using a private APN as a lateral-movement path between sites, and warns the underlying misconfiguration — a flat cellular bearer with no segmentation between customer devices — is common internationally. That is the part rail cannot treat as someone else’s problem. Carrier-managed private APNs are the standard way freight and passenger railroads and transit agencies connect trackside signal cabinets, level-crossing controllers, depot systems and rolling-stock telemetry back to a control centre, precisely because they are marketed as a closed, carrier-isolated network. Almost no operator has asked the carrier in writing what else shares that APN, whether it is segmented device-to-device, or what happens if one compromised cabinet can reach another. Reporting also links the wider campaign — roughly 30 other Polish energy sites — to Russia-linked activity, though this specific facility’s attribution rests on the CERT.PL report rather than a formal government statement.

Confirmed hands-on-OT sabotage — steam turbine and water-treatment system stopped, field devices bricked (Dec 2025 intrusion, disclosed Aug 2026) No CVE — exposed VPN appliance, flat private-APN routing, default credentials on a WAN-facing PLC management interface
No patch — APN segmentation, edge hardening, credential and remote-access controls Action: Ask your cellular carrier in writing what your private APN can route to and from, then verify the answer independently rather than accepting it. Inventory every cellular router, wayside cabinet gateway and remote terminal unit on that bearer, disable SSH and web administration on field devices that do not need them, and test whether compromising one level-crossing or signal cabinet can reach another cabinet or the control centre. Confirm you hold offline, verified copies of controller logic for wayside and depot assets — that is what let the Polish plant recover.

High Incident — Nation-state

A dozen-state PLC lockout campaign shows what an exposed wayside controller costs an operator

Since 27–28 July, actors have reached internet-exposed Rockwell Automation and Allen-Bradley MicroLogix 1100 and 1400 controllers at water and wastewater utilities in at least 12 US states, changing device IP addresses and setting unauthorised passwords to lock operators out of monitoring and control. More than 30 Minnesota community systems were affected, a Georgia utility issued a boil-water advisory after a pump station failure, and New Jersey and Alabama joined the list in mid-August. Agencies assess the activity to Iranian-affiliated APT actors, publicly named as CyberAv3ngers, under joint advisory AA26-097A; no CVE was exploited — the actors used exposure plus default or weak credentials and the manufacturers’ own PLC programming software. No rail operator has been named in this campaign. What transfers directly is the equipment class and the technique: MicroLogix and similarly general-purpose PLC families run drawbridge and lock control, tunnel ventilation, station and depot pumping, and points heating on many railroads, and the same ‘exposed and left on default credentials’ failure mode applies regardless of which sector owns the device.

Active, multi-state confirmed OT-disruptive campaign; assessed to Iranian-affiliated actors (CyberAv3ngers) under AA26-097A No CVE exploited — exposure plus default/weak credentials
No patch applicable — remove controllers from the internet, enforce credentials Action: Sweep your own external address space, and your signalling and infrastructure contractors’ ranges, for PLC and HMI protocols reachable from the internet — not just Rockwell, any brand. Prioritise assets a contractor commissioned and still supports remotely: drawbridge and lock control, tunnel ventilation, station and depot pumping, points heating. Those are where an exposed controller survives longest because ownership sits between the infrastructure team and a maintenance contract.

High Incident — Ransomware

CISA’s Gunra advisory describes the access pattern that would take down a railroad’s supporting systems, not its signalling

CISA and international partners issued a joint #StopRansomware advisory (AA26-222A) on Gunra, a Conti-derived ransomware-as-a-service operation running double-extortion campaigns against government and critical-infrastructure victims, typically entering through unpatched internet-facing VPN gateways and exposed RDP; the advisory cites Fortinet flaws CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 among the paths used, and Gunra has been observed giving victims five to seven days to pay before publishing stolen data. No rail-sector victim has been named in this advisory. The relevance to rail is the shape of the intrusion Dragos separately confirmed across the wider industrial ransomware picture this quarter: entry through an internet-facing edge device, then encryption of the enterprise and virtualisation estate rather than any direct manipulation of a control system. In rail that estate typically carries dispatch support, passenger information, ticketing, crew rostering and maintenance management — none of it safety-critical individually, all of it required to run a normal timetable.

Active ransomware-as-a-service operation with observed victims; double-extortion, 5–7 day payment deadline No single CVE — entry via unpatched VPN gateways and exposed RDP, incl. Fortinet CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472
Mitigations only — patch cited edge-device flaws, credential and remote-access hygiene Action: Deploy the advisory’s indicators against your perimeter and confirm they actually fire in a test. Then, before an incident forces the question, decide in advance — with operations, not IT alone — what timetable you run when ticketing, passenger information, crew rostering and maintenance management are all unavailable simultaneously but signalling is untouched, and who is authorised to make that call outside business hours.

Medium Incident — Ports

North Carolina’s three port facilities went to manual processing after an IT breach — and the OT question is still open

An outside actor breached IT systems at the North Carolina State Ports Authority around 5 August, forcing Wilmington, Morehead City and the Charlotte inland terminal onto manual cargo processing; a spokesperson confirmed the breach but the authority has declined to say whether ransomware was involved. The incident was described as contained, with normal operating schedules resuming 6–7 August, though some processing reportedly remains manual. No confirmation exists either way on whether any crane, gate or terminal-operating-system technology was reached — officials have not said it was compromised, but they have not ruled it out either. Ports are freight rail’s handoff point: container and bulk terminals commonly interchange directly with on-dock or near-dock rail, so a multi-day disruption at any port’s gate and yard systems ripples into intermodal train scheduling and railcar dwell even when the railroad’s own systems are untouched and the incident never appears in a railroad’s own reporting.

Confirmed IT breach with multi-facility operational impact; ransomware involvement not confirmed or denied No CVE disclosed — initial access vector not made public
No patch — contingency planning, manual-fallback capability, intermodal coordination Action: If your railroad interchanges with a port terminal, ask your intermodal counterpart what their manual-fallback procedure actually is and how long it holds before railcar dwell and yard congestion follow. Separately, confirm your own gate, yard and dispatch systems have a rehearsed manual fallback that does not depend on the same network or vendor as your primary systems.

Medium Advisory — Siemens

Siemens patches Ruggedcom APE1808, the substation platform that also runs rail wayside and trackside networking

Siemens’ August ProductCERT release includes two fixes for Ruggedcom APE1808, an application-hosting platform built into substation and field-network equipment: CVE-2026-23573, a cross-site scripting flaw, and CVE-2026-59839, a path-traversal issue inherited from bundled Fortinet components, rated CVSS 6.1 and 5.5. Siemens markets APE1808 explicitly for transportation substations as well as energy substations, and railroads use the same class of ruggedised, DIN-rail-mounted networking gear to carry signalling, SCADA and telemetry traffic between trackside cabinets, level crossings and control centres. Neither flaw is known to be exploited, but both require an update plus separate attention to the underlying Fortinet guidance, since the path-traversal issue rides in through third-party components rather than Siemens’ own code.

No confirmed exploitation reported at publication Not in CISA KEV as of this edition
Available — apply the Siemens update and the referenced Fortinet guidance Action: Identify every Ruggedcom APE1808 deployment on your network, including any installed inside third-party substation or trackside cabinets you don’t directly maintain. Ruggedcom firmware is the item most likely to sit unpatched for years on a railway because updating it usually means a possession, and nobody owns that scheduling. Get it onto the next possession or maintenance-window plan explicitly, rather than folding it into a routine ticket that never gets prioritised.

Medium Advisory — Siemens

A malformed BACnet packet can freeze Siemens Desigo building controllers used in stations and depots

Siemens patched CVE-2026-59693 (CVSS 4.3) in Desigo DXR2 and PXC3/4/5/7 room and building automation controllers: a specially crafted BACnet packet causes the controller to stop responding until it is manually reset, a denial-of-service rather than a code-execution flaw. Desigo hardware runs heating, ventilation, lighting and access-adjacent building systems in commercial and institutional buildings, including station concourses, depots and administrative buildings on many railroads and transit agencies. A frozen building controller is not itself a safety event, but stations that lose HVAC or lighting control during a service disruption compound whatever else is going wrong, and depot buildings with frozen environmental controls can affect maintenance operations. Firmware updates are available from Siemens.

No confirmed exploitation reported at publication Not in CISA KEV as of this edition
Available — Siemens firmware update Action: Building-management systems at stations and depots usually sit with facilities rather than the OT security team, which is why advisories like this drift. Confirm who owns Desigo patching at each station and depot, add it to the same tracking you use for signalling and SCADA vendor advisories, and schedule the firmware update rather than waiting for the next unrelated facilities visit.

Critical Advisory — Johnson Controls

Critical Johnson Controls C-CURE 9000 and victor flaws could hand an attacker a station’s access-control system

Johnson Controls updated its advisory (Update A, 11 August) for C-CURE 9000 and victor, its physical access-control and video platforms, covering three vulnerabilities: CVE-2026-21655 and CVE-2026-21653, two critical unauthenticated deserialization flaws, and CVE-2026-34496, a high-severity issue, with CVSS scores up to 9.6. A network-adjacent attacker who reaches the server can execute code and take over the system without authenticating. C-CURE and victor are widely deployed for station, depot and administrative-building access control and CCTV across transit agencies and railroads, meaning a successful exploit would give an attacker control of door locking, badge access and camera systems at the facilities running it — a genuine physical-security consequence rather than a purely IT one.

No confirmed exploitation reported at publication Not in CISA KEV as of this edition
Available — apply the Johnson Controls Update A fixes Action: Confirm which stations, depots and administrative buildings run C-CURE 9000 or victor, and whether the server is reachable from anything beyond the badge-reader and camera network it should be isolated to. Patch on a Critical timeline rather than the facilities team’s normal change cycle, since the practical impact of a successful exploit is an attacker unlocking doors and disabling cameras.

High Advisory — CISA KEV

An actively exploited Cisco firewall flaw hit CISA’s KEV list with a three-day deadline — and ASA/FTD sit at a lot of rail perimeters

Cisco disclosed CVE-2026-20349, a heap-inspection denial-of-service flaw in the Remote Access SSL VPN service of Secure Firewall ASA and FTD software, rated CVSS 8.6. It is being actively exploited to crash appliances, and CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 11 August with a federal remediation deadline of 14 August. Hotfixes cover all affected 9.x, 7.x and 10.x branches; there is no workaround. ASA and FTD are commonly deployed as the boundary device between corporate networks and operational technology, including the appliance that carries vendor and engineering remote access into signalling and depot systems on many railroads. An appliance an attacker can force to reload on demand is not a control anyone can rely on during an incident.

Actively exploited to crash devices CVE-2026-20349 — added to CISA KEV 11 Aug 2026, federal deadline 14 Aug 2026
Available — Cisco hotfixes for all affected branches; no workaround Action: Patch every ASA or FTD that sits at an IT/OT boundary or carries remote vendor access on the KEV clock, not the normal change calendar. Map what depends on that appliance — signalling vendor support tunnels, control-centre connectivity, remote diagnostics — and document the fallback before an unplanned reload forces you to find out live.

High Advisory — CISA ICS batch

CISA released 15 ICS advisories in a single day — several touch equipment classes rail also runs

CISA published 15 industrial control system advisories on 13 August alone, spanning AVEVA, Hitachi Energy, ANDRITZ, Siemens and Johnson Controls, with several flagged for the energy sector. Beyond the Ruggedcom, Desigo and C-CURE items covered separately, the batch includes AVEVA Enterprise SCADA / HMI (CVE-2025-7639, CVSS 7.1, untrusted deserialization exploitable by an authenticated operator-level user, patched), Hitachi Energy APM Edge transformer-monitoring appliances (CVE-2026-43284 and CVE-2026-43500, CVSS 8.8 and 7.8, with patch status unresolved and sources conflicting on firmware availability), and the Siemens License Server (CVE-2026-69109 and CVE-2026-69108, roughly CVSS 7.5, fixed). None of the 15 is known to be exploited. The volume itself is the point: a single-day batch this size is routine for August Patch Tuesday, but it means every OT security team, including rail infrastructure and rolling-stock groups, is triaging more than a dozen vendor advisories against their own asset inventory in the same week.

High Research — Exposure

Forescout counted 4,407 exposed Rockwell controllers worldwide — the same discontinued families rail depots still run

Forescout Vedere Labs scanned for internet-reachable Rockwell Automation and Allen-Bradley controllers and found 4,407 worldwide, 2,844 of them in the United States, dominated by discontinued legacy MicroLogix 1400 and 1100 units — the same product family targeted in the ongoing water-sector campaign, and 22 of the exposed devices Forescout found sit in cities already hit by that campaign. Forescout found the intruders reconfiguring devices directly, using vendor engineering software rather than exploiting a new vulnerability. Rail infrastructure and yard-equipment teams commonly run the same discontinued MicroLogix and CompactLogix families for depot power monitoring and points and signal auxiliary equipment, kept in service well past their support window because replacement means a possession. The finding is not that rail has been targeted; it is that the same reachable, out-of-support controller class this campaign is walking through is common wherever legacy Rockwell gear was never pulled off the internet.

Active mass scanning documented; no new vulnerability, reconfiguration via vendor engineering tools Not CVE-specific — exposure and legacy-device count
No patch — remove from internet exposure, replace or isolate end-of-support units Action: Run an external scan of your own address space for EtherNet/IP and similar PLC-programming protocols, specifically targeting depot, yard and points/signal auxiliary equipment where legacy MicroLogix or CompactLogix controllers are most likely to have been left reachable. Where replacement isn’t feasible before the next possession window, pull the device off any routable network today.

High Research — Ransomware

Dragos’s Q2 count puts rail at just 3 ransomware incidents — and confirms why: attacks stop at IT, not the signalling system

Dragos’s Q2 2026 industrial ransomware analysis recorded 1,140 incidents against industrial organisations worldwide, up 12% on Q1. Manufacturing absorbed 747 of them, roughly two-thirds; transportation and logistics accounted for 95 in total, split across maritime (8), aviation (6) and rail (3). No rail victim was named, and Dragos observed no case across any sector this quarter where ransomware operators reached Stage 2 of the ICS Cyber Kill Chain or directly manipulated a control system — disruption cascaded from IT encryption instead, most often through virtualisation and enterprise infrastructure. Rail’s low count this quarter is genuinely thin data rather than genuine immunity: three incidents worldwide is not a trend, and the pattern Dragos documents elsewhere — ticketing, dispatch support and passenger information going down through the same enterprise-IT path — is exactly the failure mode a railroad or transit agency should plan around even without a rail-specific incident to point to.

Quarterly incident data; no confirmed control-system manipulation observed in any sector this quarter Not vulnerability-specific
Not applicable — dependency mapping, segmentation and degraded-operations planning Action: Map what a normal timetable actually depends on in your IT estate: dispatch support systems, crew rostering, passenger information, ticketing, maintenance management, and the virtualisation hosts underneath them. Then write down, before an incident forces it, what degraded service you can run with signalling intact but that whole layer unavailable — and rehearse the crew-rostering piece specifically, since it is consistently the constraint that decides whether trains run at all.

Low Policy — Water & wastewater

Congress moves to fund water-utility cybersecurity after the PLC campaign — rail has no equivalent bill

Senators Klobuchar and Schiff introduced the Water Cyber Shield Act on 13 August, responding directly to the multi-state PLC campaign against water utilities: it would give EPA authority to require cybersecurity assessments, fund $300 million a year in state revolving loan funds, and add a cyber-risk requirement to resilience planning for large water systems. New York separately awarded $9 million in SECURE grants to 153 drinking-water and wastewater systems, up to $50,000 for assessments and $100,000 for improvements per system. Rail has no comparable legislative funding mechanism on the table this window. The industry operates under existing TSA rail cybersecurity requirements that impose reporting and mitigation obligations, but nothing resembling a dedicated federal or state grant programme sized for smaller freight short lines and transit agencies the way this bill targets small water utilities. Whether Congress extends the same model to rail, given the parallel exposure argument — legacy PLCs and general-purpose controllers reachable from the internet at resource-constrained operators — is worth watching rather than assuming.
