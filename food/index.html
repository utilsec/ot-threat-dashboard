The argument that a food-sector cyber incident is a public-health problem, not just a production one

Industrial Cyber published an analysis on 8 September making a case that is easy to nod along to and hard to act on: in food and agriculture, the systems attackers reach are the systems that keep product safe. Pasteurisation and cook control, chemical dosing in wash and CIP steps, cold-chain temperature holds and batch and lot integrity are all process-control functions, so an incident that manipulates or merely casts doubt on them becomes a food-safety and potentially a public-health event rather than a downtime event. The piece attributes the sector’s exposure to structural factors rather than negligence — thin margins that starve OT security budgets, heavy dependence on co-manufacturers and co-packers, and plant networks that are lightly monitored compared with corporate IT. This is analysis and argument, not a report of an incident.

The routers joining a processing plant, a distribution centre and head office are usually run by corporate IT or a provider — and the logs that would show tampering come from those same devices, which is precisely what this actor set out to make unreliable.
