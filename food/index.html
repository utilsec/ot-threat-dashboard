Top Story

Researchers took root on the refrigeration controllers behind cold stores and supermarkets — then turned the cooling off while the display kept reading normal

Claroty’s Team82 published research on 11–12 August covering two of the most widely deployed refrigeration supervisory platforms in food retail, cold storage and warehousing: the Copeland XWEB Pro family and the Danfoss AK-SM 800A. They reported 23 CVEs in Copeland XWEB Pro, 21 of them rated high severity, plus three in the Danfoss controller; CVE-2026-24452 and CVE-2026-25109 are among the identifiers. Several of the issues allow unauthenticated, root-level remote code execution on the controller. The finding that makes this a food-safety story rather than an IT one is the demonstrated scenario: an attacker disables refrigeration while the temperature display continues to show normal readings, so the supervisory system that would normally raise the alarm is the same system telling you everything is fine. This is coordinated disclosure — both vendors have shipped fixes and no exploitation has been reported.

Cold Chain The instrument lies while the process drifts

Claroty’s refrigeration research did not stop at root access — the researchers disabled cooling while the temperature display kept reading normally. That has landed hard in food circles, because most cold-chain assurance rests on the same supervisory system that would be compromised, and an independent temperature record is suddenly a much easier control to justify.

Ownership Nobody in the room owns the refrigeration rack

Between the Copeland and Danfoss findings and this month’s building-automation advisories for Siemens Desigo and Johnson Controls Metasys, the same question keeps surfacing: who patches the systems that keep the plant cold, lit and locked? In most food sites they belong to facilities or a service contractor, sit on the same infrastructure as production, and appear in no controls patch schedule.

Medium Incident — Food & beverage (July, resolved)

July recap: Coca-Cola’s fairlife suspended US dairy production for a ransomware incident, and had most capacity back inside two weeks

Carried here as context, not as news — this is a July event and nothing has changed on it since 27 July. Coca-Cola confirmed that its fairlife dairy business suspended production across four US sites after a ransomware intrusion detected in mid-July; Canadian operations were unaffected. The Anubis group claimed it had encrypted servers and taken roughly a terabyte of data. Coca-Cola reported most capacity restored by the end of the month and said it did not expect a material financial impact. The reason it stays on the page is the shape of the outage rather than its scale: what had to be rebuilt was the IT that plans, schedules, documents and releases production, not the control systems on the lines themselves. That is the same pattern Dragos describes across the whole quarter.

Identify which of those a line legally cannot run without — for most food plants that is the quality release and traceability record, and it sets your true recovery clock.

High Incident — Distribution

Eight Ceva Logistics warehouses stopped fulfilling for a week, and the delays reached retailers downstream

A cyberattack on Ceva Logistics between 29 July and 1 August disrupted contract logistics at eight European warehouses, delaying fulfilment for retail and e-commerce customers including Bol, De Bijenkorf and Ace & Tate; operations were still not fully restored by 6 August. Stolen data covers customer names, home addresses, email addresses, phone numbers and order details, with no payment data or credentials taken according to Ceva, and the Dutch data protection authority is investigating. No food business is named, but the pattern is the one that matters to this sector: third-party warehousing and transport is where most food companies’ finished goods sit, and a week of unavailable fulfilment against short-dated stock is spoilage, not just delay.

Confirmed incident — multi-site operational disruption and data theft No CVE disclosed — entry vector not made public
Not applicable — third-party resilience and contractual expectations Action: List the third-party cold-store and distribution partners holding your short-dated stock, and establish for each what happens if their systems are down for a week: can product be located, released and moved on paper, and who authorises disposition. Put a notification expectation in the contract if there is not one already.

Medium Research — Ransomware trends

Food and beverage took 70 industrial ransomware incidents last quarter — and not one attacker reached a control system

Dragos recorded 1,140 ransomware incidents against industrial organisations in the second quarter of 2026, up 12 percent on the 1,020 it counted in the first. Food and beverage accounted for 70 of them, within a total dominated by manufacturing at 747. The number worth carrying into a management conversation is a zero: Dragos found no case in the quarter where a ransomware operator reached Stage 2 of the ICS Cyber Kill Chain or directly manipulated a control system. Its food-sector worked example is Mackay Sugar in Australia, where two mills stopped in June with no evidence that attackers reached ICS. Production halts in this data set cascade from virtualisation and ERP compromise, which is precisely why the vCenter and remote-access items further down this page matter more to a plant than their CVSS scores suggest.

High Threat Intel — Ransomware

Medusa has passed 500 critical-infrastructure victims, and it is weaponising new vulnerabilities within a day

CISA and the FBI refreshed their #StopRansomware advisory on Medusa on 18 August. The cumulative victim count across critical infrastructure passed 500 as of April 2026, up from roughly 300 in the March 2025 version — about 200 new victims in a year. Named target sectors include critical manufacturing, healthcare, the defense industrial base, government facilities, IT and financial services; the impact described is enterprise-IT encryption plus data theft, with no control-system involvement alleged. The operationally useful finding is speed: the group weaponises newly disclosed vulnerabilities within roughly 24 hours, and has been observed using exploits up to a week before public disclosure, because it buys them rather than developing them. Any exposure window measured in weeks of change control is therefore a real one.

Confirmed active campaign — federal advisory, 500+ critical-infrastructure victims No single CVE — rapid weaponisation of newly disclosed flaws
Not applicable — patch velocity, exposure reduction and detection content Action: List the internet-facing devices that carry access into any plant — VPN concentrators, remote-support gateways, jump hosts, OEM appliances — and agree in advance who can take one out of service within 24 hours of an advisory without waiting for a maintenance window. Then run the advisory’s indicators across the estate.

High Threat Intel — Nation-state

The Iranian-affiliated PLC campaign is cooling, and no food or agriculture operator has been confirmed as a victim

Joint advisory AA26-097A, issued 7 April by the FBI, CISA, NSA, EPA, DOE, US Cyber Command’s Cyber National Mission Force and Treasury and expanded on 22 July, describes Iranian-affiliated actors reaching internet-exposed programmable logic controllers across US critical infrastructure. Confirmed victims remain water systems — at least 12 states, with more than 30 in Minnesota — and there has been no new confirmed victim since 14 August, no new named utility and no escalation. No food processor, grain co-operative or agricultural operator has been confirmed compromised. It stays on this page for one reason: no vulnerability is being exploited. The way in is a controller answering from the internet with a default, shared or absent password, and remote food and agriculture assets — outlying elevators, irrigation controls, cellular-connected pump and dryer skids — are exactly where that configuration survives.

Confirmed active campaign, cooling — no new confirmed victim since 14 Aug; none in food or agriculture No CVE exploited — internet-exposed controllers with weak or default credentials
No patch — exposure removal, credential hygiene and logic-integrity verification Action: Scan every public address and cellular range your business owns — including outlying elevators, pump stations, irrigation controls and leased storage — for controllers and HMIs that answer. Then diff the logic on anything implementing a safety shutdown, high-level alarm or dosing interlock against your last known-good baseline.
Suggested SLA: External exposure sweep and safety-logic baseline comparison — 30 days Owner:

Critical Advisory — HMI gateway

A CVSS 10.0 command injection gives unauthenticated root on Haiwell’s IoT Cloud HMI gateway

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-02 covers CVE-2026-19188, an OS command injection in the Haiwell IoT Cloud HMI Gateway rated 10.0. The Net Check feature reached through the /setting endpoint passes user input from the cmdPing Socket.io event straight to the operating system, letting an unauthenticated remote attacker run commands as root. Version 3.40.1.12 is affected; Haiwell has published Scada-v3.50.1.19. Gateways of this class reach food sites inside packaged equipment — a filler, a depalletiser, a blast chiller, a grain dryer, an irrigation controller — specified by the machine builder rather than by a controls standard, connected for remote support, and rarely present in any asset register.

No known exploitation reported as of this edition Not in CISA KEV
Patch available — Haiwell Scada-v3.50.1.19 Action: Scan for Haiwell devices rather than trusting the asset list, since these arrive inside packaged machinery. Update to Scada-v3.50.1.19 and, whatever the version, confirm nothing answers from a public or cellular-carrier address — the same check that protects you against the campaign in the top story.

Critical Advisory — Siemens

SIMATIC IoT2050 Advanced exposes Node-RED with no authentication — maximum-severity remote code execution

Siemens ProductCERT advisory SSA-834709, published 11 August, covers SIMATIC IoT2050 Advanced devices running Industrial OS with Node-RED installed. CVE-2026-58115 is rated CVSS 10.0 under both v3.1 and v4.0: the Node-RED HTTP interface enforces no authentication at all, so a remote unauthenticated attacker can create flows using programming nodes that execute system commands, giving arbitrary code execution at maximum privilege on the gateway. Every version before V4.3.4.1 is affected; the alternatives to updating are uninstalling Node-RED or hardening it per Node-RED’s own guidance. Worth noting that this one has no corresponding CISA ICSA advisory, so a team that tracks only ICS-CERT will not see it. In food and beverage these gateways typically arrive as part of an OEE, downtime-tracking or digitalisation project, bridging line equipment to a reporting platform — trusted by the control network, installed by an integrator, and almost never included in a patch schedule afterwards.

No known exploitation reported as of this edition Not in CISA KEV
Patch available — SIMATIC IoT2050 Advanced V4.3.4.1 Action: Identify every IoT2050 on site, check whether Node-RED is installed, and restrict its HTTP interface to a dedicated management host. Update to V4.3.4.1; if a production window is needed, block the interface at the network layer today. Ask whoever delivered your OEE project which gateways they left behind.
Suggested SLA: Inventory, network restriction and firmware update — 7 days (Critical) Owner:

High Advisory — Virtualisation

CISA added the exploited vCenter flaw to KEV yesterday — 361 hosts compromised across 47 countries

CISA added CVE-2026-59310, a path-traversal flaw in VMware vCenter Server, to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 18 August. Broadcom patched it on 29 July and exploitation began about five days later. German incident-response firm QUIRSO assesses with moderate confidence that a Chinese-speaking actor operates the campaign, counting around 361 affected addresses across 47 countries. The actor gains root on the vCenter appliance, plants a backdoor through cron and a reverse SSH binary, and in at least one case deployed Babuk-derived ransomware. Most food plants now run the historian, MES, SCADA nodes and label-print servers as virtual machines on one cluster, which makes the management plane a single point of failure for production and for traceability records at the same time.

Confirmed active exploitation — suspected China-nexus actor, ransomware in at least one case In CISA KEV — CVE-2026-59310 added 18 Aug 2026
Patch available — Broadcom fix released 29 Jul 2026 Action: Patch any vCenter hosting historian, MES, quality or labelling virtual machines and check the appliance for unexpected cron entries and a “linuxFile” binary. If plant and business virtual machines share one cluster, raise that as a finding independent of this CVE — it is the reason a single compromise stops a site.
Suggested SLA: vCenter patch, compromise check and management-plane isolation — 7 days (Critical) Owner:

High Advisory — Remote access

A Windows IKE flaw went into CISA’s KEV catalogue with a three-day federal deadline — it sits on the tunnels into your plants

CISA added CVE-2026-33824, a double-free in the Microsoft Windows IKE service extensions (IKEEXT), to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 18 August with a federal remediation deadline of 21 August. A deadline that short signals confirmed exploitation at scale. IKE is the key-exchange half of IPsec, and IPsec is what terminates most site-to-site links and vendor remote access into food plants, distribution centres and cold stores — the tunnels used by machine builders, refrigeration service contractors and corporate IT. Remote code execution on the endpoint terminating those tunnels produces access that reads as ordinary maintenance in the logs, along the exact route that reaches line controls and utility systems. Three other flaws were added the same day, including a VMware vCenter path traversal and a SharePoint authentication bypass.

Confirmed active exploitation — basis for the KEV listing In CISA KEV — CVE-2026-33824 added 18 Aug 2026, federal deadline 21 Aug
Patch available — apply the Microsoft update Action: Find every Windows host running the IKE extensions that terminates an IPsec tunnel to a site, a co-packer, a cold store or an OEM, and treat it as an emergency change this week. Patch, then rotate the credentials and pre-shared keys on those tunnels and review authentication logs for sessions outside an approved maintenance window.
Suggested SLA: Patch and credential rotation on IPsec endpoints reaching OT — 7 days (Critical) Owner:

Medium Advisory — Building automation

Two building-automation advisories land on the systems that share infrastructure with plant refrigeration

Two of CISA’s 13 August advisories cover controls that in food plants and cold stores often sit on the same network and the same service contract as refrigeration. ICSA-26-225-08 covers Siemens Desigo DXR2 and PXC3, PXC4, PXC5 and PXC7 controllers: CVE-2026-59693, CVSS 4.3, lets malformed BACnet packets from an adjacent network crash the controller until it is reset or rebooted — a low score with an availability consequence in a facility where a controller reboot means an unmonitored space. Patched firmware is available for every affected model. ICSA-26-225-14 covers Johnson Controls Metasys, where CVE-2026-34491 (CVSS 8.0) is a persistent cross-site scripting flaw letting a low-privilege user plant a payload that runs in an administrator’s browser. Metasys 15 is patched at 15.0.1 and 16.0 ships fixed; versions 12 and 13 are end-of-support and can only be resolved by upgrading.

No known exploitation reported as of this edition Not in CISA KEV
Patches available — Desigo firmware updates; Metasys 14.1.5 / 15.0.1, upgrade from 12 and 13 Action: Establish whether your BMS shares a network segment, a jump host or a service contractor with refrigeration and process utilities — in most food sites it does at least one of the three. Get any Metasys 12 or 13 onto a supported release plan, and keep BACnet traffic off any segment reachable from a general-purpose workstation.

High Advisory — OT monitoring

Six flaws in Malcolm — CISA’s own OT traffic-analysis suite, and it usually sits inside the plant network

CISA published ICSA-26-230-01 on 18 August against Malcolm, its own open-source network-traffic-analysis suite for OT. Six flaws are listed, led by CVE-2026-55676 at CVSS 8.8, an unrestricted PHP file upload leading to arbitrary code execution; the rest cover path traversal on archive extraction (CVE-2026-63177, 7.1), two authorisation bypasses via unnormalised URI paths and percent-encoding, and resource-exhaustion denial of service. Versions below 26.06.1 and 26.07.0 and up to 26.07.1 are affected, with fixes in 26.07.0 and 26.08.0 depending on the CVE. There is no reported exploitation. It earns a place here because of where the software lives: food producers running a low-cost OT monitoring capability typically deploy Malcolm inside the plant network with a SPAN or TAP feed, which means broad visibility and a trusted position.

No known exploitation reported as of this edition Not in CISA KEV
Patch available — Malcolm 26.07.0 and 26.08.0 depending on the CVE Action: If anyone in your organisation stood up Malcolm for OT visibility — often a plant engineer rather than IT — update it and restrict its web interface to a named management host. A monitoring appliance with a SPAN feed sees everything on the segment, so treat its own exposure with the same seriousness as an HMI.

Low Policy — National strategy

The new National Security Science and Technology Strategy names OT and ICS resilience as an explicit priority

The National Security Science and Technology Strategy was released on 19 August. Across its four pillars it names operational technology and industrial control system resilience, and technology supply chains, as explicit national priorities rather than leaving them inside a general cybersecurity heading. There is no regulatory requirement in it and nothing for an operator to comply with; it is a statement of research and investment direction. For food and agriculture the relevance is indirect but real — sector-specific OT security funding, research programmes and grant criteria tend to follow the language in documents like this one, and a strategy that separates OT resilience from IT security is easier to point at when arguing for a control-system budget line.
