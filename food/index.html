Info Threat Intelligence & Research — Analysis, engineering practice

Cyber-informed engineering argues the useful question is not how to keep attackers out, but which consequences you can design away

An analysis published 15 September sets out the case for cyber-informed engineering in critical infrastructure: that industrial systems operate under a constraint IT does not share, because a compromised network does not automatically have to become a catastrophic physical event. The argument is to move the security question upstream into design — identifying the physical consequences that actually matter, then engineering them out with controls that do not depend on the digital system remaining trustworthy. For food and beverage the consequence is a safety one, not just a production one: a compromised control system that produces an out-of-spec batch or a cold-chain excursion becomes a public-health problem. The argument is that engineered limits — mechanical stops, independent temperature cut-outs, physical verification steps — do more than any detection tool. This is practitioner analysis rather than incident reporting; it names no victim and describes no attack, but it is the most useful lens available for operators whose detection budget will never match their consequence exposure.

CISA added CVE-2026-8452 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 26 August, moving the Citrix NetScaler ADC and Gateway issue from a patching recommendation to confirmed exploitation in the wild. It is a heap overflow in SAML single sign-on parsing, rated CVSS 8.8, requiring no authentication, and reachable when the appliance is configured as a Gateway or AAA virtual server. Canada’s Cyber Centre reported indications of exploitation from around 17 August after a public proof of concept. In food manufacturing NetScaler commonly fronts the remote access used by plant engineers, out-of-hours support and the OEMs who dial in to fillers, packaging lines and refrigeration. Exploitation has not tailed off: reporting on 5 September describes continued attempts against the authentication bypass from multiple geolocations, so an appliance patched late should be treated as possibly already touched rather than simply late.

Info Guidance — Detection method for critical infrastructure networks

CISA makes the case for decoys — a detection method you can add without touching the process

CISA published “Using Cyber Decoys to Strengthen Detection and Response” on 17 September. The premise is unsentimental: assume an attacker will eventually obtain some access, and plant assets whose only purpose is to be touched by someone who should not be there. The guide covers tripwires, breadcrumbs and honeytokens, positions them alongside Zero Trust rather than instead of it, and structures the work in three phases — preparation, execution and analysis — to be iterated with red-team exercises. Planning is framed around the three MITRE Engage goals of expose, affect and elicit, with detections mapped to ATT&CK and deliberate attention to the living-off-the-land activity that signature-based tooling misses. Industrial Cyber’s coverage reports a detailed water and wastewater case study in the document, including decoys placed to look like OT HMIs; SecurityWeek’s write-up does not mention water or OT, so treat the OT case study as that outlet’s reporting until you have read the PDF. The reason this suits food and beverage plants is the one thing decoys do not require: a change to a live control system. A fake historian share, a false engineering-workstation credential or an HMI-looking host on the plant VLAN costs no production risk and no vendor sign-off.

Guidance publication — CISA document released 17 Sep 2026; no exploitation or incident claim attaches to it No CVE — this is detection guidance, not a product vulnerability
No patch applies — the work is planning, placement and, above all, making sure someone answers the alert Action: Pick three placements you can stand up without a change window and without touching a controller. A decoy file share named as though it held HACCP records, recipe or batch documentation; a set of credentials that look like engineering-workstation or historian logins, used nowhere and alerting on any use; and a host on the plant VLAN that answers like an HMI. Each one is silent until someone who should not be there finds it, which is what makes them worth having on a network where you cannot deploy an agent. Then do the part that decides whether this is useful: decide who receives the alert out of hours, what they do in the first ten minutes, and how it is distinguished from an engineer poking around. Rehearse it once before harvest peak, and read the CISA document itself if you want the water and wastewater case study, which is where the OT examples are reported to sit.

CISA retires its weekly vulnerability bulletin on 28 September — check whether your process reads it

CISA is discontinuing its weekly Vulnerability Summary bulletin with effect from 28 September 2026, reported on 17 September. The Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, alerts and advisories all continue; the change is framed as consistent with the risk-based prioritisation set out in Binding Operational Directive 26-04 in June 2026. Nothing in the reporting indicates that ICS advisories are affected. For food and beverage operators the practical question is narrow and easy to check: does anything in your vulnerability-management routine, or in a managed service provider’s, consume that weekly bulletin as a feed or as the trigger for a review meeting? Small and mid-sized processors in particular tend to have one person who reads a weekly digest rather than a subscription to per-advisory feeds, and a source that stops arriving quietly is worse than one that stops loudly.

Info Regulation & Policy — EU, now in force

EU Cyber Resilience Act reporting went live on 11 September — your line and refrigeration vendors now have 24 hours

The vulnerability and incident reporting obligations of the EU Cyber Resilience Act took effect on 11 September 2026. Manufacturers of products with digital elements must now notify an actively exploited vulnerability or a severe security incident through ENISA’s Single Reporting Platform within 24 hours of becoming aware, follow up within 72 hours with exploit and mitigation detail, and file a final report within 14 days of a fix becoming available, as well as informing affected users. It applies to manufacturers placing products on the EU market wherever they are based, with penalties up to €15 million. For a food or beverage processor this cuts two ways. Your filling, packaging, palletising, refrigeration and ag-IoT suppliers — many of them European — are now on a 24-hour clock, so you should hear about exploited flaws sooner. And if your group builds or badges any connected product sold into Europe, the obligation may apply to you directly.

Info Policy — Incident reporting burden, CIRCIA rules pending

117 federal cyber incident reporting rules, and the argument that they pull staff off the response

The McCrary Institute and the US Chamber of Commerce published analysis on 8 September counting, from GAO figures, 117 federal cyber incident reporting regulations spread across 27 agencies, 48 of which apply to private industry. Their argument is operational rather than political: in the first hours of an incident, the people who understand the affected systems are the same people drafting duplicate notifications in different formats to different regulators on different clocks. The timing matters because CIRCIA’s implementing rules are expected to be finalised this autumn. A food or agriculture business feels this acutely if it also handles payment card data, holds regulated financial or health information, ships product to state agencies, or operates in the EU alongside NIS2 and Cyber Resilience Act duties — a single ransomware event at a processing site can trigger several clocks at once.

Not applicable — policy analysis on regulatory structure; no incident or exploitation involved No CVE
No patch applies — the practical response is preparing the reporting workflow in advance Action: Build the notification matrix before you need it rather than during an incident. List every reporting obligation your business actually carries — sector regulators, contractual customer notification, cyber insurance, state breach law, EU obligations if you sell there — and against each one record the trigger, the clock, the recipient and the format. Then assign the drafting to someone who is not on the technical response: a communications or legal owner working from a prepared template, with the incident commander supplying facts, keeps your engineers on the plant floor. Rehearse it once in a tabletop with a realistic scenario, such as ransomware on the MES during a production run, and time how long the first notification takes. Track the CIRCIA final rule when it lands and update the matrix rather than rebuilding it.

Info Policy — Joint international guidance, incident communications

Six agencies publish outage-communication guidance for IT and OT service providers — and a processor is one to its retailers

On 2 September the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security joined CISA, Australia’s ASD ACSC, NCSC-NZ, NCSC-UK and the FBI to publish joint guidance on communicating during IT and OT service outages, titled “Communicating Under Pressure: Best Practices for Service Providers.” It asks for a written incident communications plan with defined trigger thresholds and named audiences, plain-language updates, root-cause and technical detail end users can act on, and messaging aligned with legal and regulatory duties.
