High Threat Intel — Ransomware tradecraft, AI-assisted intrusion

A ransomware affiliate ran an AI coding agent inside victim networks — and the exposed server shows exactly how

CloudSEK published analysis on 27 August of a misconfigured server belonging to a Russian-speaking Aurora ransomware affiliate, exposing tooling, command history, credential material and the operator’s Cursor chat transcripts. The campaign ran from April to July 2026 across nine countries; the operator reached domain-level or interactive access at 17 organisations, and four appeared on Aurora’s leak site. Food, agriculture, manufacturing and professional-services organisations were among those affected, which puts this page’s audience squarely in the targeting set. The notable detail is not that AI was used but where: the transcripts show the assistant helping plan Active Directory escalation and intrusion steps, compressing the time between initial access and domain control. For a processor the useful question is whether the domain account your IT team uses every day can also authenticate to the MES, the batch server or a line HMI, because this operator reached victims through domain privilege rather than any control-system exploit.

Critical Threat intel — Refrigeration controller research with vendor advisories

Refrigeration controllers can be switched off while the temperature display keeps reading normal

Claroty’s Team82 published research into commercial refrigeration controllers, with corresponding advisories from Danfoss and Copeland. The finding that matters to a food business is not remote access in itself but the divergence between what the controller does and what the operator sees: cooling can be disabled or setpoints altered while the display continues to report a normal temperature. That is a food-safety failure mode dressed as a security finding. Every HACCP monitoring assumption you hold rests on the reading being true, and a compromised controller breaks the assumption silently — there is no alarm, because the system reporting the alarm is the system that was changed.

No known exploitation reported as of this edition — the research demonstrates the technique and vendors have issued advisories Not in CISA KEV as of this edition
Vendor advisories published — Danfoss and Copeland have issued guidance and updates for affected controller families Action: Apply the Danfoss and Copeland updates to cold store, blast chiller, display case and process refrigeration controllers, and remove any that answer from the internet — remote monitoring contracts are the usual reason one does. Then address the trust problem directly: introduce at least one temperature verification that does not come from the same controller, whether an independent logger, a manual check on a defined schedule, or a separate monitoring system. Where a controller is under a supplier monitoring contract, ask in writing how that connection is authenticated and who else can reach it.

Medium Advisory — Fuel management at farms, depots and processing sites

Fuel-Boss ships with end-of-life web components — a reminder of what sits behind the farm and depot fuel island

CISA’s 27 August batch includes All-Line Equipment Company’s Fuel-Boss, the fuel management and dispensing control system used at agricultural co-operatives, grain and feed operations, trucking depots and processing sites to authorise and log fuel draws. The advisory documents two flaws inherited from bundled end-of-life components: an argument-injection issue in the University of Washington IMAP toolkit as reached through PHP’s imap_open(), which allows remote command execution, and a buffer-handling flaw in PHP-FPM configurations up to and including PHP 7.1.5 that can produce a remote code execution condition. Neither is exotic; both are the predictable result of an embedded web stack that stopped receiving updates years ago. For a food and agriculture operator the exposure is practical rather than dramatic — fuel dispatch controls harvest logistics and cold-chain haulage, and an unavailable fuel island stops trucks.

No known exploitation reported as of this edition — the underlying component flaws are long-known and publicly documented Not in CISA KEV as of this edition
Mitigations published — CISA’s guidance is to remove these systems from internet exposure and reach them only through a maintained VPN or gateway Action: Search your external footprint for fuel management systems before anything else — these devices are frequently installed by a fuel supplier or equipment dealer with a cellular router and a port forward so they can support it remotely, and they are often invisible to whoever owns the rest of the network. Anything answering from the internet comes off now. Where remote support is genuinely needed, put it behind a maintained VPN with named accounts, and ask the vendor in writing what their plan is for the embedded PHP stack. Then include the fuel island in your continuity planning: know how you authorise and record a fuel draw manually, and make sure someone on each shift has done it.

Critical Advisory — Actively exploited, CISA KEV, historian and MES databases

A seven-year-old SQL Server flaw is now confirmed exploited — and it is the database under your historian

CISA added six vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 26 August, and the one that matters most on the plant floor is CVE-2019-1068, a remote code execution flaw in Microsoft SQL Server patched in July 2019 and only now confirmed as exploited in the wild. Successful exploitation runs code under the SQL Server Database Engine service account; where that account is over-privileged — which is common on systems installed by an integrator years ago — the attacker moves off the database and onto the Windows host. In a processing plant this is the database under the MES, the batch and recipe store, the traceability and lot-genealogy system, and the food-safety records you would produce in a recall — often SQL Server instances a line integrator installed and left at the build they shipped with, on the same flat network as the HMIs. No public write-up of the in-the-wild technique has appeared, so detection guidance is thin; the federal remediation deadline is 29 August. Citrix NetScaler CVE-2026-8452 was added in the same batch.

Critical Advisory — Backup and monitoring infrastructure, unauthenticated

Two unauthenticated flaws in Veeam ONE 13 — the monitoring layer that watches the backups you would restore from

Veeam disclosed vulnerabilities in Veeam ONE 13 in knowledge-base articles published on 25 August. CVE-2026-64633 is rated CVSS v4.0 10.0 and allows remote, unauthenticated code execution on the Veeam ONE agent host; CVE-2026-65641 is rated 9.3 and lets a remote unauthenticated attacker coerce SMB authentication out of the Veeam ONE service account, which is the first step in relaying that account somewhere more useful. Veeam ONE 13.x is affected and the 12.x line is not. In food and beverage Veeam ONE typically watches backups of the MES, the recipe and batch servers and the engineering workstations — the recovery capability that decides whether a ransomware event costs a shift or a production week, in a sector where just-in-time delivery leaves no slack. There is no report of exploitation yet, but backup and monitoring servers are a standing target precisely because compromising them removes the recovery option before the ransomware runs.

No confirmed exploitation reported — vendor-disclosed, and not currently listed in CISA KEV Not in CISA KEV — CVE-2026-64633 (CVSS v4.0 10.0) and CVE-2026-65641 (9.3), vendor-rated
Patched — Veeam ONE 13.1 Patch 0 build 13.1.0.7233, or 13.0.2 Patch 1 build 13.0.2.7159 Action: Confirm whether Veeam ONE 13.x is deployed, including any instance a systems integrator runs for you; the 12.x line is unaffected. Patch to 13.1 Patch 0 or 13.0.2 Patch 1. Verify the Veeam ONE server has no path to or from the process network and that its service account is not a domain administrator, since the SMB coercion issue only matters when the coerced identity is privileged. Info Policy — EU Cyber Resilience Act reporting obligation

From 11 September 2026 exploited vulnerabilities in digital products must be reported within 24 hours

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s reporting obligations begin on 11 September 2026. Manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must notify an actively exploited vulnerability in their product, or a severe incident affecting its security, within 24 hours of becoming aware, with fuller reporting to follow. Food and agriculture businesses are mostly buyers here, and that is the useful angle: the suppliers of fillers, packaging lines, refrigeration controllers, weighing systems and ag-IoT platforms sold into Europe acquire a defined notification duty that can be written into support contracts.
