Forced-defrost attack hits refrigeration at about 700 French supermarkets; CERT-FR says campaigns are probably still ongoing

Over the nights of 11–13 October, attackers remotely took over web-managed refrigeration controllers in French supermarkets and forced continuous or extended defrost, pushing some cabinets to about 50°C and spoiling stock; the Paris prosecutor reports fires breaking out. Finance Minister Roland Lescure told Parliament on 13 October that about 700 of France’s 10,000 or so supermarkets were hit, and Carrefour, Coopérative U, E.Leclerc, Intermarché/Netto and Monoprix have confirmed impact. CERT-FR’s alert CERTFR-2026-ALE-012 named Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC controllers on 12 October, added Copeland and Dixell xweb supervisors on 13 October, and in a 16 October update said attack campaigns are “probably still ongoing.” Danfoss says most affected units are end-of-life AK-SM 800 and AK-SC 355 models or AK-SM 800A units on old firmware. No group has claimed the attacks, no ransom was demanded, and the entry route has not been established; the prosecutor’s cybercrime section is investigating.

Confirmed attack — CERT-FR reports numerous compromises, the ~700-store figure is the Finance Minister’s, five chains confirm impact, and the Paris prosecutor’s cybercrime section opened an inquiry on its own initiative (assigned to OFAC). Confirmed vs claimed: fires are the prosecutor’s report; Le Monde’s account of defrost cycles stretched from about one hour to as many as eight, delaying alarms, is press reporting; Intermarché/Netto store counts differ (about 150 per Les Mousquetaires via SecurityWeek, about 300 per AFP); stores hit in Spain, Portugal, Poland, Germany and Belgium are Sekoia’s statement to Reuters and no foreign authority has confirmed them; no actor, entry route or CVE has been named No CVE or indicators in the CERT-FR alert — Claroty Team82’s August AK-SM 800A flaws (CVE-2025-41450, -41451, -41452) have not been linked to the attack by ANSSI or Danfoss; not in CISA KEV

Ahold Delhaize cyber incident stopped work at some Albert Heijn distribution centres; shelves are being restocked, the online store is still down

Ahold Delhaize detected a security issue on Sunday 11 October and switched systems off as a precaution. High Active campaign — FortiGate firewall and SSL VPN credential theft (FBI/USSS); food and agriculture among targeted sectors; no CVE

FBI and Secret Service: the FortiBleed credential-theft campaign against Fortinet firewalls is still active, and food and agriculture is among its targets

The FBI and US Secret Service warned on Tuesday 6 October that the FortiBleed campaign against internet-facing Fortinet FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPNs remains active. There is no CVE: the attackers reuse leaked or stolen credentials, crack passwords kept in legacy SHA-256 storage and intercept authentication traffic, then add admin accounts and sometimes delete or reset the owners’ accounts, locking them out. The agencies say the targets span all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors, including energy and food and agriculture, and the FBI links the access to Payload ransomware; SOCRadar says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments from it. SOCRadar’s figure of 86,644 working device credentials across 194 countries dates from 19 June and is not a new count. No compromise of an OT system has been described. Editorial assessment: FortiGate firewalls often sit at the IT/OT boundary of processing plants and cold stores, so a hijacked admin account there can rewrite the segmentation that protects production.

Confirmed active campaign — FBI/USSS joint advisory; the SOCRadar figures and the Russian-speaking access-broker profile are researcher assessments, not agency attribution No CVE — credential abuse, not a product flaw
No patch — move admin credentials to PBKDF2 storage (FortiOS 7.2.11+), reset passwords and add MFA Action: Restrict FortiGate management to a dedicated admin network, end active sessions and reset VPN and admin passwords, enforce phishing-resistant MFA on SSL VPN, review admin accounts and API keys for unfamiliar names (reported examples include fortiAdmin and forticloud-sync), and move admin credentials to PBKDF2 storage.

Medium Research — AI-assisted exploit reproduction against Danfoss refrigeration controllers (Claroty Team82); lab test only

Claroty: an AI coding agent rebuilt a root exploit for Danfoss refrigeration controllers in about 28 minutes

Prompted by the French attacks, Claroty’s Team82 reported on 13 October how quickly an AI coding agent could rebuild an exploit for Danfoss AK-SM 800A flaws it had disclosed in August (CVE-2025-41450, -41451 and -41452). With a researcher steering it and a Ghidra integration, the agent reproduced the authentication bypass and chained it with command injection to gain root on a lab unit in about 28 minutes, for roughly US$6 of AI usage, using firmware anyone can download. Its point is that the time between disclosure and a working exploit may be shrinking. Editorial assessment: refrigeration and building controllers often run firmware years out of date, so faster exploit reproduction raises the cost of slow patching.

Medium Research — IoT and edge-device botnet (Nozomi Networks Labs); no OT impact reported

Nozomi: the Cling botnet hides its command traffic in STUN requests and spreads through old router, camera and DVR flaws

Nozomi Networks Labs reported on 1 October that a botnet it calls Cling infects routers, access points, repeaters, DVRs and cameras, mainly through CVE-2021-35394 in the Realtek Jungle SDK, and carries exploits for seven other flaws, including CVE-2014-8361, CVE-2023-26801 and CVE-2024-3721. Infected devices contact public STUN servers, and the operators send commands encoded in STUN transaction IDs while spoofing source addresses so the traffic appears to come from Google’s STUN service. Nozomi saw the bots used for denial-of-service attacks on an internet provider, a university computing cluster and game servers; it gives no victim count and reports no OT or ICS compromise. Its advice is to patch the listed flaws, restrict inbound access and watch for unusual STUN patterns. Editorial assessment: low-cost routers, DVRs and cameras are common at farms, packhouses, grain sites and cold-storage warehouses, often on the same flat network as refrigeration or irrigation controllers.

High Advisory — WAGO controllers and CODESYS runtime (CERT@VDE), no known exploitation

CERT@VDE: a CVSS 9.8 flaw in the BACnet stack of WAGO controllers, plus CODESYS runtime fixes for WAGO and other PLCs

CERT@VDE published WAGO advisory VDE-2025-102 on 1 October for CVE-2025-41753 (CVSS 9.8), a path-traversal flaw in the BACnet/IP stack: by giving BACnet File Objects names that act as file paths, an unauthenticated attacker can read or overwrite arbitrary files, which can lead to full compromise of the device. It affects 750-811x, 750-821x, 751-9x01 and 752-8303 controllers and 762-series panels on firmware below 4.8.9 (FW30). A second WAGO advisory the same day (VDE-2025-081) covers CODESYS runtime flaws in the same families that let an unauthenticated attacker crash the device (the highest, CVE-2025-41738, is CVSS 8.3), plus a manipulated project file that can run code if a user opens it; the fix is firmware 4.10.0 (FW32). On 30 September CODESYS fixed a runtime race condition, CVE-2026-79625 (CVSS 8.1, needs authenticated monitoring access), in 3.5.22.40, with Linux-based variants due in 4.23.0.0 in the fourth quarter. No exploitation is reported for any of them. Editorial assessment: WAGO controllers and CODESYS-based PLCs are common in food-processing automation, refrigeration plant control and building management, where BACnet is the usual building-systems protocol.

Low Policy — Industry coalition statement on the French attack and its call for a federal OT directive

OT Cybersecurity Coalition: the French attack came from missed basics, and US refrigeration controllers could be next

The Operational Technology Cybersecurity Coalition said in a 14 October statement that failures in basic cybersecurity practice led to a significant incident in France, repeated ANSSI’s advice on VPN-only access, default passwords and firmware updates, and warned that attackers could treat refrigeration controllers in the US as a way in, while noting that no US incidents have been reported. It renewed its 6 October call for CISA to issue a binding operational directive on OT security at federal civilian agencies, with an accountable OT security officer and minimum practices drawn from existing federal guidance, citing a GAO finding that only 7 of 22 large civilian agencies fully met OMB’s networked-device requirements. CISA has announced no directive. A directive would bind federal agencies, not private food companies.

Info Regulation — Federal incident-reporting rule under White House review; covers critical-infrastructure sectors including food and agriculture

CIRCIA final rule reaches White House review; 72-hour incident and 24-hour ransom-payment reporting still awaits its start dates

CISA sent its final rule under the Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act to the White House Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs, which logged it for review on 1 October. The law sets 72-hour reporting of covered cyber incidents and 24-hour reporting of ransom payments for covered entities in the 16 critical-infrastructure sectors, which include food and agriculture, but obligations begin only on dates the final rule sets, and who is covered depends on that rule, whose text is not yet public. The statutory deadline passed in October 2025 and later targets slipped; HIPAA Journal reports publication is expected before the end of the year. The text can still change during review.

NIST released the initial public draft of SP 800-82 Rev. 4, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, on 21 September. The draft broadens sector coverage to building automation, water and wastewater, food and agriculture, freight rail and maritime, plus industrial IoT and cloud convergence. High Guidance — Third-party ICS integrator access (CISA/FBI fact sheet)

CISA and the FBI urge critical-infrastructure operators to put ICS integrators on least privilege and monitored remote access

CISA and the FBI released a fact sheet, dated 23 September and reported on 24 September, on the risks of working with third-party ICS integrators. It cites an FBI-analyzed intrusion at a US industrial automation firm in March–April 2025 in which foreign actors took roughly 800 files, including customer SCADA information, ICS device details and schematics. Recommendations: assess what data integrators hold, apply least privilege, secure and monitor remote connections, keep offline backups, write cybersecurity requirements into contracts, disconnect public-facing devices, and weigh geopolitical factors for foreign-owned providers. Editorial assessment: food plants rely on integrators for line controls, batch systems and refrigeration, so the same exposure applies.
