Top Story

China’s Volt Typhoon used throwaway relay chains to reach critical-infrastructure targets, maritime among them, through management and SD-WAN platforms, Lumen says; no OT access described

Lumen’s Black Lotus Labs published research on 28 October on “Silent Train”, the covert access framework it says the China state-sponsored group Volt Typhoon has used since November 2023. The operators route each session through a short-lived chain of compromised home-office routers, IoT devices and enterprise equipment, built for one target and then discarded; CyberScoop reports one chain was assembled in under two minutes and that sessions average about four hours. Lumen says the aim is to gain and keep access to management and control-plane technology, and it describes zero-day campaigns against N-able N-central (CVE-2025-8875 and CVE-2025-8876), Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN (CVE-2026-20127, CVSS v3.1 10.0 per Cisco) and VeloCloud Orchestrator (CVE-2026-16812), plus Versa Director and, with moderate confidence, Quest KACE. All four CVEs are already in CISA’s KEV catalog (added August 2025, February 2026 and July 2026); none is the VeloCloud flaw on the card below. Lumen lists water, energy, transportation, maritime, rail, aviation and emergency services among the critical-infrastructure sectors with victims and says it has sent hundreds of victim notifications, but it names no victim, gives no count by sector and does not mention ports. It describes no access to OT or industrial control systems and no operational impact, saying only that the pattern is consistent with efforts to pre-position access inside critical infrastructure. No government statement on the findings was found. Editorial assessment: in port, terminal and ship-management networks, the SD-WAN orchestrator, remote-management tool or network-management server can reconfigure every site at once, so it needs the same protection as the OT behind it.

High Incident — State espionage intrusion at a NATO naval shipbuilder; IT network only, no OT impact reported

Russia’s APT28 spent at least two months inside a NATO naval shipbuilder’s IT network, Symantec says; no OT impact reported

Symantec’s Threat Hunter Team reported on 20 October that Swallowtail, the group also tracked as APT28 or Fancy Bear, which the US government links to Russia’s GRU military intelligence, spent at least two months inside the IT network of a naval shipbuilder in a NATO member state. A macro-laden Word attachment opened from Outlook on 24 July installed the Hookedge backdoor. Within a week the operators had a persistent SSH tunnel to their own server, were capturing NTLM credentials and mapping the network, and had loaded a previously undocumented loader whose payload was never recovered. The last activity was seen on 24 September. Symantec found the same group inside a military aircraft-engine maker and a law-enforcement agency, names none of the victims or countries, and does not say data was taken. No OT, ICS or production impact is reported, and Symantec had published no follow-up by 31 October. Editorial assessment: shipyards hold vessel designs and support data, so their business networks are an espionage target in their own right.

High Campaign — Credential theft and credential stuffing against Fortinet firewalls and SSL VPNs, no CVE

FortiGate credential abuse keeps spreading: the FBI says FortiBleed is still active, Hudson Rock reports more leaked configuration secrets, and the n0n group used VPN credential stuffing

In a joint advisory dated 6 October, the FBI and US Secret Service warned that attackers are still scanning internet-facing FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN gateways with credentials gathered earlier in the FortiBleed campaign. No vulnerability is involved: the agencies point to reused or leaked passwords and legacy SHA-256 password storage, and researchers describe offline hash cracking. Intruders have created rogue admin accounts and deleted or reset legitimate ones, locking owners out, and the access is resold; SOCRadar says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments from it. The agencies say targeted organizations span all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors, a set that includes transportation systems. On 27 October Hudson Rock published what it calls FortiBleed 2.0: 1,703 FortiGate configuration reports, almost all stamped 20 February 2026, holding 40,573 cleartext secrets, most of them local-user records but including 1,576 Active Directory bind credentials. It cautions that the 975 new domain names in the data are not confirmed victims, names no sector and does not say how the files were obtained. On 28 October SOCRadar reported that the n0n extortion group, which listed 38 maritime firms this month (see the n0n card), broke into victims through weak or stolen credentials, including credential stuffing against 305 FortiGate SSL-VPN appliances; SOCRadar does not say whether the maritime firms were among those victims. No maritime or OT victim of FortiBleed has been named. Editorial assessment: FortiGate appliances commonly guard links between shipping offices, terminals and remote sites, and a copied configuration exposes the directory and VPN credentials behind them.

Active campaign — the FBI and USSS say it remains an active threat; Hudson Rock’s new data and SOCRadar’s n0n findings are researcher reports; no maritime or OT victim named No CVE — credential abuse, so no CISA KEV entry applies
No patch applicable — credential reset, MFA and modern hash storage Action: Reset FortiGate admin and VPN passwords, end active sessions, enable phishing-resistant MFA, look for unexpected admin accounts (reported names include fortiAdmin, forticloud-sync and fgtsecure), and move admin credentials to PBKDF2 storage (FortiOS 7.2.11 or later). Treat any FortiGate whose configuration may have been copied as exposed and rotate the local-user, LDAP bind, VPN, RADIUS and SMTP secrets stored in it. Confirm no firewall policy between business networks and vessel- or terminal-OT networks was changed.

CISA added CVE-2026-102764 (CVSS v4.0 9.3), a BeyondTrust Remote Support flaw, to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 16 October with a 19 October federal deadline. Editorial assessment: OEMs and service providers use remote-support tools like this to reach vessel and terminal workstations.

Moxa says none of its products is affected, and Rockwell had confirmed none as of 23 October. PHP itself is fixed in 8.2.35, 8.3.36, 8.4.27 and 8.5.12. Critical Advisory — Exploited VPN zero-day chain, three CVEs in CISA KEV, fix for one link unverified

Ivanti Connect Secure: CISA adds two more flaws from the root-access chain to KEV, and the build that fixes the privilege-escalation link is unconfirmed

Ivanti disclosed on 13 October that a chain of flaws gave unauthenticated attackers root on fully patched Connect Secure appliances at a limited number of customers. The first link, CVE-2026-104935 (CVSS v3.1 8.6, Ivanti), went into CISA’s KEV catalog that day with a 16 October deadline. On 30 October CISA added two more: CVE-2026-111357, a privilege-escalation flaw in which a system service trusts cached configuration data, letting an attacker reach root (CVSS v3.1 7.0, MITRE as CNA; the record has been public since 15 October; due 2 November), and CVE-2026-118527, which CISA lists as a predictable-identifier flaw (due 13 November). CISA says both can be chained with CVE-2026-104935. Whether Ivanti has shipped a fix for CVE-2026-111357 could not be verified: NVD lists 22.8R2 through 22.8R2.5 and 25.1.x before 25.1.1.3 as affected, so do not assume the builds that fixed CVE-2026-104935 close the whole chain, and check Ivanti’s advisory for the build that fixes CVE-2026-111357. Volexity found the chain during an incident response and tracks the actor as UTA0576; exploitation dates from at least early October, with Volexity and Defused placing it at 1–3 October. The intruders left no web shell, stole domain credentials, queried Active Directory for LAPS passwords and BitLocker keys, sprayed passwords and wiped logs.
