Critical Incident — State-sponsored reconnaissance infrastructure seized

FBI seizes the Chinese scanning and relay platforms built out of other people’s routers and IoT devices

The Justice Department and FBI announced court-authorised seizures on 26 August of two linked platforms, QScan and QTRouter, operated by a PRC state-sponsored group the government calls QTFY and tied to a Nanjing contractor. QScan handled reconnaissance against US critical infrastructure; QTRouter relayed the resulting intrusions through compromised third-party routers and IoT devices so the traffic arrived from ordinary domestic addresses. Both platforms are now inoperable because the seized domains were hard-coded into the malware. For ports, terminals and fleet operators the relay design is the part worth absorbing: the small, unmanaged, internet-connected devices that fill a maritime estate — VSAT routers, gate cameras, crane telemetry gateways, remote-monitoring boxes — are exactly the population these platforms recruited, and source addresses proved nothing.

High Research — AI-assisted exploit porting against PLCs

AI-assisted exploit porting: a working PLC exploit moved to a second controller model in hours

Forescout’s Vedere Labs set a large language model the task of taking a working remote-code-execution exploit for one WAGO controller — the 750-852, via CVE-2021-31886, a pre-authentication buffer overflow in its Nucleus FTP server — and re-targeting it at a related model, the 750-831. It worked, but not cheaply or unattended: the final stage alone ran more than eight hours and over $500 in API costs, with a researcher steering throughout. For terminals and vessels the point is portability. Cargo handling, ballast and power-management controllers come in close model families, and an exploit proven on one variant is now cheaper to move to its siblings than most risk registers assume.

Critical Threat intel — Coordinated GNSS interference affecting navigation

Baltic GNSS interference is a coordinated transmitter network, not scattered jamming

Analysis of Baltic GNSS interference published this month characterises the activity as a coordinated network of transmitters rather than opportunistic local jamming, with consistent geographic patterns and sustained operation. That framing changes the planning assumption for a bridge team: interference in these waters is a persistent environmental condition to be navigated around, not an intermittent fault to be reported and waited out. The wider picture supports it — maritime GNSS interference reached record levels earlier this year, and a single 24-hour period in the Arabian Gulf reportedly affected more than 1,100 vessels with degraded AIS data and elevated collision risk. Position, navigation and timing loss also propagates into timestamping, ECDIS and some engine-monitoring systems.

Confirmed ongoing interference — sustained, geographically consistent GNSS jamming and spoofing documented across Baltic and Gulf waters through 2026 No CVE — this is radio-frequency interference and electronic warfare activity, not a product vulnerability
No patch applies — mitigation is navigational procedure, redundant PNT sources and crew training Action: Treat degraded GNSS as a standing passage-planning input for affected areas rather than an exception. Confirm bridge teams can fix position without GNSS — radar, visual, echo sounder — and that they practise it rather than assuming it. Check which onboard systems silently depend on GNSS timing: ECDIS, AIS, VDR, some engine and cargo monitoring, and any log that timestamps from GPS. Where a system will behave unpredictably without a valid time source, know that in advance. Report interference through the appropriate national channel so the picture stays current for everyone else.

A CVSS 10 pre-auth flaw in SonicWall’s SMA1000 remote-access gateway is being exploited, and CISA gave agencies three days

SonicWall disclosed on 1 September that two previously unknown vulnerabilities in its SMA1000 secure remote access gateway and SSL-VPN appliance are being exploited in the wild; the company says it found both the flaws and the exploitation internally. Critical Advisory — Actively exploited, CISA KEV, historian and MES databases

A seven-year-old SQL Server flaw is now confirmed exploited — and it is the database under your historian

CISA added six vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 26 August, and the one that matters most on the plant floor is CVE-2019-1068, a remote code execution flaw in Microsoft SQL Server patched in July 2019 and only now confirmed as exploited in the wild. Successful exploitation runs code under the SQL Server Database Engine service account; where that account is over-privileged — which is common on systems installed by an integrator years ago — the attacker moves off the database and onto the Windows host.
