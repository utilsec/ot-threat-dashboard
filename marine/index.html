Attack Path The management plane is this month’s front door

Across September the exploited flaws that matter to ports have clustered in the systems that manage other systems, not in the controllers. Examples are Cisco’s ISE and Secure Firewall Management Center, Check Point’s VPN gateways and its management server (confirmed exploited and added to KEV on 22 September), and Arista’s VeloCloud Orchestrator (a CVSS 10 flaw added the same day). The discussion among terminal and fleet IT teams has moved from patch speed to blast radius. A compromised policy server, SD-WAN orchestrator or VPN concentrator can quietly change what reaches the terminal operating system, the gate and the crane network at every site at once. That makes configuration baselines, and the ability to spot a change nobody approved, as important as the patch itself.

Governance The Coast Guard now has one office that owns maritime cyber policy

The USCG established the Office of Maritime Cybersecurity Policy on 1 September, pulling domestic policy, international standards work and a coordinated compliance and enforcement strategy for the Marine Transportation System under a single directorate reporting to the Director of Inspections and Compliance. The reaction across ports and operators has been less about the announcement itself than about what it implies for the next inspection cycle: a central authority with an explicit enforcement remit is a different counterparty from a distributed set of Captains of the Port interpreting the MTS cyber rule case by case. The GAO’s February 2025 findings sit behind it — incomplete records of cyber deficiencies found during vessel and facility inspections, and no defined competency requirements for the people doing the inspecting — and the office’s first priorities will show how directly it means to close them.

Critical Threat Intelligence — Supply chain, ship-to-shore crane control

Undocumented cellular modems in ZPMC ship-to-shore cranes — confirmed hardware, assessed intent, no observed use

This is standing context rather than new reporting: US Maritime Administration Advisory 2026-007 was issued on 24 April 2026 and remains in force to 21 October, and the distinction inside the crane supply-chain problem is the part worth carrying into a risk register. What is confirmed is physical: US Coast Guard and congressional inspections found undocumented cellular modems fitted to cranes built by Shanghai Zhenhua Heavy Industries, wired with direct access to the crane industrial controllers and bypassing the port firewalls that are meant to sit in front of them. What is assessed rather than observed is the consequence — an intelligence judgement that those modems could be used to monitor crane activity or disable cranes during a crisis. No such use has been reported anywhere, and this is structural exposure rather than an incident. The reason it still matters is population. ZPMC builds an estimated 72–78 per cent of the world’s ship-to-shore cranes and roughly 80 per cent of those installed in US and European ports, so the exposure is most of the fleet rather than an unlucky subset. MARAD Advisory 2026-007 names ZPMC cranes alongside China’s LOGINK logistics-data platform and Nuctech scanning systems as foreign adversarial technology risks to US port infrastructure, and asks facilities to apply access control, network segmentation and monitoring and offline backups, and to report anomalies to the Coast Guard, CISA and the FBI. Ports are responding with retrofits — removing the foreign modems, replacing crane software, separating crane OT networks — supported by an executive order and more than $20bn in federal loans, with Konecranes and a revived Mitsui E&S North American line named as alternative suppliers.
