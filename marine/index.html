GPS and AIS interference surges near the Strait of Hormuz, throwing 1,100-plus vessels off position

A July surge of GPS and AIS interference across the Strait of Hormuz and the wider Middle East Gulf left navigation systems unreliable along one of the world’s busiest oil corridors. Maritime trackers logged more than 1,100 vessels reporting simultaneous interference within a single 24-hour window amid heightened regional tensions, with ships falsely positioned over airports, a nuclear plant and dry land, and 21-plus new AIS jamming clusters mapped across UAE, Qatari, Omani and Iranian waters. Navigational warnings urged crews to fall back on radar and visual fixes and to treat GPS position and timing as suspect; the strait carries roughly 20% of the world’s seaborne oil, and transit was heavily disrupted. This is electronic-warfare interference, not a network intrusion — but it corrupts the position, timing and AIS data that bridge and terminal OT depend on.

High Incident — Port Operations

Cyberattack forced a systems-wide outage at North Carolina Ports; operations recovered by 7 August

North Carolina Ports confirmed a cyberattack that triggered a systems-wide outage across its facilities — the Port of Wilmington, the Port of Morehead City and the Charlotte Inland Port — detected late on 4 August. Gate-processing systems went down and teams switched to manual processing, opening gates late and warning of delays. The authority activated its cybersecurity contingency plan, engaged outside forensics and worked with the US Coast Guard and state agencies; by 7 August it said gate operations and vessel activity were back to normal schedules, with no data compromised. The confirmed impact is IT-side — an outage of gate and terminal-processing systems rather than any reported manipulation of cranes or control systems — but the effect on cargo flow was real. No threat actor had claimed responsibility as of this edition.

High Threat Intel — Nation-State

Federal alert: Iran-affiliated hackers manipulating the PLCs that also run port and terminal OT

Federal agencies sharpened their warning that Iran-affiliated hackers are actively manipulating the programmable logic controllers behind US critical infrastructure — the same controllers that run cranes, fuel, power and water systems inside MTSA-regulated ports and terminals. Advisory AA26-097A, updated 22 July, describes actors reaching internet-exposed Rockwell, Schneider and Siemens gear, stealing project files, and rewriting or deleting control logic, in some cases disabling shutdown and alarm functions without alerting operators. Port facilities are exactly the mixed IT/OT environment the campaign favours: diverse controllers, remote maintenance access and edge devices often reachable from the internet. The activity has caused operational disruption and financial loss across critical-infrastructure sectors. It is opportunistic rather than sophisticated — the way in is weak credentials and exposed controllers, not a novel exploit.

Foundational context: CYTUR’s 2026 Maritime Cyber Threat White Paper, published 19 February 2026, reports 828 maritime cyber incidents in 2025 — up 103% year over year — with OT-focused attacks rising roughly 150% and GPS spoofing degrading navigation for around 1,000 vessels a day and 40,000-plus vessels overall. Critical Active Exploitation — KEV

Arista VeloCloud Orchestrator zero-day (CVSS 10) exploited — the SD-WAN edge behind ports and vessels

Arista patched CVE-2026-16812, an unauthenticated OS command-injection flaw in VeloCloud Orchestrator On-Prem (formerly VeloCloud Orchestrator by Broadcom) carrying a maximum CVSS of 10.0, after confirming exploitation in the wild as a zero-day. No credentials or special configuration are required: the orchestrator web interface is exposed by default, and network access alone lets an attacker run privileged internal functions and compromise the confidentiality, integrity and availability of the orchestrator and every SD-WAN edge it manages. CISA added it to the KEV catalog on 27 July with a three-day federal remediation deadline. VeloCloud SD-WAN commonly stitches together port and terminal sites, logistics networks and vessel-to-shore links, so orchestrator takeover is a route into the connectivity fabric in front of maritime OT. Fixed in VCO 5.2.3.14, 6.1.3.4, 6.4.2.4 and 7.0.0.1.

High Active Exploitation — KEV

Cisco Secure FMC zero-day exploited — the edge that fronts port and vessel networks

Cisco disclosed CVE-2026-20316, a static (hard-coded) credential flaw in the web interface of Secure Firewall Management Center (FMC) that is being exploited as a zero-day. An unauthenticated remote attacker can log in with a built-in low-privileged account and read sensitive data, and Cisco warns it can be chained with other FMC bugs to escalate privileges. CISA added it to the KEV catalog on 29 July with a 1 August federal remediation deadline. FMC manages the firewalls that sit on the perimeter and remote-access path in front of many port, terminal and vessel-shore networks, so a foothold there is a foothold toward the OT behind it. CVSS is only 5.3, but active exploitation makes it urgent.

High Active Exploitation — KEV

N-able N-central RMM auth-bypass exploited — a supply-chain path into port and logistics IT

CISA added CVE-2026-18577 (CVSS 8.2), an authentication-bypass flaw in the N-able N-central remote monitoring and management (RMM) platform, to the KEV catalog on 3 August after exploitation was observed in the wild from 1 August; the companion CVE-2026-18556 was added on 4 August. The bug is an incomplete fix for the earlier CVE-2026-18556 and lets a remote, unauthenticated attacker bypass authentication and take administrative control of a vulnerable N-central server — and, through it, the managed endpoints beneath it. For maritime operators this is a supply-chain exposure: many ports, terminals and logistics firms have their IT managed by MSPs running N-central, so a compromise of that console hands an attacker a route into downstream operator networks. N-able shipped hotfix 2026.3.1.7 on 2 August and urges immediate upgrade.

Info Regulation — USCG

USCG Marine Transportation System cyber rule: training deadline past, Cyber Security Officer & plan due 2027

The US Coast Guard's Marine Transportation System cybersecurity rule continues to phase in for US ports and MTSA-regulated facilities. Reportable cyber incidents must be reported to the National Response Center (within 12 hours for incidents affecting seaworthiness or security compliance); the annual cyber-training requirement took effect in January 2026; and by 16 July 2027 owners and operators must designate a Cybersecurity Officer, complete a cybersecurity assessment and submit a cybersecurity plan for approval. The Coast Guard has issued additional FAQs clarifying the requirements without adding new ones. For operators the near-term work is governance: named accountability, incident-reporting playbooks, and a cyber plan that maps to the rule.

Regulation — no exploitation USCG
Compliance — governance & reporting Action: Confirm a designated Cyber Security Officer, stand up a 12-hour incident-reporting procedure to the NRC, and align the facility cyber plan with the MTS rule's requirements.

Info Regulation — IACS

IACS UR E26/E27 keep raising the cyber bar for newbuild vessels

The IACS Unified Requirements UR E26 (whole-ship cyber resilience) and UR E27 (equipment-level security) apply to vessels with construction contracts signed on or after 1 July 2024, covering identification, protection, detection, response and recovery across integrated IT/OT and third-party equipment. The requirements shift cyber resilience into ship design and supplier obligations, so owners, yards and integrators building 500 GT-plus tonnage need cyber baked into specifications, not bolted on later.

Regulation — no exploitation IACS
Compliance — newbuild design & supplier terms Action: For newbuild and major-conversion programs, hold yards and equipment suppliers to UR E26/E27 in specifications and factory acceptance, and ensure delivered vessels have the required detection and recovery capabilities.
