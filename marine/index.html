High Research — Detection and response

CISA’s red team took a domain undetected — the tooling was fine, the monitoring was not

CISA published AA26-237A on 25 August comparing two red-team assessments run with near-identical tradecraft: one against a government services and facilities organisation, one against a water and wastewater systems entity. Both opened with phishing and escalated through Active Directory weaknesses that are ordinary rather than exotic — a Machine Account Quota left at its default of 10, and misconfigured Active Directory Certificate Services templates. The outcomes diverged sharply. At the organisation CISA labels A, the team obtained elevated domain privileges and reached sensitive business systems and cloud resources without ever being detected, despite the organisation running multiple security operations centres and multiple EDR products. CISA’s stated reason deserves reading twice: the SOCs did not communicate with one another, and thousands of false positives from routine business activity buried the real signal. Port authorities, terminal operators and ship managers should read this as a description of their own environment. The corporate Active Directory that this tradecraft targets is very often the same directory authenticating terminal operating system clients, gate and crane HMI workstations, and the shoreside servers that hold vessel performance and cargo data. Both weaknesses named are free to fix. The detection finding is the harder one for this sector: monitoring is frequently split between a corporate IT team, a port-wide MSSP and an OT integrator, which is precisely the multi-console arrangement in which CISA found alerts going uncorrelated.

No exploitation — this is CISA’s own assessment activity, published as defensive findings No CVE — the findings are configuration and detection failures, not product flaws
No patch — remediation is Active Directory hardening and SOC process Action: Set your domain Machine Account Quota to 0 and grant machine-join rights explicitly; audit AD Certificate Services templates for enrolment misconfigurations that permit certificate-based impersonation. Then map your monitoring boundaries honestly: if corporate IT, an MSSP and an OT integrator each watch part of the estate, name the person who correlates across them and ask for a measured false-positive rate on the consoles covering terminal operations. Finally, confirm terminal operating system and gate automation authentication does not silently inherit corporate domain trust — the North Carolina Ports outage is a recent reminder of how quickly an IT-side compromise becomes a manual-gate problem.

High Threat intel — Navigation / GNSS interference

Baltic GNSS spoofing is a coordinated transmitter network, not scattered jamming — and its footprint now reaches seven states

Two independent lines of evidence hardened a picture the maritime industry has been treating as background noise. Lithuania’s communications regulator reported on 18 August that spoofing antennae operating from Kaliningrad have grown from three in early 2025 to 36, giving a broadcast radius of roughly 450 km that now covers Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, Finland, Sweden, Belarus and the Baltic itself. Separately, researchers from GPSPatron and Gdynia Maritime University published shore-based and at-sea measurements taken aboard the research vessel Imor near the Polish coast and the Kaliningrad maritime boundary. They identified four distinct transmitter types at four locations that switched on and off together — the signature of centralised tactical control rather than opportunistic interference. Every recorded event combined multi-constellation jamming with false GPS L1 signals, meaning receivers were not merely denied a fix but handed a plausible wrong one. That distinction matters on a bridge: a jammed receiver alarms, a spoofed one does not. The researchers describe effects on ECDIS, on some autopilot implementations, and on the position picture VTS operators use to sequence traffic. For operators this is electronic warfare rather than a network compromise, but the operational consequence — a watch team, a pilot and a port that cannot agree where a vessel is — belongs in the same resilience planning as a cyber incident, and it is now a standing condition in a major European trade lane rather than an occasional event.

Active and routine — daily interference confirmed by regulator reporting and independent at-sea measurement Not a software vulnerability — RF denial and deception, no CVE applies
No patch — mitigate through procedure, redundant position sources and crew training Action: Confirm bridge teams on Baltic, North Sea and eastern Mediterranean routes are briefed that spoofing produces a confident wrong position with no alarm, and that cross-checking GNSS against radar, visual fixes, echo sounder and gyro is a standing requirement rather than a contingency. Verify ECDIS is configured to flag position-source disagreement, check that any autopilot mode dependent on GNSS can be identified and disengaged quickly, and make sure AIS anomalies from other traffic are treated as suspect data rather than ground truth. Log and report interference events to the relevant coastal state and to IMO channels — the reporting is what sizes the problem for everyone else.

Six agencies warn the Gunra ransomware crew is walking into critical infrastructure through two 2025 Fortinet bugs

CISA, the FBI, the Defense Cyber Crime Center, the NSA, the Secret Service and the Korean National Police Agency published joint advisory AA26-222A on 10 August on Gunra, a ransomware operation that has hit organisations across critical infrastructure sectors since April 2025. The advisory’s operational point is unglamorous: Gunra’s initial access is overwhelmingly through internet-facing edge devices, and specifically two Fortinet flaws — CVE-2024-21762 and CVE-2024-55591 — both patched in 2025 and both still reachable on unpatched appliances. Critical Advisory — Exploited web tier, CVSS 10.0

CISA sets a three-day federal deadline on a CVSS 10.0 Oracle web-tier flaw now under active attack

CISA added CVE-2026-21962 to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 24 August with a remediation date of 27 August — the tightest window Binding Operational Directive 26-04 permits. The flaw is an improper access control issue in Oracle HTTP Server and in the Oracle WebLogic Server Proxy Plug-in for Apache HTTP Server and Microsoft IIS, scored CVSS 10.0. It is reachable over HTTP by an unauthenticated attacker with no user interaction and no existing privileges, and successful exploitation allows access to the instance or modification of the data it fronts. Oracle shipped the fix in its January 2026 critical patch update; reporting on 25 August describes broad exploitation against WebLogic servers. A three-day federal deadline is the signal worth reading — CISA reserves that window for exploitation it judges both live and consequential. This is not an ICS product and it will not be on a vessel, but Oracle’s web tier is a very common reverse proxy in front of the shoreside applications a port runs: terminal operating system web clients, port community systems, appointment and gate booking portals, EDI and customs interfaces, and the reporting layers that expose berth and yard data to carriers and truckers. Those are the systems whose loss produces exactly the manual-processing scenario North Carolina Ports worked through earlier this month.

Medium Advisory — Life safety / facility systems

Fire-alarm management software stores its credentials in cleartext in memory, CISA warns

CISA published ICSA-26-232-01 on 20 August covering Johnson Controls Simplex Incident Manager, the software operators use to monitor and manage Simplex fire alarm panels across a property portfolio. Versions up to and including V2.01 keep user passwords and authentication tokens unencrypted in system memory while the application runs, tracked as CVE-2026-27875 and classified as CWE-316. A local attacker who already holds low-privileged access to the workstation can read those credentials out of memory and reuse them against the application and the systems it connects to. CISA lists critical manufacturing, commercial facilities, government facilities, transportation systems and energy among the affected sectors, with deployment worldwide. Johnson Controls has published remediation guidance in JCI-PSA-2026-28. Terminals, cruise facilities and port administration buildings run Simplex fire detection, and on a port estate the fire-alarm management workstation is often shared, physically accessible and administered by a facilities contractor. That is the local low-privileged foothold this flaw needs, and the credentials it yields reach every panel on the property.

Medium Advisory — Building automation / stored XSS

A low-privilege Metasys user can plant a payload that runs in an administrator’s browser

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-14, published 13 August, describes a stored cross-site scripting flaw in Johnson Controls Metasys. A user with low privileges can inject a payload through a crafted URL; the payload persists across logins and then executes in the browser context of anyone who subsequently views it, administrators included. Metasys 12, 13, 14 and 15 are affected. That is a privilege-escalation path inside the building management platform rather than a remote takeover, and there is no report of exploitation. Metasys turns up across port authority administrative buildings, terminal offices, cold-storage sheds and cruise terminals, usually managed by a facilities contractor rather than the OT team. The flaw itself is modest, but it grants a foothold that executes with administrator context inside the platform that runs terminal HVAC and, at many facilities, feeds door control — and BMS estates are habitually excluded from the port’s vulnerability management scope entirely.

Medium Regulation — USCG / 33 CFR 101 Subpart F

Coast Guard cyber rule: annual training already required, Cybersecurity Plans due 16 July 2027

The Coast Guard’s Cybersecurity in the Marine Transportation System rule, codified at 33 CFR Part 101 Subpart F, took effect on 16 July 2025 and applies to owners and operators of US-flagged vessels, Outer Continental Shelf facilities and facilities regulated under the Maritime Transportation Security Act. Two dates govern current planning. Personnel training required under 33 CFR 101.650 has been mandatory since 12 January 2026 and recurs annually. The substantive deadline is 16 July 2027, by which point owners and operators must have designated a Cybersecurity Officer, conducted a Cybersecurity Assessment, and submitted a Cybersecurity Plan to the Coast Guard for review and approval. The Coast Guard has also signalled increased Port State Control attention to poor cybersecurity practice, where findings can produce deficiencies, detention, denial of entry or Captain of the Port restrictions on vessel movement.

Low Policy — Joint guidance

US and Australian agencies publish guidance on isolating OT and operating that way for an extended period

A joint publication from US and Australian agencies sets out how critical infrastructure operators should prepare to isolate vital operational technology and supporting systems from external connectivity, and to keep operating in that isolated state for an extended period rather than the few hours most continuity plans assume. It is guidance, not a requirement. Its value in a maritime context is that it converts a claim most terminals make confidently — that they can work cargo on paper if systems go down — into a dependency checklist covering time synchronisation, authentication, licence servers, customs and cargo release interfaces, and the physical availability of staff who have done it before. The North Carolina Ports incident is a live example of the scenario: terminal operations continued while processing shifted to manual, and the constraint was documentation flow rather than machinery.
