Top Story

An outside actor breached North Carolina Ports’ IT systems, forcing all three facilities onto manual cargo processing

The North Carolina State Ports Authority disclosed that an intrusion detected on 5 August compromised IT systems supporting all three of its facilities: the Port of Wilmington, the Port of Morehead City, and the Charlotte inland terminal. A spokesperson confirmed the breach was carried out by an outside actor and that cargo processing shifted to manual procedures while the authority worked to contain the incident; the breach was publicly reported on 6 August. The operator declined to say whether ransomware was involved, leaving that question unresolved. The authority described the intrusion as contained, and said normal operating schedules resumed on 6 and 7 August, though some processing remained manual afterward. No group has publicly claimed responsibility. Critically, the authority has not confirmed that any operational technology — crane control systems, terminal operating systems, or gate automation — was compromised, but it has also not explicitly ruled that out; the public statements to date describe an IT-system breach and a manual fallback, not a completed OT forensic finding. That gap matters for the sector: a manual-processing fallback that worked is exactly the kind of contingency capability the Coast Guard’s cybersecurity rule for the Marine Transportation System expects operators to have rehearsed, and this incident is a live test of whether that capability exists industry-wide rather than at one operator. Three facilities running on manual procedures simultaneously for roughly two days also shows how quickly an IT-only breach can visibly affect cargo flow even without any claim of OT manipulation.

Confirmed incident — IT systems breached, cargo processing went manual at all three facilities No CVE disclosed — initial access vector and ransomware involvement not confirmed
No patch — incident containment and manual-operations fallback Action: Confirm your own manual-processing fallback for gate, yard and crane-adjacent scheduling functions is written, rehearsed, and does not share credentials or network paths with the systems it replaces. Ask your OT team directly whether they have independently verified — not assumed — that crane and terminal operating systems were unreachable from the compromised IT environment, since the operator has not made that claim itself.

Medium Incident — Supply chain

A CEVA Logistics intrusion halted outbound shipments at eight European warehouses, rippling into retail and gaming supply chains

CEVA Logistics, a contract-logistics and freight-forwarding provider, suffered an intrusion on 29 July that disrupted its European operations and stopped outbound shipments from eight warehouses. The company notified customers on 1 August, and the scale of the disruption became public in the week of 10 August. Air, ocean, ground and rail transport services were not affected — the disruption was confined to warehouse and contract-logistics IT. CEVA says personal data including names, addresses, phone numbers, emails and order details was compromised, but that payment and credential data was not. Downstream retailers and brands named as affected include Bol, De Bijenkorf, Ace & Tate, Pokémon Center (UK and Germany, with some order cancellations), Valve/Steam, Ajax and ING; the Dutch data protection authority is investigating. No group has claimed the intrusion. For ports and terminals the relevance is dependency, not direct exposure: contract-logistics and warehousing providers like CEVA sit between ocean carriers, consignees and inland distribution, and an eight-warehouse outage shows how a logistics-IT breach with no port or vessel OT involvement can still back up cargo movement downstream of the berth.

Critical Advisory — Johnson Controls

Johnson Controls patches critical deserialization flaws that could hand over physical access-control systems

Johnson Controls updated its advisory (Update A, 11 August) for C•CURE 9000 and victor, its physical access-control and video platforms, covering two critical unauthenticated deserialization vulnerabilities (CVE-2026-21655 and CVE-2026-21653) and a high-severity issue (CVE-2026-34496), with CVSS scores up to 9.6. A network-adjacent attacker who reaches the application server can exploit the deserialization flaws without credentials to execute arbitrary code and take control of the access-control system itself. C•CURE and victor deployments commonly manage terminal gate access, badge readers, perimeter doors and camera integrations at ports and other regulated facilities, which is the deployment pattern that makes this advisory relevant here even though it is not marine-specific. No public exploitation has been reported. Patches are available.

Siemens patched CVE-2026-3014, an OS command-injection flaw rated CVSS 9.1, in Siveillance Video management servers as part of the August ICS Patch Tuesday cycle. High Advisory — Gunra RaaS

CISA and international partners warn Gunra ransomware is breaking in through unpatched internet-facing VPN gateways

CISA, the FBI and international partners published joint advisory AA26-222A on 10 August covering Gunra, a Conti-derived ransomware-as-a-service operation running double-extortion attacks against government and critical-infrastructure targets. The advisory says initial access typically comes through unpatched internet-facing VPN gateways and exposed RDP, citing Fortinet flaws CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 specifically, and describes a five-to-seven-day payment deadline once operators gain a foothold. Ports and terminals rely on the same class of equipment — VPN concentrators and remote-access gateways — for vendor, engineering and remote-berth connectivity, which is the entry path this advisory describes rather than any port-specific targeting.

Medium Research — Ransomware

Dragos counts eight maritime ransomware incidents in Q2, inside a transportation total of 95 — and no direct control-system manipulation

Dragos’s Q2 2026 industrial ransomware analysis, published 10 August, recorded 1,140 ransomware incidents against industrial organizations worldwide, up 12% on Q1. Within the transportation and logistics category — 95 incidents total — Dragos counted 8 against maritime targets, alongside 6 in aviation and 3 in rail; manufacturing dominated the overall total at 747 of 1,140. The most active groups named were Qilin, Akira, The Gentlemen, DragonForce and LockBit 5.0. Dragos’s key structural finding is that most intrusions cascaded into operational disruption through IT-system encryption rather than direct manipulation of control systems — no Stage 2 ICS Kill Chain activity was observed. North Carolina Ports fits that pattern: an IT breach with real operational consequences at the gate, without any confirmed OT compromise, which is the same mechanism Dragos describes across the wider ransomware count.

High Research — Exposure

The live water-utility PLC campaign is a warning for any port with an internet-reachable controller

Since late July, actors assessed by CISA, the FBI, NSA and other agencies to be Iranian-affiliated (joint advisory AA26-097A) have reached internet-exposed Rockwell Automation MicroLogix 1100 and 1400 controllers at water utilities in at least 12 states, changing device IP addresses and setting unauthorized passwords to lock operators out — using exposure and weak or default credentials, not a disclosed CVE, to get in. Forescout Vedere Labs separately catalogued 4,407 internet-exposed Rockwell/Allen-Bradley controllers worldwide, 2,844 of them in the US, dominated by discontinued legacy MicroLogix hardware, including 22 units in cities already hit by the attacks. Neither finding is about the marine sector directly, but the mechanism generalizes to any facility running internet-reachable legacy controllers with weak authentication — a description that fits crane, ballast, bilge and gate-automation PLCs at some terminals as much as it fits a water utility’s pump controls. The operative lesson is exposure and credential hygiene, not a patch to apply.

Live, ongoing campaign against a different sector using the same access pattern No CVE — exposure plus default/weak credentials, per joint advisory AA26-097A
No patch applicable — exposure and access-control issue, not a vulnerability Action: Run the same exposure check the water sector is now doing on itself: search Shodan/Censys for any of your PLCs, crane controllers or gate-automation devices reachable from the internet, and confirm none still uses default or unchanged vendor credentials.

Low Policy — Maritime (MTSA)

Dragos publishes phased segmentation guidance to help port and vessel operators meet MTSA’s network-separation requirement

Dragos published guidance on 3 August (“Who’s Allowed on the Bridge”) aimed at port and vessel operators working toward the Coast Guard’s Maritime Transportation Security Act cybersecurity requirement for network segmentation. It lays out a phased crawl/walk/run approach: building an enterprise-to-OT DMZ, controlling remote-access brokers, and separating operational wireless and radio links — bridge, crane and shipboard systems among them — from control networks. The guidance targets operators starting from limited or no existing OT security architecture rather than assuming a green-field build.
