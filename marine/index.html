Top Story

North Carolina Ports hit by intrusion that took cargo processing systems offline across three facilities

The North Carolina State Ports Authority detected an unauthorised intrusion on 4 August that triggered a systems-wide IT outage across all three of its maritime logistics hubs: the Port of Wilmington, the Port of Morehead City and the Charlotte Inland Port. Processing workflows were directly affected, with truck movements, cargo processing and administrative systems disrupted while restoration was underway. Terminal operations and vessel activity continued as scheduled, and some cargo processing shifted to manual handling. The incident is a clean illustration of a pattern this sector keeps repeating: the ships kept moving and the cranes kept working, but the paperwork layer that tells the terminal what to move and releases it to a trucker went down, and that is enough to back up a gate queue and ripple into inland rail-served facilities.

High Incident — Logistics / Data theft

CEVA Logistics breach affects European warehouses and spills customer data through its retail clients

CEVA Logistics, one of the world’s largest shipping and logistics operators, was attacked between 29 July and 1 August, with the intrusion affecting at least eight warehouses across Europe. The France-headquartered company runs more than a thousand warehouses worldwide and reported 18.3 billion dollars in revenue last year. The consequences surfaced indirectly: several companies that rely on CEVA for fulfilment disclosed that their customers’ personal information was taken in the breach, including Dutch e-commerce group Bol, department store De Bijenkorf, eyewear company Ace & Tate, football club Ajax, and Steam’s European hardware business. For marine and port operators the lesson is about position in the chain rather than the specific victim: a third-party logistics provider holds consignee data, routing detail and warehouse operations for hundreds of shippers at once, so one compromise produces notification obligations across a long list of unrelated customers.

Check whether your own contracts with shippers make you the notifying party if a subcontractor is breached — several operators discovered that only after the fact.

Critical Incident — Nation-state / PLCs

Five federal agencies warn AI-written scripts are reading Siemens S7 controllers, commercial facilities in scope

The NSA, CISA, FBI, Department of Energy and EPA issued a joint advisory on 19 August warning that threat actors are combining the open-source snap7 and python-snap7 libraries with AI-assisted scripting to build tools that mimic legitimate OT monitoring software, giving read and write access to Siemens S7 controller memory, configuration data and ladder logic over S7comm. Critical manufacturing, energy, water, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities are named as the most-targeted sectors. Actors locate exposed devices using internet scanning services and gain access through default or weakly configured credentials rather than a vulnerability. Affected lines span the S7-200, S7-300, S7-400, S7-1200 and S7-1500 including F-series safety controllers. Shoreside terminals are squarely inside the commercial facilities scope: S7 and comparable controllers run bulk and conveyor handling, shore power, fuelling systems, gate barriers and terminal building services, and many were commissioned by an integrator with remote access still enabled.

Active threat — agencies describe observed read access and preparation for write operations No CVE — internet exposure plus default or weak controller credentials
No patch — remove exposure, harden credentials, restrict S7comm Action: Ask your terminal engineering team and your crane, conveyor and shore-power vendors for a list of every Siemens S7 controller on the estate, then check each against your external attack surface. Block port 102 at the perimeter. Terminals frequently find controllers reachable through a vendor’s own cellular router that nobody in the port authority commissioned.

Industrial ransomware rose 12 percent in Q2, and disrupted output without touching control systems

Dragos recorded 1,140 ransomware incidents at industrial organisations in the second quarter of 2026, up from 1,020 in the first quarter, with manufacturing accounting for 747, construction 176, equipment manufacturing 114 and food and beverage 70. The finding most relevant to ports and vessel operators concerns mechanism rather than sector counts: disrupting the IT systems supporting an industrial environment is regularly sufficient to interrupt operations even when attackers never reach the control system, and extortion continued shifting from file encryption toward data theft. The North Carolina Ports outage is a textbook instance — cranes and berths unaffected, cargo processing halted. For vessel operators the equivalent exposure sits in fleet management, planned maintenance, crewing and charter systems ashore, whose loss disrupts commercial operation long before anything on the bridge is at risk.

Medium Research — Facility CPS exposure

Claroty finds one in five facility cyber-physical assets a single hop from internet-exposed systems

Team82 analysed more than 750,000 cyber-physical assets across major data centre facilities and found nearly one in five sitting one hop from a system making outbound internet connections. Close to 90 percent of the building management systems examined communicated over insecure protocols and 40 percent ran outdated firmware, with around a third of HVAC and cooling systems one hop from an internet-exposed system. The research is about data centres, but the asset classes are the same ones a port authority or terminal operator runs: power distribution units, building management, physical access control, fire protection and cooling. The methodology transfers cleanly — count the hops from any internet-facing system to the controllers governing reefer power, gatehouse access control and terminal building services, and most operators have never measured it.

Reefer power in particular is a cargo-integrity risk with an insurance dimension, and it is rarely inventoried alongside crane and conveyor OT.

Critical Advisory — Exploited edge / KEV

Cisco ASA and FTD VPN zero-day exploited to reload firewalls, added to CISA KEV

Cisco has patched CVE-2026-20349, a flaw in the remote-access SSL VPN service of Secure Firewall ASA and Secure Firewall Threat Defense software that attackers are exploiting to force appliances offline. A specially crafted HTTP request to the vulnerable service causes an unexpected reload and a denial-of-service condition, requiring no authentication and no user interaction. Cisco’s PSIRT confirmed active exploitation in August, and CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue with a federal remediation deadline of 14 August. Hot fixes cover ASA 9.16, 9.18, 9.20, 9.22, 9.23 and 9.24 and FTD 7.0, 7.2, 7.4, 7.6, 7.7 and 10.0. In a maritime context these appliances typically terminate the links carrying vendor support into terminal systems, shore-to-ship connectivity, and remote access for staff at inland and satellite facilities — so an attacker can remove your remote support path at the moment you most need it.

Actively exploited — Cisco PSIRT confirmed in-the-wild attacks in August 2026 In CISA KEV — CVE-2026-20349, federal remediation deadline 14 Aug 2026
Patch available — Cisco hot fixes for ASA 9.16–9.24 and FTD 7.0–10.0 Action: Identify every ASA or FTD appliance carrying vendor or staff access into terminal, gate or vessel-support systems and confirm it runs a fixed release. Where patching must wait, restrict the SSL VPN service to known source ranges and confirm you have a tested alternative route to reach remote and inland facilities.

High Advisory — CISA KEV additions

Four more exploited flaws added to KEV on 18 August, including Windows IKE and VMware vCenter

CISA added four vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 18 August: CVE-2026-33824 in Microsoft Internet Key Exchange, CVE-2026-55040 in Microsoft SharePoint, CVE-2026-59310 in Broadcom VMware vCenter and CVE-2026-65400 in Apple macOS. Two further additions followed on 20 August, both in TrueConf Server. For port and vessel operators the IKE and vCenter entries carry the most weight. IKE terminates the site-to-site tunnels linking terminals, inland facilities and headquarters, and frequently the shore end of vessel connectivity. vCenter typically manages the virtual infrastructure hosting terminal operating systems, berth planning and historians — a compromise there is a hypervisor-level problem affecting everything running on it. SharePoint is worth a second look in this sector specifically because facility and vessel security plan documentation, procedures and drawings are so often stored there.

High Advisory — CISA ICS

CISA publishes an advisory for flaws in Malcolm, its own free OT monitoring suite

Advisory ICSA-26-230-01 covers vulnerabilities in Malcolm, the network traffic analysis suite CISA distributes free for OT and ICS environments, which could let an attacker cause a denial-of-service condition or execute arbitrary code. Affected versions are below 26.06.1, below 26.07.0 and up to and including 26.07.1. On the same day CISA published ICSA-26-230-02, a stack overflow in Siemens Simcenter Nastran that can lead to remote code execution if a user is tricked into running the affected binary with a malicious string; Siemens has released fixed versions. Malcolm turns up more often in this sector than expected, because smaller port authorities and terminal operators without a commercial OT monitoring budget deploy it to satisfy visibility expectations under the Coast Guard rule — typically on a span or tap seeing the whole terminal network, and typically outside the patch inventory.

A sensor with full terminal-network visibility is a high-value target and should be scoped as a critical asset in its own right.

Medium Regulation — USCG / 33 CFR 101 Subpart F

Coast Guard cyber rule: annual training already required, Cybersecurity Plans due 16 July 2027

The Coast Guard’s Cybersecurity in the Marine Transportation System rule, codified at 33 CFR Part 101 Subpart F, took effect on 16 July 2025 and applies to owners and operators of US-flagged vessels, Outer Continental Shelf facilities and facilities regulated under the Maritime Transportation Security Act. Two dates govern current planning. Personnel training required under 33 CFR 101.650 has been mandatory since 12 January 2026 and recurs annually. The substantive deadline is 16 July 2027, by which point owners and operators must have designated a Cybersecurity Officer, conducted a Cybersecurity Assessment, and submitted a Cybersecurity Plan to the Coast Guard for review and approval. The Coast Guard has also signalled increased Port State Control attention to poor cybersecurity practice, where findings can produce deficiencies, detention, denial of entry or Captain of the Port restrictions on vessel movement.

Low Policy — Joint guidance

US and Australian agencies publish guidance on isolating OT and operating that way for an extended period

A joint publication from US and Australian agencies sets out how critical infrastructure operators should prepare to isolate vital operational technology and supporting systems from external connectivity, and to keep operating in that isolated state for an extended period rather than the few hours most continuity plans assume. It is guidance, not a requirement. Its value in a maritime context is that it converts a claim most terminals make confidently — that they can work cargo on paper if systems go down — into a dependency checklist covering time synchronisation, authentication, licence servers, customs and cargo release interfaces, and the physical availability of staff who have done it before. The North Carolina Ports incident is a live example of the scenario: terminal operations continued while processing shifted to manual, and the constraint was documentation flow rather than machinery.
