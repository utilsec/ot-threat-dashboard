Info Guidance — CISA and G7 joint call to begin the post-quantum cryptography transition; no deadline attached

CISA and the G7 tell operators to start the post-quantum inventory now — the deadline is set by your asset lifetimes, not by them

CISA and the G7 Cyber Security Working Group issued a joint call on 4 September for governments and organisations to begin the transition to post-quantum cryptography, setting out a phased, risk-based approach: inventory the cryptography in use and the dependencies that carry it, identify the data and systems whose exposure would outlast the migration, and hold a written transition plan rather than a procurement wish. The statement is explicit about store-now-decrypt-later — traffic captured today can be decrypted later — and about the second-order problem, which is that authentication and firmware-signing chains rest on the same public-key algorithms. It is guidance, not a rule, and no deadline attaches. For an airport the relevance is lifecycle: BMS, access control and baggage systems specified this year will still be in service well past the migration window, and their firmware-signing and remote-access cryptography is fixed at purchase.

On 2 September the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security joined CISA, Australia’s ASD ACSC, NCSC-NZ, NCSC-UK and the FBI to publish joint guidance on communicating during IT and OT service outages, titled “Communicating Under Pressure: Best Practices for Service Providers.” It calls for a written incident communications plan with defined trigger thresholds and named audiences, plain-language updates, root-cause and technical detail end users can act on, and messaging aligned with legal and regulatory duties. Britain moves to bar designated high-risk suppliers from critical infrastructure — without naming them publicly

The UK government has tabled late amendments to the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill that would let ministers stop critical-sector organisations from using technology suppliers designated as high risk. The Bill, introduced in November 2025 and now near the end of its passage through the Lords, already extends duties to energy, water, transport and health operators and pulls managed service providers, data centres and digital infrastructure into scope. The amendments were tabled on 24 August and reported on 2 September; they follow reporting that a small UK generating station ran manually for four days after a cyberattack. Designations would not necessarily be made public, which is the detail operators should notice: a supplier could be designated without a public list to check against, so the obligation to know your own supply chain moves from good practice to compliance exposure. None of this is in force yet, and the Bill has not completed its passage.

From 11 September 2026 exploited vulnerabilities in digital products must be reported within 24 hours

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s reporting obligations begin on 11 September 2026. Manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must notify an actively exploited vulnerability in their product, or a severe incident affecting its security, within 24 hours of becoming aware, with fuller reporting to follow. Airports and airlines are mainly buyers here rather than obligated manufacturers, and that is the useful angle: the suppliers of baggage systems, common-use platforms, jet bridges, lighting controls and building management equipment sold into Europe acquire a defined notification duty that can be written into support contracts.
