Medium Cross-sector incident — food retail in France; carried as a lesson for airport building and cold-chain systems (editorial assessment); not an aviation incident

Attackers took over refrigeration controllers at about 700 French supermarkets — a warning for every internet-reachable building controller on an airport estate

Over the nights of 11–13 October, attackers remotely changed settings on web-managed refrigeration controllers in French supermarkets, forcing long defrost cycles with temperatures reported as high as about 50°C, spoiling stock and, according to the Paris prosecutor, starting fires at some stores. Finance Minister Roland Lescure told Parliament on 13 October that about 700 of France’s roughly 10,000 supermarkets were affected, and Carrefour, Coopérative U, E.Leclerc, Intermarché and Monoprix have confirmed impact. CERT-FR’s alert CERTFR-2026-ALE-012 named Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC controllers on 12 October, added Copeland xweb and Dixell xweb on 13 October, and in its 16 October update, still the latest version as of 28 October, says the campaigns are probably still running; no new attack nights have been reported since 13 October. Danfoss and Copeland both say most affected units are older or end-of-life models. No group has claimed the attack, no ransom has been demanded and the entry route has not been established; the prosecutor’s cybercrime section has assigned the inquiry to OFAC, France’s anti-cybercrime office. This is not an aviation incident and it does not count toward this page’s threat level. Our assessment of why it is here: airport terminals, flight-catering units and cargo cold stores run the same class of remotely managed refrigeration and building controllers, often maintained by a contractor through a web interface, and this attack shows how directly a changed setpoint becomes physical loss.

High Threat Intelligence — FBI and US Secret Service warning on an active credential campaign against FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN; no CVE; aviation relevance is our assessment

FBI and Secret Service: the FortiBleed credential campaign against FortiGate VPNs is still running, and their advisory covers every critical-infrastructure sector, transportation included

On Tuesday 6 October the FBI and the US Secret Service issued joint advisory JCSA-20261006-01 warning that the FortiBleed campaign remains active: attackers are still scanning internet-facing Fortinet firewalls and SSL VPN gateways and logging in with credentials obtained earlier. There is no CVE. The agencies tie the campaign to reused or leaked passwords and legacy SHA-256 password storage; researchers describe password spraying and stuffing from leak dumps, offline cracking of extracted hashes and a sniffer that captures authentication traffic across 24 protocols. Some owners were locked out after the intruders created their own administrator accounts and deleted or reset the real ones. The access is resold, the FBI links it to Payload ransomware, and SOCRadar says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments from it. SOCRadar’s figure of more than 86,644 working device credentials in 194 countries dates from 19 June; it is not a new count. The advisory says it applies to all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors and names transportation systems among them, and Cybersecurity Dive reports that targeted organisations span every one of the 16. No OT system compromise has been described and no aviation victim is named. Our assessment: FortiGate is a common boundary device between airport and airline corporate networks and terminal and airfield systems, so a stolen admin login there is a stolen boundary.

Confirmed active campaign — FBI and Secret Service advisory of 6 Oct 2026 reports continuing logins and lockouts; ransomware follow-on reported; the operator is described by researchers, not the agencies, as a Russian-speaking access broker; no aviation victim named No CVE — a credential campaign, not a product flaw, so there is nothing to add to KEV
No patch — the fix is credentials and configuration: store admin passwords with PBKDF2 (Fortinet guidance for FortiOS 7.2.11 and later), restrict management access, enforce phishing-resistant MFA Action: On every FortiGate you or a provider runs at an airport or airline boundary: end active VPN and admin sessions, reset VPN and administrator passwords, and look for accounts you did not create — the advisory lists 19 commonly seen names, including fortiAdmin, forticloud-sync, fgtsecure and adminsslvpn — and for genuine admins that were deleted or changed. Take the management interface off the internet with a local-in policy, enforce phishing-resistant MFA on SSL VPN, review API keys and confirm admin credentials are stored with PBKDF2. If an unknown account turns up, treat the firewall, and what it fronts on the operational side, as exposed.

Medium Research — Internet exposure study after the French refrigeration attack; no new incident; aviation relevance is our assessment

Forescout finds almost 13,000 internet-exposed refrigeration management services in 99 countries — most of the Danfoss systems on old firmware

Forescout’s Vedere Labs published on 22 October a count of refrigeration controllers reachable from the internet, prompted by the attacks on French supermarkets over 11–13 October. Searching for ten product families, it found 12,976 exposed service observations on 6,017 public addresses in 99 countries and territories; the unit is services, not devices, because one address can expose several ports. Copeland and Dixell XWEB accounted for 7,075 observations, Danfoss StoreView for 4,697, Copeland Site Supervisor for 551 and CAREL pCOWeb for 513. About 80% of the Danfoss AK-SM 800A StoreView services ran firmware older than 4.3.1, which carries published flaws, and the original AK-SM 800 has been out of support since May 2021. Forescout says the entry route, exploited flaws and actor in the French attacks are still unknown. It also counts more than 20 attacks on refrigeration systems claimed by pro-Russian hacktivist groups since January 2026, mostly in Spain and Italy; those are claims, not confirmed incidents. Our assessment: airport food courts, flight-catering units and cargo cold stores, including pharmaceutical storage, use the same class of controller, usually installed and watched by a refrigeration contractor rather than the airport’s IT or OT team.

Medium Threat Intelligence & Research — Sector statistics from leak-site tracking

Two trackers, two August records: NCC Group counts 1,073 ransomware attacks and Comparitech 997 — and a newer group, Aurora, lists transportation among its targets

NCC Group’s review of August 2026, reported by Industrial Cyber on 28 September, counted 1,073 ransomware attacks, up 12% on July’s 960, which Industrial Cyber reports as a 2026 high. The industrial sector accounted for 329 attacks, about 31%; North America for 473, about 44%; and Europe for 276, about 26%. Qilin led with 164 attacks, about 15%, overtaking The Gentlemen, and DragonForce was also active. NCC also profiles Aurora, a ransomware-as-a-service group active since late April 2026 that targets manufacturing, legal, research and development and transportation organisations through weak or unauthenticated VPN access and PowerShell credential harvesting, then encrypts hypervisors. Comparitech, reported on 14 September, counted 997 attacks in August, a 23% rise on July’s 809 and above its previous monthly record of 988, with utility, healthcare and business-services attacks behind the rise. The two figures differ because the trackers count differently; both rest on leak-site claims and disclosures, a claimed victim is not a confirmed one, and neither describes confirmed OT downtime. For aviation the useful detail is Aurora’s way in. Weak VPN authentication is exactly the gap that integrator and contractor remote access into airports tends to leave, and encrypting the hypervisor takes down every virtual server on it at once — which is how a back-office ransomware event becomes a check-in, baggage or turnaround outage.

Info Research — Dragos incident-response observation and cleanup guidance; no CVE, no actor

Dragos says old worms such as Conficker and WannaCry keep turning up in OT networks — and that a careless cleanup can do more harm than the infection

In a post published 15 October, Dragos’s Lesley Carhart and Jan Hoff say requests for help with legacy commodity infections — Sality, Gammima, WannaCry and Conficker among them — have risen sharply in recent years. Many OT networks Dragos is called into carry 20 to 70 distinct commodity-malware variants spread across several subnets, often on machines older than Windows XP SP2. They attribute the rise to more networking and remote access, better visibility, and rules that require critical-infrastructure networks to be clean, and they set out a “Now / Next / Never” framework, with a white paper, for deciding what to remove and when. Their main warning is that a badly planned removal can cause more operational damage than the malware itself. Nothing here is a targeted campaign. Our assessment for this page: workstations that run baggage handling, airfield lighting and building management at airports often sit on old Windows builds that cannot be patched or rebooted on demand, so the same triage question applies before anyone runs a cleanup tool.

Critical Critical Advisory — Exploited zero-day chain, CISA KEV (due 16 October); one link still unpatched; aviation relevance is our assessment

A three-flaw chain gives attackers root on fully patched Ivanti Connect Secure VPNs — one CVE is fixed and in KEV, the privilege-escalation link is not

Ivanti disclosed CVE-2026-104935 on 13 October: an authentication bypass in Connect Secure that, together with a file-write flaw, lets a remote unauthenticated attacker run commands (CVSS v3.1 8.6, Ivanti). Both flaws are fixed under that single CVE in 22.8R2.5, 25.1.1.2 and 25.1.3.0/25.1.3.1. A third bug, a privilege escalation in the dsinvoked broker, completes the chain to root; as of 28 October we still found no Ivanti fix for it; Volexity said on 13 October that one would come in the coming weeks. Volexity found the chain while responding to an intrusion and attributes it to a new actor it tracks as UTA0576. Exploitation began in early October — Volexity’s case dates from 3 October and Defused saw activity from 1 October — against an appliance that was fully patched at the time. The intruders left no web shell, ran commands through the exploit requests themselves, stole LDAP and domain credentials, queried Active Directory for LAPS passwords and BitLocker keys, Kerberoasted, sprayed passwords and wiped Ivanti debug and Linux audit logs. CISA added the CVE to KEV on 13 October with a 16 October deadline. CISA and Volexity warn that Ivanti’s Integrity Checker Tool may not detect a compromise; Volexity says the chain patches library code in memory and restores configuration data, so a clean result is not proof. Ivanti says a limited number of customers were affected, and no aviation victim is named. Our assessment: Connect Secure is a common route into airline operations and engineering systems and for integrator access to terminal estates, and directory credentials taken through the VPN outlive the patch.
