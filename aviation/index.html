High Incident — GNSS

NTSB is investigating a fatal medevac crash that followed GPS loss during a military jamming exercise

The NTSB is investigating the crash of a Beechcraft King Air C90 medevac aircraft into New Mexico’s Capitan Mountains on 14 May 2026, which killed two pilots and two nurses. The aircraft lost GPS signal during an active military GPS-jamming exercise at White Sands; three other aircraft reported GPS loss the same night. Be precise about what is established: the NTSB confirms the jamming exercise took place and that GPS was lost, but has not determined probable cause or contributing factors, so any causal link remains unproven. The context is a documented global rise in GNSS interference — the FAA’s updated GPS/GNSS Interference Resource Guide (v1.1, April 2026) cites a 65% year-on-year increase in GPS signal loss per 1,000 flights and eight persistent jamming and spoofing hotspots worldwide.

Critical Incident — OT disruption

Attackers took hands-on control of water-utility PLCs across a dozen states — locking out operators and knocking a treatment plant offline

Between 26–27 July 2026 a coordinated campaign disrupted automated controls at more than 30 Minnesota community water systems, and confirmed intrusions have since been reported across roughly a dozen states including Georgia, Michigan, South Dakota, Alabama and New Jersey. This is hands-on manipulation of control equipment, not a data breach: CISA says the actors changed PLC passwords and altered device IP addresses to lock operators out of their own equipment. In Braham, Minnesota a pump failure forced a treatment facility offline and prompted a resident conservation notice; Clayton County, Georgia issued a precautionary boil-water advisory after a pump-station disruption, later lifted. Dragos’s Rob Lee told NPR he could not recall “this many targets at once with operational impact.” Every affected utility reported that drinking water stayed safe, with staff falling back to manual operation. Federal officials have pointed to Iran-linked activity — a TLP:AMBER WaterISAC assessment reportedly found the evidence aligned with campaigns previously attributed to Iran — but no formal public attribution has been made, so treat the actor as suspected rather than confirmed.

Confirmed OT-disruptive incident — operator lockout, pump failure, plant offline across multiple utilities No single CVE — abuse of internet-exposed PLCs, default/absent credentials and weak remote access

Then change every default and shared PLC password, put remote access behind MFA and a broker, back up and hash your PLC project files and logic so you can prove and restore known-good state, and rehearse running the plant manually with the SCADA layer assumed hostile.

Cisco ASA and Firepower join KEV under active exploitation — the OT perimeter is the target again

CISA added CVE-2026-20349, a heap-inspection vulnerability in Cisco Secure Firewall ASA and Firepower Threat Defense software, to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 11 August 2026, citing evidence of active exploitation. The same batch added CVE-2026-68820, a use-after-free in the Windows afd.sys driver enabling local privilege escalation — relevant because Windows engineering workstations and HMI servers are exactly where that gets chained after initial access — and CVE-2026-72898, a SQL injection in Metabase exposing stored credentials. None of these are ICS products. All of them sit in front of, or inside, the path into OT.

High Advisory — Remote access

An N-able N-central auth bypass was exploited, patched incompletely, then exploited again

CISA added two N-able N-central authentication-bypass flaws to KEV within two days: CVE-2026-18556 and CVE-2026-18577, both CVSS 8.2, the second being an incomplete-fix follow-on to the first. N-able confirmed a limited number of customer breaches. Attackers who exploit it take over administrator accounts and can pivot to managed endpoints through the platform’s Take Control remote-session feature. Huntress reported that 55.6% of reachable cloud servers were still unpatched at the time of its analysis. The fix is 2026.3.1.7 / 2026.3 HF1. RMM platforms matter disproportionately in OT because they are how integrators, machine builders and managed-service providers reach plant equipment — often through a path that was never security-reviewed and does not appear on the network diagram.

Cyviation chief executive Eliran Almog argues that material aviation cyber losses come from ground systems — reservations, ground handling, MRO IT, crew scheduling — rather than in-flight compromise, while aircraft remain exposed as data endpoints consuming navigation databases and loadable software with limited integrity checking. High Threat Intel — Ransomware

Dragos counted 1,140 industrial ransomware incidents in Q2 — and not one reached stage 2 of the ICS kill chain

Dragos’s Q2 2026 industrial ransomware analysis recorded 1,140 incidents against industrial organisations, up 12% on Q1. Manufacturing absorbed 747 of them (65%), led by construction at 176 and equipment manufacturing at 114; food and beverage accounted for 70. The finding worth carrying into your own risk conversations is the negative one: Dragos found no case in Q2 where a ransomware operator reached stage 2 of the ICS Cyber Kill Chain. Production stopped because enterprise IT and ERP systems stopped — order entry, scheduling, shipping, labelling — not because anyone touched a controller. Named Q2 cases include West Pharmaceutical Services and Foxconn, the latter with a roughly two-week outage at Wisconsin and Texas sites. That pattern should shape where you spend: the loss is real, but the control is IT/OT dependency mapping and recovery, not deeper controller hardening.

High Regulation — Oversight

GAO found TSA has not defined who owns aviation cybersecurity — and is still working from a 2018 roadmap

GAO report 26-107693 examined how the FAA and TSA manage aviation cybersecurity. The FAA has defined cybersecurity roles across seven entities but under-reported cybersecurity costs to OMB for fiscal years 2024 to 2026, and has fully implemented three of seven Zero Trust strategic objectives. The finding against TSA is more serious: GAO says TSA has not clearly defined cybersecurity roles and responsibilities and is still operating from a 2018 Cybersecurity Roadmap that is misaligned with current DHS strategy. GAO issued five recommendations, four to the FAA and one to TSA. For airport and airline operators the practical read is that the agency setting your cyber requirements is itself working from an outdated internal framework — expect requirements to change, and build programmes that can absorb that rather than compliance artefacts tuned to today’s directive text.

Medium Regulation — EASA Part-IS

EASA Part-IS now binds air operators, ANSPs and maintenance organisations — full compliance is due August 2027

EASA’s Part-IS regime — Delegated Regulation (EU) 2022/1645 and Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/203 — requires an information security management system covering risk assessment, incident detection and response, mandatory incident reporting and supply-chain risk evaluation. Air operators, air navigation service providers, maintenance and training organisations and competent authorities came into scope on 22 February 2026, following aerodrome operators and design and production organisations on 16 October 2025. Full compliance across the sector is required by 22 August 2027. The supply-chain evaluation requirement is the one most organisations underestimate: it reaches the ground handlers, MRO providers and IT suppliers whose compromise has caused most real aviation disruption, and it demands documented assessment rather than a signed questionnaire.

Medium Regulation — Avionics

The FAA finalised a rule requiring interference-tolerant radio altimeters

The FAA published a final rule on 31 July 2026 requiring all US aircraft to be fitted with radio altimeters meeting a new interference tolerance mask standard, so they withstand adjacent-band C-band wireless signals. The stated concern is that RF interference could produce erroneous altitude data feeding navigation and safety systems. Compliance deadlines are 30 December 2030 for major and foreign air carriers and 31 October 2034 for other operators. It is a safety and spectrum rule rather than a cyber advisory, and it is included here for a specific reason: it is a rare case of a regulator mandating hardware-level resilience to RF interference in a safety-critical sensor path, which is the same class of problem as the GNSS interference and unauthenticated RF signalling issues showing up across aviation, rail and maritime.

Not applicable — RF interference resilience rule No CVE
Not applicable — equipment fitment programme with 2030/2034 deadlines Action: Fold the altimeter fitment programme into your existing fleet-modification planning now rather than treating it as a 2030 problem — retrofit slots and supplier capacity are the constraint, not the deadline. Meanwhile treat any safety-critical sensor whose input can be influenced from outside the airframe as part of your threat model.
