Top Story

Researchers show a coin-sized implant can manipulate 737 NG flight management data over ARINC 429

A team from the University of California San Diego and Oberlin College has demonstrated that a hardware implant roughly the size of a coin, costing under 100 dollars to build, can interfere with communications between key Boeing 737 NG avionics. The device attaches to a maintenance connection in the electronics bay beneath the cockpit, reachable in about fifteen seconds with no special tools through an exterior hatch that maintenance and ground staff routinely access. Rather than breaking into a computer, it injects electrical signals onto the ARINC 429 data bus, overpowering legitimate traffic to substitute manipulated information. In the laboratory the team altered a flight plan and manipulated weight and performance data, and explored concealing changes from the pilot display. The work was done on a bench-built avionics testbed, not an operational aircraft.

High Incident — Nation-state / PLCs

Five federal agencies warn AI-written scripts are reading Siemens S7 controllers, commercial facilities in scope

The NSA, CISA, FBI, Department of Energy and EPA issued a joint advisory on 19 August warning that threat actors are combining the open-source snap7 and python-snap7 automation libraries with AI-assisted scripting to build custom tools that mimic legitimate OT monitoring software, providing read and write access to Siemens S7 controller memory, configuration data and ladder logic over S7comm. Critical manufacturing, energy, water, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities are named as most targeted. Actors find exposed devices using internet scanning services and gain access through default or weakly configured credentials rather than a vulnerability. Affected lines span S7-200, S7-300, S7-400, S7-1200 and S7-1500 including F-series safety controllers. Airports sit inside the commercial facilities scope through their ground OT: baggage handling and sortation, jet bridges, fuelling and hydrant systems, de-icing, airfield lighting, and terminal building management — much of it installed by systems integrators with remote support connectivity the airport did not arrange itself.

Medium Research — Facility CPS exposure

Claroty finds one in five facility cyber-physical assets a single hop from internet-exposed systems

Team82 analysed more than 750,000 cyber-physical assets across large facilities and found nearly one in five sitting one hop from a system making outbound internet connections. Close to 90 percent of the building management systems examined communicated over insecure protocols and 40 percent ran outdated firmware, with around a third of HVAC and cooling systems one hop from an internet-exposed system. The study covers data centres, but the asset classes are the same as a terminal building: power distribution, building management, physical access control, fire protection and cooling. At an airport those systems are usually owned by property or facilities rather than by an OT team, procured under concession or capital contracts, and connected by suppliers for remote monitoring — and physical access control in particular is a security-restricted-area control that regulators care about, not simply a building service.

Critical Advisory — Remote access edge

Citrix is telling admins to patch NetScaler now — an unauthenticated bypass on the appliance that fronts most OT remote access

Citrix published bulletin CTX696939 on 20 August covering two NetScaler ADC and NetScaler Gateway flaws. The serious one, CVE-2026-19490, is an authentication bypass carrying a CVSS score of 9.3: on Gateway and AAA virtual-server configurations an unauthenticated attacker can skip the login step entirely. The second, CVE-2026-19489, is a memory overflow that can produce unpredictable behaviour or a denial of service, and only applies where SIP ALG is enabled on a Large Scale NAT group. Fixed builds are 14.1-73.32 and 13.1-63.21 or later. Citrix, Rapid7 and Finland’s national cyber authority all said they were not aware of in-the-wild exploitation at publication, while urging immediate patching — NetScaler has a long record of exploited edge flaws. At an airport NetScaler commonly fronts remote access for ground-handling, baggage-system, BMS and airfield lighting vendors, and for airlines it fronts crew, ops and MRO application access. An unauthenticated bypass on that appliance defeats the boundary that most airport security programmes are relying on to keep vendor access controlled.

Medium Advisory — Life safety / facility systems

Fire-alarm management software stores its credentials in cleartext in memory, CISA warns

CISA published ICSA-26-232-01 on 20 August covering Johnson Controls Simplex Incident Manager, the software operators use to monitor and manage Simplex fire alarm panels across a property portfolio. Versions up to and including V2.01 keep user passwords and authentication tokens unencrypted in system memory while the application runs, tracked as CVE-2026-27875 and classified as CWE-316. A local attacker who already holds low-privileged access to the workstation can read those credentials out of memory and reuse them against the application and the systems it connects to. CISA lists critical manufacturing, commercial facilities, government facilities, transportation systems and energy among the affected sectors, with deployment worldwide. Johnson Controls has published remediation guidance in JCI-PSA-2026-28. CISA names transportation systems and commercial facilities among the affected sectors, and terminals are exactly that. Airport fire detection is managed from workstations that sit with facilities or a building-services contractor, are often physically accessible in back-of-house areas, and rarely appear in the airport security programme asset inventory. That is the local access this flaw needs.

Critical Advisory — Exploited edge / KEV

Cisco ASA and FTD VPN zero-day exploited to reload firewalls, added to CISA KEV

Cisco has patched CVE-2026-20349, a flaw in the remote-access SSL VPN service of Secure Firewall ASA and Secure Firewall Threat Defense software that attackers are exploiting to force appliances offline. A specially crafted HTTP request to the vulnerable service causes an unexpected reload and a denial-of-service condition, requiring no authentication and no user interaction. Cisco’s PSIRT confirmed active exploitation in August, and CISA added the flaw to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue with a federal remediation deadline of 14 August. Hot fixes cover ASA 9.16, 9.18, 9.20, 9.22, 9.23 and 9.24 and FTD 7.0, 7.2, 7.4, 7.6, 7.7 and 10.0. In aviation these appliances commonly terminate supplier access into baggage, fuelling and building systems, connect outstations and remote airfield facilities, and carry airline access into airport-hosted systems — so an unexpected reload removes remote support at exactly the wrong moment during a disruption.

Medium Policy — GAO oversight

Watchdog finds FAA cybersecurity framework unchanged since 2023 and zero-trust alignment incomplete

A Government Accountability Office review of aviation cybersecurity, GAO-26-107693, found that the FAA and TSA are collaborating but that key shortfalls remain. Among the specific findings: the FAA has not fully reported its cybersecurity spending or implemented its cybersecurity strategy, its cybersecurity framework has been unchanged since August 2023, and it fully aligns with fewer than half of the National Institute of Standards and Technology’s zero-trust migration practices. The GAO also noted that no successful cyberattack against avionics has been reported. For operators the report is context rather than obligation, but it is useful context: it indicates where federal attention is likely to be directed next, and it lands in the same month as research demonstrating a physical-access path into flight management data — a combination that tends to accelerate rulemaking rather than slow it.

Medium Regulation — EU Cyber Resilience Act

From 11 September 2026 exploited vulnerabilities in digital products must be reported within 24 hours

The EU Cyber Resilience Act’s reporting obligations take effect on 11 September 2026. From that date, manufacturers of products with digital elements placed on the EU market must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents affecting those products: an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware, a full notification within 72 hours, and a final report within 14 days of a corrective measure being available, or within a month for a severe incident. Reporting flows through a single reporting platform to the relevant national CSIRT, with information made available to ENISA simultaneously. The obligations cover products already on the EU market, not only new releases. For aviation the immediate effect is on the supply side: manufacturers of ground support equipment, baggage systems, passenger processing platforms and building controls sold into the EU acquire a disclosure duty, and airports and airlines buying from them should expect faster, more formal notification from September.
