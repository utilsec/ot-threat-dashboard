This is not a cyber incident. It is carried here, clearly labelled, because it is the aviation infrastructure story of the period and readers will reasonably have wondered. On 8 September a failure at NATS, the UK air navigation service provider, forced Heathrow to suspend arrivals and disrupted Gatwick and Manchester, affecting more than 2,000 flights across 8–9 September. NATS’ preliminary report, published on 18 September, places the cause in a small part of the National Airspace System: a software defect in the code that allocates aircraft identification codes. A code request was paused when a higher-priority task pre-empted it, and the update did not complete; Reuters quotes the report as saying that had the higher-priority request arrived “even one millisecond earlier or later, the update would have completed normally.” Restrictions lasted about six hours and clearing the backlog took more than two days. A full report is to follow, and NATS proposes a further £1bn of investment by 2033. Addendum, 21 September: FlightGlobal reports that a separate “connectivity” issue at NATS’ Prestwick centre in Scotland caused delays at several airports less than two weeks after the 8 September failure. No cause has been published, NATS has not linked it to the earlier defect, and nothing in the reporting suggests a cyber element. High Research — GNSS spoofing against airborne receivers; controlled exercise, not an attack

Norway spent forty minutes convincing a rescue helicopter it was somewhere else — and the avionics never reported a fault

At Jammertest 2026, the annual open interference exercise on Andøya, researchers escalated a spoofing signal on 10 September until a rescue helicopter’s GPS display showed a false position out over the Norwegian Sea and a track of tight overlapping turns, while the aircraft in fact flew normally. The finding worth carrying is the detection asymmetry rather than the spectacle: jamming announces itself as signal loss and crews are trained for it, whereas spoofing leaves the receiver confidently reporting data that is internally consistent, plausible and wrong. This was a controlled test, not an in-the-wild incident, and no aircraft was endangered. It lands against a real backdrop the researchers cited — RAF aircraft encountering GPS jamming near the Russian border and daily GNSS interference over Sweden through 2026 — and it reinforces the updated jamming and spoofing guidance EASA issued to operators in July 2026. For airport operators the read-across runs past the flight deck. GNSS is not only a navigation input; it is a cheap and widely deployed time source, and GNSS-disciplined clocks quietly underpin surveillance feeds, surface-movement and logging across ground estates. A time source that can be walked rather than broken is a category of failure most airport OT inventories have never recorded. One defensive development came out of the same exercise: Splash247 reported on 17 September that ESA tested Galileo’s Signal Authentication Service (SAS), working alongside OSNMA, during Jammertest. Five operational Galileo satellites broadcast encrypted signals for two hours; under spoofing, conventional receivers reported false positions while SAS-equipped receivers detected the interference. Further testing and accreditation come first, and the initial service — free and worldwide — is due in 2027, so this belongs on the receiver roadmap rather than in today’s mitigation.

Cyber-informed engineering argues the useful question is not how to keep attackers out, but which consequences you can design away

An analysis published 15 September sets out the case for cyber-informed engineering in critical infrastructure: that industrial systems operate under a constraint IT does not share, because a compromised network does not automatically have to become a catastrophic physical event. The argument is to move the security question upstream into design — identifying the physical consequences that actually matter, then engineering them out with controls that do not depend on the digital system remaining trustworthy. Aviation's safety culture already embeds the principle — independent systems, engineered fallbacks, defined degraded modes. The argument here is to apply it explicitly to airport and airline OT, asking where a network compromise could produce a physical or safety consequence and what design change would remove that possibility. CISA publishes a cyber-decoys guide — the rare detection control you can add to an airport network without touching what it controls

CISA released “Using Cyber Decoys to Strengthen Detection and Response” on 17 September. The guide defines decoys as assets that look like legitimate systems, accounts or data but exist to distract an adversary, reveal their presence, or collect intelligence on how they operate, and it sets out three phases: preparation, execution and analysis. CISA positions the technique alongside Zero Trust rather than as a replacement for it. The reason it reads well for aviation is the constraint every airport security team knows: you cannot install an agent on a baggage controller, a jet-bridge PLC or an airfield-lighting panel, and a change window on those systems is expensive. A decoy is additive — a fake engineering workstation, a planted credential that no legitimate process should ever use, a file share named to look like integrator documentation — and it produces a signal with almost no false positives, because nothing legitimate should touch it. The catch is that a decoy nobody monitors is decoration, so the alerting path matters more than the decoy.

Policy Policy — CISA publication change, effective 28 September

CISA is retiring its weekly vulnerability bulletin on 28 September — a small change that breaks a lot of quiet Monday-morning routines

CISA will stop publishing its weekly Vulnerability Summary bulletin from 28 September, pointing readers instead to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, its alerts and its advisories. The agency frames the move as consistent with the risk-based prioritisation set out in binding operational directive BOD 26-04 of June 2026: a weekly digest of everything published is a poor instrument next to a catalog of what is actually being exploited. Nothing in the reporting indicates the ICS advisory stream is affected. It is worth flagging for aviation readers because the weekly bulletin is quietly load-bearing in small vulnerability-management routines — the airport or airline team without a dedicated threat-intel function that reads one email a week and triages from it. If that describes your process, the replacement is not a new email; it is a subscription to KEV and the ICS advisory feed, and a decision about who reads them and how often.

Info Regulation & Policy — US Department of Transportation

A US rule now counts a cyberattack as a “not controllable” cause of delay — and ties the relief to being compliant

A Department of Transportation rule reported on 11 September creates a new cause-of-delay category and designates ten types of disruption as “not controllable,” cyberattacks among them. Where a carrier is compliant with the cybersecurity regulations that apply to it, it no longer owes meals, hotels or comparable amenities under its customer service plan when one of those causes grounds flights; carriers that are not compliant remain subject to the requirements. Nothing here touches an aviation control system — this is advisory-only for OT. What it changes is the cost calculus behind a grounding cyber incident, and it puts a financial edge on being able to evidence compliance, because the carrier that can show it met its obligations is the one that keeps the relief. Reporting places the rule against the backdrop of the Scattered Spider intrusions at airlines, the Collins Aerospace incident that delayed European flights and the 2024 CrowdStrike outage, and notes there is no formal accounting of how often cyberattacks previously triggered compensation claims. Carried here factually as a regulatory change, with no comment on the policy debate around it.

Info Regulation & Policy — EU Cyber Resilience Act

ENISA switches on the Single Reporting Platform as the Cyber Resilience Act’s vulnerability reporting duties take effect

The EU Agency for Cybersecurity deployed the initial operating capability of its Single Reporting Platform, announced 14 September, giving manufacturers and open-source software stewards the channel they need to meet Cyber Resilience Act reporting obligations that took effect this month. Under the CRA, makers of products with digital elements must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents on a defined clock, with an early warning followed by a fuller notification. Airports and airlines are mostly beneficiaries here, but suppliers of baggage, BMS, access-control and airfield equipment placing products on the EU market now carry an active-exploitation reporting duty with a hard clock on it.
