Top Story

NTSB ties a fatal New Mexico air-ambulance crash to GPS jamming from an authorised military exercise — not a cyberattack

In May 2026, a Hawker air ambulance crashed near Ruidoso, New Mexico, killing all four people aboard. NTSB investigators have since tied the loss of GPS signal aboard the aircraft to an authorised electronic-warfare training exercise under way at the nearby White Sands Missile Range. This is a radio-frequency interference and safety-of-navigation event, not a cyberattack: no network was breached, no device was hacked, and no malicious actor was involved. The exercise itself was lawful military activity, but the interference it produced reportedly reached beyond the intended test area and degraded satellite navigation for at least this aircraft during a time-critical medevac flight. Pilots and air traffic controllers in the region have filed GPS-interference reports for roughly a decade, according to follow-up coverage, without that pattern translating into changed procedures before this crash. Because the flight was an air-ambulance mission, the loss of reliable position data compounded an already safety-sensitive, low-margin operation. Broader follow-up reporting examines expanding military jamming exercises and unauthorised drone activity as a growing navigation-safety risk for civil aviation generally, not only around this one range. This item sits on a cyber threat dashboard because GNSS integrity underpins the same navigation and surveillance infrastructure that a cyberattack could also degrade, and a signal-loss event with this consequence is an operational and safety failure regardless of its cause. Four people died in this incident, and it should be read on its own terms rather than folded into cyber-incident statistics.

Confirmed RF interference from an authorised military exercise — not a cyberattack or intrusion No CVE — GNSS signal-loss safety event, not a software or hardware vulnerability
No patch — airspace deconfliction, NOTAM timeliness and GPS-denied contingency procedures Action: Confirm your interference-reporting and NOTAM channel reliably reaches flight-following and dispatch for time-critical operations such as air-ambulance and cargo flights. Check whether crews and controllers near known electronic-warfare test ranges are briefed on exercise schedules, and verify that GPS-denied approach, diversion and manual-navigation procedures exist, are current and are actually trained on — not filed away for the one flight near White Sands.

High Incident — Ransomware

CISA and the FBI refresh the Medusa advisory past 500 critical-infrastructure victims — and flag exploits used before disclosure

CISA and the FBI updated their #StopRansomware advisory on Medusa on 18 Aug 2026. The cumulative victim count passed 500 critical-infrastructure organisations as of April 2026, up from roughly 300 in the March 2025 version — about 200 new victims in a year. Named sectors are healthcare, critical manufacturing, the defense industrial base, government facilities, IT and financial services; aviation is not among them, and the advisory alleges no control-system involvement. Impact is enterprise-IT encryption plus data theft. The detail worth carrying into an airport or airline programme is timing: the agencies report Medusa weaponises newly disclosed vulnerabilities within about 24 hours, and has been observed using exploits up to a week before public disclosure, buying capability rather than developing it. That compresses the exposure window to something well short of the change-control cycle most terminal facilities and airline IT estates actually run on.

Active — 500+ critical-infrastructure victims counted; no aviation victim named and no control-system manipulation alleged Not tied to a single CVE — acquires and weaponises newly disclosed flaws, sometimes pre-disclosure
Mitigations only — patch cadence, MFA on remote access, segmented and offline backups Action: Assume the gap between disclosure and exploitation is a day, not a month, and check that you have a genuine emergency patch route for internet-facing and remote-access systems that does not queue behind the monthly change board. Then confirm which passenger-facing and ground-handling systems could run manually, and for how long, if the enterprise estate were encrypted — that is where an IT-only ransomware event becomes an operational one.

Medium Incident — Ransomware

CISA’s Gunra ransomware advisory lands as Dragos counts six aviation incidents in a rising industrial wave

CISA and international partners issued a joint #StopRansomware advisory on Gunra, a Conti-derived ransomware-as-a-service operation using double extortion against government and critical-infrastructure targets, typically gaining initial access through unpatched internet-facing VPN gateways and exposed RDP, including known Fortinet flaws. It arrives alongside Dragos’s Q2 2026 finding of 1,140 industrial ransomware incidents overall, up 12% quarter on quarter, of which 95 hit transportation and logistics — 8 maritime, 6 aviation, 3 rail. Dragos found no case this quarter of ransomware operators directly manipulating an industrial control system; disruption came through IT and virtualisation layers instead. For an airport or airline, that IT layer typically carries stand and gate allocation, baggage reconciliation, crew rostering and flight-information displays — none individually safety-critical, all required for a normal operating day. Gunra’s extortion notes cite a five-to-seven-day payment deadline.

Active — named ransomware operation with observed victims; six aviation incidents counted in Q2 2026 No single CVE — advisory cites Fortinet CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 among initial-access paths
Mitigations only — VPN/RDP hardening, credential controls, detection content Action: Deploy the AA26-222A indicators and confirm they fire against your logging. Patch or isolate any internet-facing VPN gateway running the cited Fortinet flaws. Then agree in advance with operations what throughput check-in, bag drop and boarding can sustain on manual processing, and at what point you cap departures rather than let delay accumulate — decide that on a quiet day, not during an incident.

Critical Advisory — Johnson Controls

Two critical, unauthenticated flaws in Johnson Controls C-CURE 9000 and victor could hand over an airport’s access-control system

Johnson Controls’ updated advisory (Update A, 11 Aug) covers three vulnerabilities in the C-CURE 9000 access-control platform and the victor video management system: CVE-2026-21655 and CVE-2026-21653, two critical unauthenticated deserialization flaws with CVSS scores up to 9.6, plus a high-severity issue tracked as CVE-2026-34496. C-CURE 9000 and victor are widely deployed for badge access, door control and CCTV integration in airport terminals, airside gates and secured operational areas, which makes this one of the more consequential advisories this window for aviation specifically, even though no exploitation has been reported.

Critical Advisory — Siemens

A CVSS 9.1 command-injection flaw in Siemens Siveillance Video sits on the platform that runs terminal and apron CCTV

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-09 covers CVE-2026-3014, an OS command injection flaw scored CVSS 9.1 in the Management Server API of Siemens Siveillance Video. Any user holding edit permissions on the Management Server can run arbitrary commands in the context of the Management Server Service. Hotfixes are available for V2023R3 below v23.3.27, V2024R1 below v24.1.16 and V2025 below v25.1.15. Siveillance is a video management system deployed for terminal, apron, perimeter and access-point surveillance, which makes the consequence two-sided: code execution on a server that usually sits trusted inside the security network, and influence over what the operations or security control centre is looking at. No exploitation has been reported, and the flaw needs an existing privileged account rather than anonymous network access.

High Advisory — Johnson Controls

Johnson Controls Metasys flaw lets a low-privilege user reach an administrator’s session — and versions 12 and 13 are end of support

CISA advisory ICSA-26-225-14 covers CVE-2026-34491, a persistent cross-site scripting flaw scored CVSS 8.0 in Johnson Controls Metasys. A low-privilege Metasys user can plant a payload through a crafted URL that then executes in an administrator’s browser, inheriting that administrator’s reach over the building management system. Metasys 12 and 13 are affected in all versions, 14 below 14.1.5 and 15 below 15.0.1; version 15.0 was patched on 25 Mar 2026 and 16.0 ships with the fix. Metasys is among the most widely deployed building management platforms in airport terminals, governing HVAC, lighting and plant that a terminal’s operating day depends on. No exploitation has been reported.

High Advisory — CISA KEV

Two of CISA’s four KEV additions on 18 August land squarely on the remote-access and virtualisation layers airports run on

CISA added four vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 18 Aug 2026, all carrying a 21 Aug federal remediation deadline. Two matter directly for aviation infrastructure. CVE-2026-33824 is a double-free flaw in the Microsoft Windows IKE Extensions service (IKEEXT) leading to remote code execution — IKE and IPsec are what terminate airline tenant links, site-to-site connections to remote facilities and vendor remote support into ground systems. CVE-2026-59310 is a Broadcom VMware vCenter path-traversal flaw leading to code execution, already under mass exploitation before the listing formalised it; airport and airline supervisory workloads, from flight-information displays to building-management front ends and historians, increasingly run as guests on that estate. The other two additions are Microsoft SharePoint (CVE-2026-55040) and Apple macOS Screen Sharing (CVE-2026-65400). A three-day deadline is itself a statement about exploitation scale.

High Research — Aircraft systems

USENIX researchers show that ~60 seconds of physical access to a Boeing 737 maintenance port can alter flight data

Academic researchers from UC San Diego and Oberlin, presenting at USENIX Security 2026, built a sub-$100, coin-sized ESP32 device that, inserted through an unlocked maintenance port, can intercept and override ARINC 429 traffic between the flight management computer and the display unit on a Boeing 737. Once connected, the device can alter flight plans, autopilot settings or weight-and-balance data, and the researchers say roughly 60 seconds of unsupervised physical access is enough to install it. Boeing states that the aircraft’s layered design and existing operational protections mitigate the risk; the researchers disagree that the issue is fully resolved. This is a proof of concept demonstrated in a research setting, not an observed exploitation, and it requires physical access to an unlocked port rather than any remote or network entry — which makes it a ramp, turnaround and MRO access-control question as much as an avionics one.

Proof of concept — requires ~60 seconds of unsupervised physical access to an unlocked port; no exploitation in the wild reported No CVE published — hardware-interface and data-integrity research finding
No fix issued — Boeing cites existing layered design and operational protections; researchers dispute this fully resolves it Action: Review who can reach an aircraft’s external and maintenance ports during turnaround, overnight parking and contract MRO work — cleaning, catering, fuelling and third-party maintenance crews all have windows — and whether any step in your process would notice an unfamiliar device attached. Ask engineering how a spoofed weight, temperature or flight-plan value would be caught before it reached a performance calculation or the flight crew, and whether anything currently cross-checks it independently.
Suggested SLA: Ramp/MRO physical-access review — 60 days; port-inspection procedure — 90 days Owner:

Medium Advisory — OT monitoring

Six flaws in CISA’s own Malcolm OT monitoring suite are a reminder that the sensor is part of the attack surface

CISA advisory ICSA-26-230-01 discloses six vulnerabilities in Malcolm, CISA’s own open-source OT network traffic analysis suite. The most serious, CVE-2026-55676 (CVSS 8.8), is an unrestricted file upload leading to arbitrary code execution; the others cover path traversal during archive extraction (CVE-2026-63177, 7.1), a role-based access-control bypass via unnormalised URI paths (CVE-2026-63134), a percent-encoding authorization bypass (CVE-2026-19670) and two further issues, alongside resource-exhaustion denial of service. Fixes land in Malcolm 26.07.0 and 26.08.0 depending on the CVE. No exploitation has been reported. It earns a place on an aviation page because of where Malcolm sits: typically inside the OT network on a SPAN or TAP feed, with visibility of baggage, building-management and airfield traffic. A monitoring platform in that position is an unusually well-placed foothold if it is compromised, and it is often the one system nobody has threat-modelled because it is the security tool.

No confirmed exploitation reported Not in CISA KEV as of this edition
Available — Malcolm 26.07.0 and 26.08.0, depending on the CVE Action: If you run Malcolm anywhere in an airport or airline OT environment, upgrade and confirm its web console is reachable only from a dedicated management segment. Then ask the same question of every other monitoring, asset-discovery or historian collector you operate: it sees everything, so what would an attacker gain by owning it, and would you notice? Sensors deserve the same patch cadence and access control as the assets they watch.

Medium Policy — Federal oversight

GAO: TSA is still working from a 2018 cybersecurity roadmap, and FAA has fully implemented three of seven strategy objectives

GAO’s aviation cybersecurity review, GAO-26-107693, found that TSA has not clearly defined cybersecurity roles and responsibilities for the aviation sector and is still operating against a Cybersecurity Roadmap issued in 2018 that no longer aligns with current DHS strategy. On the FAA side, GAO found the agency has not fully reported its cybersecurity spending to OMB, and that its Zero Trust Implementation Plan only partially aligns with recognised NIST practice; of the seven objectives in FAA’s own Cybersecurity Strategy, three had been fully implemented. GAO issued five recommendations and the departments accepted them. Published in July, it remains the current federal assessment, and in a window with no aviation incident it is the most substantive aviation-specific item on this page. The practical read for an operator is that federal role clarity is still being built, so do not assume a coordinating body already owns a gap at your airport.

Not applicable — federal oversight report, no incident or exploitation Not vulnerability-specific
No patch — governance, defined roles and strategy implementation Action: Do not wait on federal role clarity to arrive. Write down, for your own airport or airline, who owns cyber risk for airfield systems, terminal building automation, ground-handling contractors and airline tenants — and where the boundaries between those owners actually sit. Then settle the question everyone discovers too late: on a Sunday night, who declares an OT incident, and to whom is it reported.

Low Policy — ATC modernisation

FAA issues a $74.3M initial task order to begin replacing the network behind air traffic control

The FAA awarded AT&T a $74.3 million initial task order on 14 Aug 2026 under what is anticipated to become a multibillion-dollar, 15-year programme to modernise the telecommunications network underpinning air traffic control. This is a procurement milestone, not a security event: nothing in it indicates a compromise, a vulnerability or an attack. It sits on this page because the infrastructure being replaced is the aging ATC networking that GAO and others have repeatedly flagged in resilience assessments, and because a multi-year national network transition is itself a security programme. Cutover windows, parallel running of legacy and new circuits, and a much larger population of contractor hands on operationally critical links all create risk that has to be managed deliberately. How cybersecurity requirements are written into the individual task orders will matter more over the programme’s life than the headline contract value.

Not applicable — contract award, no security event or exploitation Not vulnerability-specific
No patch — multi-year infrastructure modernisation programme Action: If your facility depends on FAA-provided telecommunications, start asking now how the transition will be sequenced at your site, what the fallback is if a cutover fails, and who holds access during migration. Treat programme cutover windows as change risk on your own risk register rather than as somebody else’s project, and make sure your contingency procedures assume a communications path can be unavailable for planned reasons as well as unplanned ones.
