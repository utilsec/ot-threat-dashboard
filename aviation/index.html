It closed the southern runway at 20:20 and lifted the closure at 21:05, dpa reports, leaving the airport with no take-offs or landings for about 45 minutes. Numerous flights were delayed and several were diverted. The Federal Police searched and found nothing, and Brandenburg police have opened an investigation into dangerous interference with air traffic. No operator or motive has been identified. Some early English-language reports gave the closure as about five minutes, apparently misreading the time zone; dpa’s timeline gives 45. The trend matters more than the single event. UK air traffic failure traced to a software defect in aircraft code allocation — NATS’ preliminary report describes a timing fault, not an attack

This is not a cyber incident. It is carried here, clearly labelled, because it is the aviation infrastructure story of the period and readers will reasonably have wondered. On 8 September a failure at NATS, the UK air navigation service provider, forced Heathrow to suspend arrivals and disrupted Gatwick and Manchester, affecting more than 2,000 flights across 8–9 September. NATS’ preliminary report, published on 18 September, places the cause in a small part of the National Airspace System: a software defect in the code that allocates aircraft identification codes. A code request was paused when a higher-priority task pre-empted it, and the update did not complete; Reuters quotes the report as saying that had the higher-priority request arrived “even one millisecond earlier or later, the update would have completed normally.” Restrictions lasted about six hours and clearing the backlog took more than two days. NATS says a mitigation is in place while a permanent fix is safety-tested and deployed. Chief executive Martin Rolfe, who had already ruled out cyber on 10 September, dismissed speculation of military interference and said the fault is unrelated to the 2023 outage; the early accounts blaming an anomalous military flight plan are not supported by the stated cause. Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander called the failure “completely unacceptable” and has tasked the Civil Aviation Authority with an independent review of NATS’ findings, its resilience investment plans and regulatory accountability. A full report is to follow, and NATS proposes a further £1bn of investment by 2033. For aviation OT teams the lesson is availability engineering rather than attack defence: a defect that only surfaces when two tasks collide within a millisecond is exactly the kind of fault conventional testing can miss, so the useful questions are how your own flight-critical systems behave when an update stalls and how quickly you recover when one does. Addendum, 21 September: FlightGlobal reports that a separate “connectivity” issue at NATS’ Prestwick centre in Scotland caused delays at several airports less than two weeks after the 8 September failure. No cause has been published, NATS has not linked it to the earlier defect, and nothing in the reporting suggests a cyber element. We carry it as an unattributed operational fault and will update if NATS publishes a cause.

High Research — GAO audit of aircraft datalinks and spectrum-threat monitoring; no exploitation reported

GAO: the FAA cannot see spectrum attacks on aircraft communications in real time, and the ACARS and CPDLC datalinks could carry a forged clearance

The US Government Accountability Office published GAO-26-108439 on 21 September, titled “Aviation Cybersecurity: Enhanced Air Safety Requires FAA to Better Mitigate Threats to Aircraft Communications.” Auditors examined the text-based datalinks that carry messages between aircraft, airline operations and air traffic control — ACARS and Controller-Pilot Data Link Communications — and found weaknesses in authentication, encryption and protocol design such that, in GAO’s words, “a malicious actor could transmit fraudulent clearance cancellations, possibly leading to flight delays or safety issues.” On the spectrum side, GAO found that the FAA acknowledges jamming and spoofing as threats but “did not have a defined, real-time monitoring and detection capability for all spectrum-related threats,” and that its risk assessments and security documentation for the National Airspace System are incomplete. GAO makes nine recommendations, covering NAS risk assessments, real-time spectrum-threat monitoring, interagency coordination and stronger authentication and encryption for aircraft communications; the Department of Transportation concurred with all nine, and all nine were open at publication. This is an audit, not an incident. GAO reports no exploitation of either weakness; the report is carried because it is the most substantive primary-source aviation item of the fortnight, not because anything happened. It matters to this page for two reasons. The datalink finding belongs to airlines and their communications providers, and it puts on the record that a message channel crews act on is not authenticated. The spectrum finding is the same problem the Jammertest work exposed for GNSS, stated at national scale: a signal that is manipulated rather than removed produces no alarm anywhere in the chain, and nobody currently owns detecting it.

High Research — GNSS spoofing against airborne receivers; controlled exercise, not an attack

Norway spent forty minutes convincing a rescue helicopter it was somewhere else — and the avionics never reported a fault

At Jammertest 2026, the annual open interference exercise on Andøya, researchers escalated a spoofing signal on 10 September until a rescue helicopter’s GPS display showed a false position out over the Norwegian Sea and a track of tight overlapping turns, while the aircraft in fact flew normally. The finding worth carrying is the detection asymmetry rather than the spectacle: jamming announces itself as signal loss and crews are trained for it, whereas spoofing leaves the receiver confidently reporting data that is internally consistent, plausible and wrong. This was a controlled test, not an in-the-wild incident, and no aircraft was endangered. It lands against a real backdrop the researchers cited — RAF aircraft encountering GPS jamming near the Russian border and daily GNSS interference over Sweden through 2026 — and it reinforces the updated jamming and spoofing guidance EASA issued to operators in July 2026. For airport operators the read-across runs past the flight deck. GNSS is not only a navigation input; it is a cheap and widely deployed time source, and GNSS-disciplined clocks quietly underpin surveillance feeds, surface-movement and logging across ground estates. A time source that can be walked rather than broken is a category of failure most airport OT inventories have never recorded. One defensive development came out of the same exercise: Splash247 reported on 17 September that ESA tested Galileo’s Signal Authentication Service (SAS), working alongside OSNMA, during Jammertest. Five operational Galileo satellites broadcast encrypted signals for two hours; under spoofing, conventional receivers reported false positions while SAS-equipped receivers detected the interference. Further testing and accreditation come first, and the initial service — free and worldwide — is due in 2027, so this belongs on the receiver roadmap rather than in today’s mitigation.

The EU Agency for Cybersecurity deployed the initial operating capability of its Single Reporting Platform, announced 14 September, giving manufacturers and open-source software stewards the channel they need to meet Cyber Resilience Act reporting obligations that took effect this month.
