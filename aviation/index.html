Top Story

NTSB ties a fatal New Mexico air-ambulance crash to GPS jamming from an authorised military exercise — not a cyberattack

In May 2026, a Hawker air ambulance crashed near Ruidoso, New Mexico, killing all four people aboard. NTSB investigators have since tied the loss of GPS signal aboard the aircraft to an authorised electronic-warfare training exercise under way at the nearby White Sands Missile Range. This is a radio-frequency interference and safety-of-navigation event, not a cyberattack: no network was breached, no device was hacked, and no malicious actor was involved. The exercise itself was lawful military activity, but the interference it produced reportedly reached beyond the intended test area and degraded satellite navigation for at least this aircraft during a time-critical medevac flight. Pilots and air traffic controllers in the region have filed GPS-interference reports for roughly a decade, according to follow-up coverage, without that pattern translating into changed procedures before this crash. Because the flight was an air-ambulance mission, the loss of reliable position data compounded an already safety-sensitive, low-margin operation. Broader follow-up reporting examines expanding military jamming exercises and unauthorised drone activity as a growing navigation-safety risk for civil aviation generally, not only around this one range. This item sits on a cyber threat dashboard because GNSS integrity underpins the same navigation and surveillance infrastructure that a cyberattack could also degrade, and a signal-loss event with this consequence is an operational and safety failure regardless of its cause. Four people died in this incident, and it should be read on its own terms rather than folded into cyber-incident statistics.

Confirmed RF interference from an authorised military exercise — not a cyberattack or intrusion No CVE — GNSS signal-loss safety event, not a software or hardware vulnerability
No patch — airspace deconfliction, NOTAM timeliness and GPS-denied contingency procedures Action: Confirm your interference-reporting and NOTAM channel reliably reaches flight-following and dispatch for time-critical operations such as air-ambulance and cargo flights. Check whether crews and controllers near known electronic-warfare test ranges are briefed on exercise schedules, and verify that GPS-denied approach, diversion and manual-navigation procedures exist, are current and are actually trained on — not filed away for the one flight near White Sands.

High Incident — Nation-state

Federal advisory AA26-097A is a live reminder of what an internet-exposed PLC costs an airport

Federal agencies continue to update joint advisory AA26-097A, which assesses that Iranian-affiliated actors including the group CyberAv3ngers are exploiting internet-exposed programmable logic controllers across US critical infrastructure. The campaign, live since late July, has reached water and wastewater utilities in at least a dozen states by taking Rockwell Automation MicroLogix and other exposed controllers, changing their IP addresses and setting unauthorised passwords to lock operators out. No CVE was exploited — the actors used exposure plus default or weak credentials and the manufacturers’ own PLC programming software. Aviation was not a named target this window, but the technique generalises directly to airport ground OT: fuel-farm and hydrant systems, baggage sortation, de-icing plant, jet-bridge controllers and airfield lighting frequently run on the same general-purpose automation families and are just as often reachable from the internet through a contractor’s remote-access arrangement nobody at the airport controls. The advisory urges strict limits on who can reach PLCs and validation of project files for unauthorised changes.

Active against water/wastewater utilities — assessed to Iranian-affiliated actors; no aviation victim confirmed No CVE named — exposure plus default/weak credentials, not a software flaw
No patch — remove controllers from the internet, credential hygiene, project-file integrity checks Action: Inventory every PLC and RTU your airport or ground-handling contractors expose to the internet, starting with fuel, de-icing, baggage and airfield-lighting systems maintained under a supplier contract rather than your own network diagram. Confirm none rely on default or shared credentials, and get a source-of-truth copy of the controller logic that should be running so you can detect unauthorised changes.

Medium Incident — Ransomware

CISA’s Gunra ransomware advisory lands as Dragos counts six aviation incidents in a rising industrial wave

CISA and international partners issued a joint #StopRansomware advisory on Gunra, a Conti-derived ransomware-as-a-service operation using double extortion against government and critical-infrastructure targets, typically gaining initial access through unpatched internet-facing VPN gateways and exposed RDP, including known Fortinet flaws. It arrives alongside Dragos’s Q2 2026 finding of 1,140 industrial ransomware incidents overall, up 12% quarter on quarter, of which 95 hit transportation and logistics — 8 maritime, 6 aviation, 3 rail. Dragos found no case this quarter of ransomware operators directly manipulating an industrial control system; disruption came through IT and virtualisation layers instead. For an airport or airline, that IT layer typically carries stand and gate allocation, baggage reconciliation, crew rostering and flight-information displays — none individually safety-critical, all required for a normal operating day. Gunra’s extortion notes cite a five-to-seven-day payment deadline.

Then agree in advance with operations what throughput check-in, bag drop and boarding can sustain on manual processing, and at what point you cap departures rather than let delay accumulate — decide that on a quiet day, not during an incident.

Critical Advisory — Johnson Controls

Two critical, unauthenticated flaws in Johnson Controls C-CURE 9000 and victor could hand over an airport’s access-control system

Johnson Controls’ updated advisory (Update A, 11 Aug) covers three vulnerabilities in the C-CURE 9000 access-control platform and the victor video management system: CVE-2026-21655 and CVE-2026-21653, two critical unauthenticated deserialization flaws with CVSS scores up to 9.6, plus a high-severity issue tracked as CVE-2026-34496. A network-adjacent attacker could exploit the deserialization bugs to execute arbitrary code and take control of the physical access-control system without any credentials. C-CURE 9000 and victor are widely deployed for badge access, door control and CCTV integration in airport terminals, airside gates and secured operational areas, which makes this one of the more consequential advisories this window for aviation specifically, even though no exploitation has been reported.

Medium Advisory — Building automation

CISA’s 15-advisory day includes a Siemens Desigo flaw that can freeze an airport building controller

CISA published 15 ICS advisories in a single day on 13 Aug 2026, spanning AVEVA, Hitachi Energy, ANDRITZ, Siemens and Johnson Controls. Among them, CVE-2026-59693 (CVSS 4.3) affects Siemens Desigo DXR2 and PXC3/4/5/7 building controllers: a malformed BACnet packet can stop the controller responding until it is manually reset. Desigo is a building-management platform used in HVAC, lighting and life-safety integration, including at airports, where a frozen controller during a terminal operating day is a facilities incident even without any data compromised. Firmware updates are available. None of the advisories in this 15-advisory batch have been reported as exploited.

High Advisory — CISA KEV

Actively exploited Cisco ASA/FTD flaw hits a device class that sits on many airport network boundaries

Cisco disclosed and patched CVE-2026-20349, a CVSS 8.6 heap-inspection denial-of-service flaw in the Remote Access SSL VPN service of Secure Firewall ASA and Firepower Threat Defense software. Cisco confirmed active exploitation in the wild, and CISA added the CVE to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue on 11 Aug with a 14 Aug federal remediation deadline. Hotfixes cover all affected 9.x, 7.x and 10.x branches; there is no workaround. These appliances are commonly deployed as the IT/OT boundary and remote-access point at airports and airlines, carrying tenant, ground-handler and vendor-support connections, so an attacker forcing repeated reloads can disrupt more than the firewall itself.

High Research — Aircraft systems

USENIX researchers show that ~60 seconds of physical access to a Boeing 737 maintenance port can alter flight data

Academic researchers from UC San Diego and Oberlin, presenting at USENIX Security 2026, built a sub-$100, coin-sized ESP32 device that, inserted through an unlocked maintenance port, can intercept and override ARINC 429 traffic between the flight management computer and the display unit on a Boeing 737. Once connected, the device can alter flight plans, autopilot settings or weight-and-balance data, and the researchers say roughly 60 seconds of unsupervised physical access is enough to install it. Boeing states that the aircraft’s layered design and existing operational protections mitigate the risk; the researchers disagree that the issue is fully resolved. This is a proof of concept demonstrated in a research setting, not an observed exploitation, and it requires physical access to an unlocked port rather than any remote or network entry — which makes it a ramp, turnaround and MRO access-control question as much as an avionics one.

Proof of concept — requires ~60 seconds of unsupervised physical access to an unlocked port; no exploitation in the wild reported No CVE published — hardware-interface and data-integrity research finding
No fix issued — Boeing cites existing layered design and operational protections; researchers dispute this fully resolves it Action: Review who can reach an aircraft’s external and maintenance ports during turnaround, overnight parking and contract MRO work — cleaning, catering, fuelling and third-party maintenance crews all have windows — and whether any step in your process would notice an unfamiliar device attached. Ask engineering how a spoofed weight, temperature or flight-plan value would be caught before it reached a performance calculation or the flight crew, and whether anything currently cross-checks it independently.
Suggested SLA: Ramp/MRO physical-access review — 60 days; port-inspection procedure — 90 days Owner:

Medium Research — Ransomware

Dragos counts six aviation ransomware incidents in Q2 — and none touched a control system

Dragos’s Q2 2026 industrial ransomware analysis recorded 1,140 incidents against industrial organisations worldwide, up 12% quarter on quarter. Manufacturing accounted for the bulk at 747; transportation and logistics totalled 95, split across maritime (8), aviation (6) and rail (3). The finding that matters most for aviation is what did not happen: Dragos identified no case this quarter where ransomware operators directly manipulated an industrial control system — disruption cascaded from IT and virtualisation-layer encryption instead. That matches the operational pattern aviation has already shown at scale, where a shared check-in or boarding platform going down can push several airports to manual processing without a single airfield system being touched.

Active — six confirmed aviation ransomware incidents counted in Q2 2026, per Dragos Not vulnerability-specific — incident-volume and impact analysis

Record the manual fallback and its sustainable throughput for each, and test one under realistic load rather than only on paper.

Medium Research — Exposure

Forescout finds thousands of exposed Rockwell controllers — the same exposure pattern airport ground OT shares

Forescout Vedere Labs counted 4,407 internet-exposed Rockwell Automation and Allen-Bradley controllers worldwide, 2,844 of them in the US, dominated by discontinued legacy MicroLogix 1400/1100 models — including 22 in cities already hit by the ongoing water-sector attacks. Forescout notes that the intruders in that campaign reconfigured devices directly rather than exploiting any new flaw; exposure and weak credentials were sufficient. Airports are not named in this research, but the same controller families and the same procurement pattern — equipment installed and remotely supported by a contractor, outside the airport’s own network inventory — show up across fuel, baggage, HVAC and lighting systems, which is why this exposure count is a useful proxy for what a similar sweep of airport ground OT would likely find.

Low Policy — Critical infrastructure

A new federal water-cybersecurity bill has no aviation equivalent yet — but the pattern it addresses does

Senators Klobuchar and Schiff introduced the Water Cyber Shield Act on 13 Aug 2026, following the multistate water-utility PLC attacks. It would give the EPA authority to require cybersecurity assessments, provide $300 million a year in state revolving funds, and mandate cyber-risk planning for large water systems. The bill is specific to water and wastewater utilities and does not apply to aviation. It is included here because the exposure pattern it responds to — internet-reachable controllers, weak credentials, resource-constrained operators — is not sector-specific, and airport and airline ground-OT owners should expect similar scrutiny if a comparable incident occurs in aviation, rather than assume the regulatory attention stops at water.

Low Policy — Funding

New York’s $9 million water-cybersecurity grant program is a funding model worth watching for other public operators

New York State awarded up to $9 million to 153 drinking-water and wastewater systems — up to $50,000 per system for cybersecurity assessments and $100,000 for improvements — following the summer’s attack wave and the state’s March cybersecurity mandates for water utilities. The program is water-specific and does not extend to airports or airlines. It is relevant to aviation because many airports are themselves publicly owned or municipally operated, structurally similar to the small water utilities this grant targets, and a state that has just built an assessment-and-improvement funding mechanism for one type of public infrastructure operator has a template it could extend to another.

Not applicable — state funding program Not vulnerability-specific
No patch — funding and assessment program Action: If your airport authority is municipally or state-owned, check whether your state has, or is developing, a similar assessment-and-improvement grant program and get ahead of the application criteria rather than waiting for an incident to prompt one.
