Attackers took over refrigeration controllers at about 700 French supermarkets — a warning for every internet-reachable building controller on an airport estate

Over the nights of 11–13 October, attackers remotely changed settings on web-managed refrigeration controllers in French supermarkets, forcing long defrost cycles with temperatures reported as high as about 50°C, spoiling stock and, according to the Paris prosecutor, starting fires at some stores. Finance Minister Roland Lescure told Parliament on 13 October that about 700 of France’s roughly 10,000 supermarkets were affected, and Carrefour, Coopérative U, E.Leclerc, Intermarché and Monoprix have confirmed impact. CERT-FR’s alert CERTFR-2026-ALE-012 named Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC controllers on 12 October, added Copeland xweb and Dixell xweb on 13 October, and in its 16 October update says the campaigns are probably still running. Danfoss and Copeland both say most affected units are older or end-of-life models. No group has claimed the attack, no ransom has been demanded and the entry route has not been established; the prosecutor’s cybercrime section has assigned the inquiry to OFAC, France’s anti-cybercrime office. This is not an aviation incident and it does not count toward this page’s threat level. Our assessment of why it is here: airport terminals, flight-catering units and cargo cold stores run the same class of remotely managed refrigeration and building controllers, often maintained by a contractor through a web interface, and this attack shows how directly a changed setpoint becomes physical loss.

Medium Incident — Airspace intrusion and detection; no cyber component

Berlin Brandenburg stops all flying for 45 minutes on a drone sighting, its 28th drone this year against eight in all of 2025 — the gap is detection, not defence

On the evening of Wednesday 23 September, Germany’s air navigation service DFS closed Berlin Brandenburg’s northern runway after a drone was sighted at about 20:00 local time. It closed the southern runway at 20:20 and lifted the closure at 21:05, dpa reports, leaving the airport with no take-offs or landings for about 45 minutes. Numerous flights were delayed and several were diverted. The Federal Police searched and found nothing, and Brandenburg police have opened an investigation into dangerous interference with air traffic. No operator or motive has been identified. Some early English-language reports gave the closure as about five minutes, apparently misreading the time zone; dpa’s timeline gives 45. The trend matters more than the single event. DFS figures reported by dpa put drones recorded at BER at 28 in the first half of 2026, against eight in the whole of 2025, and flying was suspended seven times in the half-year. Earlier in the month, Brussels Airport halted traffic for roughly half an hour on the evening of Friday 12 September after a drone was sighted near the airfield; no injuries or damage were reported and movements resumed once the area was cleared, with the drone’s origin and intent unattributed. The following day Vilnius closed for 38 minutes on a drone alert that scrambled a NATO jet before the object was confirmed to be a flock of birds. Both follow the US Labor Day weekend incursion at San Diego that grounded around 400 flights, where the TSA reportedly could not identify the operator and coverage noted that US airports carry no mandatory drone-detection requirement at all. None of this is a cyberattack and none of it should be filed as one. It belongs on this page because the operational consequence is identical to an OT failure and lands on the same people: a ground stop cascades into stand and gate reallocation, jet-bridge and ramp re-sequencing, baggage re-flow and fuel and de-icing re-planning, and the decision to stop or resume is increasingly taken from software — counter-UAS detection feeds, ground-movement displays, airfield lighting control — that few airports treat as operational technology with an integrity requirement. Vilnius is the instructive one: the cost was not the drone, it was the ambiguity.

Confirmed disruptions, no cyber element — the Berlin Brandenburg closure of 23 Sep 2026 (about 45 minutes, per dpa; operator unidentified, police investigating), the Brussels halt, the Vilnius closure and the San Diego grounding are confirmed; the Vilnius trigger is confirmed to have been birds, and neither the Brussels drone operator nor the San Diego one has been identified No CVE — this is an airspace-security and sensing problem, not a vulnerability
No patch applies — the exposure is unverified detection input driving operational decisions, and the absence of any detection mandate in several jurisdictions Action: Treat counter-UAS and airspace-detection feeds as OT data sources rather than security gadgets. Write down which sensors and which vendor platform can trigger or justify a ground stop, who can see them, how the data reaches the tower and the airport operations centre, and whether that path is authenticated and logged — a detection feed that can be spoofed, replayed or simply dropped becomes a denial-of-service against your own runway. Then rehearse the restart, not the stop: most airports can halt movements in minutes and take hours to recover, so time the sequence of stand reallocation, jet-bridge and ramp re-tasking, baggage re-flow and fuel and de-icing re-planning, and identify which of those steps depends on a system that has no manual fallback. Finally, agree the ambiguity rule in advance — what evidence is sufficient to resume — because the Vilnius closure shows that indecision costs more than the intrusion.
Suggested SLA: Airspace-detection feed and decision-path inventory — 30 days; ground-stop restart sequence timed and documented — 45 days; resume-criteria decision rule agreed with the tower and handlers — 30 days Owner:

Not Cyber Context — ATC operational failure; preliminary report finds a software defect, and cyber was ruled out by the operator

UK air traffic failure traced to a software defect in aircraft code allocation — NATS’ preliminary report describes a timing fault, not an attack

This is not a cyber incident. It is carried here, clearly labelled, because it is the aviation infrastructure story of the period and readers will reasonably have wondered. On 8 September a failure at NATS, the UK air navigation service provider, forced Heathrow to suspend arrivals and disrupted Gatwick and Manchester, affecting more than 2,000 flights across 8–9 September. NATS’ preliminary report, published on 18 September, places the cause in a small part of the National Airspace System: a software defect in the code that allocates aircraft identification codes. A code request was paused when a higher-priority task pre-empted it, and the update did not complete; Reuters quotes the report as saying that had the higher-priority request arrived “even one millisecond earlier or later, the update would have completed normally.” Restrictions lasted about six hours and clearing the backlog took more than two days. NATS says a mitigation is in place while a permanent fix is safety-tested and deployed. Chief executive Martin Rolfe, who had already ruled out cyber on 10 September, dismissed speculation of military interference and said the fault is unrelated to the 2023 outage; the early accounts blaming an anomalous military flight plan are not supported by the stated cause. Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander called the failure “completely unacceptable” and has tasked the Civil Aviation Authority with an independent review of NATS’ findings, its resilience investment plans and regulatory accountability. A full report is to follow, and NATS proposes a further £1bn of investment by 2033. For aviation OT teams the lesson is availability engineering rather than attack defence: a defect that only surfaces when two tasks collide within a millisecond is exactly the kind of fault conventional testing can miss, so the useful questions are how your own flight-critical systems behave when an update stalls and how quickly you recover when one does. Addendum, 21 September: FlightGlobal reports that a separate “connectivity” issue at NATS’ Prestwick centre in Scotland caused delays at several airports less than two weeks after the 8 September failure. No cause has been published, NATS has not linked it to the earlier defect, and nothing in the reporting suggests a cyber element. We carry it as an unattributed operational fault and will update if NATS publishes a cause.

High Threat Intelligence — FBI and US Secret Service warning on an active credential campaign against FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN; no CVE; aviation relevance is our assessment

FBI and Secret Service: the FortiBleed credential campaign against FortiGate VPNs is still running, and their advisory covers every critical-infrastructure sector, transportation included

On Tuesday 6 October the FBI and the US Secret Service issued joint advisory JCSA-20261006-01 warning that the FortiBleed campaign remains active: attackers are still scanning internet-facing Fortinet firewalls and SSL VPN gateways and logging in with credentials obtained earlier. There is no CVE. The agencies tie the campaign to reused or leaked passwords and legacy SHA-256 password storage; researchers describe password spraying and stuffing from leak dumps, offline cracking of extracted hashes and a sniffer that captures authentication traffic across 24 protocols. Some owners were locked out after the intruders created their own administrator accounts and deleted or reset the real ones. The access is resold, the FBI links it to Payload ransomware, and SOCRadar says it has confirmed at least 12 ransomware deployments from it. SOCRadar’s figure of more than 86,644 working device credentials in 194 countries dates from 19 June; it is not a new count. The advisory says it applies to all 16 US critical-infrastructure sectors and names transportation systems among them, and Cybersecurity Dive reports that targeted organisations span every one of the 16. No OT system compromise has been described and no aviation victim is named. Our assessment: FortiGate is a common boundary device between airport and airline corporate networks and terminal and airfield systems, so a stolen admin login there is a stolen boundary.

High Research — GAO audit of aircraft datalinks and spectrum-threat monitoring; no exploitation reported

GAO: the FAA cannot see spectrum attacks on aircraft communications in real time, and the ACARS and CPDLC datalinks could carry a forged clearance

The US Government Accountability Office published GAO-26-108439 on 21 September, titled “Aviation Cybersecurity: Enhanced Air Safety Requires FAA to Better Mitigate Threats to Aircraft Communications.” Auditors examined the text-based datalinks that carry messages between aircraft, airline operations and air traffic control — ACARS and Controller-Pilot Data Link Communications — and found weaknesses in authentication, encryption and protocol design such that, in GAO’s words, “a malicious actor could transmit fraudulent clearance cancellations, possibly leading to flight delays or safety issues.” On the spectrum side, GAO found that the FAA acknowledges jamming and spoofing as threats but “did not have a defined, real-time monitoring and detection capability for all spectrum-related threats,” and that its risk assessments and security documentation for the National Airspace System are incomplete. GAO makes nine recommendations, covering NAS risk assessments, real-time spectrum-threat monitoring, interagency coordination and stronger authentication and encryption for aircraft communications; the Department of Transportation concurred with all nine, and all nine were open at publication. This is an audit, not an incident. GAO reports no exploitation of either weakness; the report is carried because it is the most substantive primary-source aviation item of the fortnight, not because anything happened. It matters to this page for two reasons. The datalink finding belongs to airlines and their communications providers, and it puts on the record that a message channel crews act on is not authenticated. The spectrum finding is the same problem the Jammertest work exposed for GNSS, stated at national scale: a signal that is manipulated rather than removed produces no alarm anywhere in the chain, and nobody currently owns detecting it.

Not an incident — GAO audit findings published 21 Sep 2026; no exploitation of the datalink or spectrum weaknesses is reported; DOT concurred with all nine recommendations, all open at publication No CVE — the findings are protocol-design and programme-level, not a product vulnerability

Airports and ANSPs: inventory the operational inputs on your estate that arrive over the air with no integrity check — GNSS-disciplined timing on surveillance and logging, datalink ground stations, ADS-B feeds into operations displays — and record, for each, what the consuming system does when the input is wrong rather than absent. Medium Research — GNSS signal authentication tested against live spoofing; controlled exercise, not an attack

Galileo’s encrypted test signal kept a receiver on the right position under spoofing at Jammertest, while a commercial receiver was led astray

On the afternoon of 16 September, five operational Galileo satellites broadcast a new encrypted test signal over Europe for two hours, and receivers at Andøya in Norway and at ESA’s ESTEC navigation laboratory in the Netherlands fixed their positions from it, ESA reported. The signal belongs to Galileo’s Signal Authentication Service (SAS), which uses encrypted ranging signals and is designed to work with OSNMA, the navigation-message authentication service operating since 2025, so that receivers can detect and reject a wide range of spoofing. In a spoofing run at Jammertest, the annual September interference exercise in Norway, the SAS-enabled receiver held its true position while a commercial receiver fed the same false signal calculated wrong ones. EUSPA, the European Commission, ESA and industry plan validation and accreditation in 2027, after which the service is to be declared operational; both SAS and OSNMA are to be free and available worldwide. The same exercise showed why it matters: TechSpot reported on 10 September that researchers spoofed a rescue helicopter’s GPS until its display showed a false track over the Norwegian Sea while the aircraft flew normally. Jamming shows up as signal loss and crews are trained for it; spoofing leaves a receiver confidently reporting a plausible, wrong answer. For airports the exposure runs beyond the flight deck to GNSS-disciplined clocks behind surveillance, surface-movement and logging systems. Authentication belongs on the receiver roadmap; it is not yet today’s mitigation.

Confirmed test results, not an attack — ESA’s 16 Sep 2026 trial and the helicopter spoofing were run inside a licensed national exercise; no real-world incident or actor is involved No CVE — civil GNSS signals carry no authentication today; that is a design gap, not a product defect
No patch yet — SAS validation and accreditation are planned for 2027; current mitigation is cross-checking, holdover design and procedure Action: Inventory every place GNSS-derived time or position enters your estate — roof-antenna NTP servers, surveillance and multilateration feeds, surface-movement displays, CCTV and access-control timestamps — and record what each system does when the signal is wrong rather than absent. Alarm on a change of time source or a step in clock offset, not only on signal loss, and prefer holdover-capable clocks with a terrestrial backup. When receivers or timing equipment are next replaced, ask vendors for OSNMA support now and a stated plan for SAS.
Suggested SLA: GNSS-dependency inventory across ground systems — 45 days; time-source-change alarming — 60 days; OSNMA and SAS requirements added to receiver and timing procurement — next refresh Owner:

NCC Group’s review of August 2026, reported by Industrial Cyber on 28 September, counted 1,073 ransomware attacks, up 12% on July’s 960, which Industrial Cyber reports as a 2026 high. Info Guidance — CISA, detection and response

CISA publishes a cyber-decoys guide — the rare detection control you can add to an airport network without touching what it controls

CISA released “Using Cyber Decoys to Strengthen Detection and Response” on 17 September. The guide defines decoys as assets that look like legitimate systems, accounts or data but exist to distract an adversary, reveal their presence, or collect intelligence on how they operate, and it sets out three phases: preparation, execution and analysis. CISA positions the technique alongside Zero Trust rather than as a replacement for it. The reason it reads well for aviation is the constraint every airport security team knows: you cannot install an agent on a baggage controller, a jet-bridge PLC or an airfield-lighting panel, and a change window on those systems is expensive. A decoy is additive — a fake engineering workstation, a planted credential that no legitimate process should ever use, a file share named to look like integrator documentation — and it produces a signal with almost no false positives, because nothing legitimate should touch it. The catch is that a decoy nobody monitors is decoration, so the alerting path matters more than the decoy.
